Volume Six　The Shuofang Army

Chapter 34　Hun Shizhi

In the tenth month of the first year of Guangde, the Tibetans entered Chang'an.

That autumn the frontier reports from Longyou had been sent to the capital one after another, and once they reached the palace nothing more was heard of them; only when two hundred thousand Tibetan horse had crossed the Wei did the palace panic. The Sage One went out by the park gate and east to Shanzhou; few of the hundred officials went with him, and anyone in the city who could run ran for the Southern Mountains. The Tibetans came from the west, and entered by the Kaiyuan Gate and the Jinguang Gate; the Yining Ward lay just inside the Kaiyuan Gate, and on the first night the streets outside the ward wall were all the sound of hooves.

Yisi did not leave, and the monastery's people did not leave. On the first evening the households of the ward that could not run—the old, the sick, those carrying children—came one after another to beat on the monastery gate. Elder Samaan, his account book in his arms, reckoned the monastery's rice and said they could take thirty people; any more, and the rice would not last ten days. Jingjing, Deacon of Charity then, stood inside the gate listening, and listening a while, glanced at Yisi. Yisi said only one thing: "Charity is in your charge. Whether this gate opens is for you to decide." Jingjing remembered that second night in the Xiushan Ward in Luoyang, when other people's doors had not opened to their knocking, turned round, and told the guard brothers to open the gate. In those ten-odd days the monastery took in a hundred and forty people all told; the felt tents in the back courtyard were full, the hall too was full of sleepers; Elder Samaan cut the gruel from two meals a day to one, and from thick to thin, and himself drank it thin with the rest.

Tibetan soldiers came to the monastery gate twice. The first time it was seven or eight horsemen, who circled outside the gate, saw the cross-shaped hollow chiselled in the lintel, pointed and said a few words, and left. The second time twenty or thirty came and beat on the gate with the backs of their swords. Sargis drew up the guard brothers inside the gate in a shield wall; Yisi opened the small door and went out, and an old Tocharian believer of the monastery, who in his youth had dealt in horses in Tibetan country, followed behind him to interpret; they talked a long while, and Yisi had two sacks of rice carried out of the store and set outside the gate. The leading Tibetan looked at the rice, looked at the shield wall inside the gate, had the rice loaded onto a horse, and left. Elder Samaan entered the two sacks of rice in his account book, with a note beside it in Syriac which Jingjing later translated for Yisi: "Rice to ransom the gate."

The Tibetans stayed in Chang'an fifteen days, plundered the treasuries, burned the houses of many wards, set up a member of the imperial clan as emperor, and then left. It was Guo Ziyi who drove them out. Guo Ziyi had had no troops in hand; he gathered a few thousand stragglers in a hurry round Shangzhou, and had men make a great show of banners on the mountains south of Chang'an, lighting fires all over the mountains at night and beating drums, while people in the city took up the shouting; the Tibetans thought a great Tang army had come, broke camp and went west. In the twelfth month the Sage One returned to Chang'an.

In the first month of the following year an edict came down sending Guo Ziyi to Hezhong to command the old Shuofang troops. A few days later an administrative officer of the Guo mansion came to the monastery with a dispatch, saying Lord Guo had petitioned for Yisi to serve on assignment to the Vice-Generalissimo's headquarters, in charge specially of receiving the hu tribal troops coming over from Pugu Huaien's side; he should pack and follow to Hezhong. The officer handed him the dispatch, drank a bowl of water and left. Guo Ziyi himself Yisi did not see. Jingjing stayed in Chang'an to run his gruel kitchen; Yisi took his mace down from the wall, slung his shield on his back, and took to the road again.

When the Shuofang troops at Hezhong heard Lord Guo had come, they ran to tell each other. Over at Fenzhou the soldiers in Huaien's camp grew fewer night by night—ten gone today, a hundred tomorrow—and those who left all went south, to Hezhong, to Guo Ziyi. Among those who came over were many hu soldiers of the Tiele, Tongluo and Pugu tribes; every day Yisi stood at the camp gate asking batch after batch which tribe they came from, which general they had followed, whether they knew anyone in the camp, and when he had asked, reported to the administrative officer to make up the rolls. In the mornings Guo Ziyi ate his millet gruel as usual; in all these days at Hezhong Yisi was not once called into the rear tent. Huaien's son Pugu Yang, commanding troops at Yuci, was killed by his own men. Huaien, with the three hundred-odd horsemen left about him, crossed the Yellow River and went north. North was Lingzhou.

In the second month at Lingzhou the ice on the river began to melt. When the ice melted it made a sound; lying in bed at night you could hear a boom from across the river, and after a while another boom, muffled, like someone beating a broken drum very far away. The people of Lingzhou called this the river opening, and once the river opened it was spring.

Hun Shizhi was forty-nine that year, his beard half white, his left ear still missing its half. He was Acting Military Governor of Shuofang; Guo Ziyi had gone, Huaien had gone too, and the city of Lingzhou was left in his hands, with a few thousand men under him, most of them old soldiers who, like him, had lived half their lives in this city. Every morning he got up and rode round the foot of the city wall. At the west of the city he passed a side courtyard: Yisi's old home. The courtyard gate was shut, the lock on it rusted solid; over the wall, the branches of the apricot tree in the monastery's back courtyard reached across into the side courtyard, not yet in bud. West of the side courtyard was that small monastery, kept by Old Master Jiao alone; the roof had been mended a few years back and no longer leaked, and every evening Old Master Jiao still lit a lamp at the monastery gate.

Last winter there had not been enough firewood, and some soldiers had wanted to pull that empty courtyard down and burn the beams. They brought a ladder, and had just climbed to the top of the wall when Hun Shizhi rode up and told them to come down. They came down, and a bold one muttered that the hu monk had been gone years now, was an official in Chang'an, likely wasn't coming back; these few empty rooms—you couldn't burn them, you couldn't live in them. Hun Shizhi looked at him from the saddle and said: "Your Pa goes off to war, so you pull down your family's house to make a fire? There are two rotten stables north of the city; go and pull those down. This courtyard is not to be touched. Whoever touches one rafter, I'll take one of his bones." The soldiers went off carrying the ladder. Hun Shizhi did not leave at once; he got down from his horse and sat a while on the step at the courtyard gate; on the step lay last year's dry apricot leaves, and he pushed them aside with the toe of his boot before he mounted and rode back.

One day in mid-second month, around the hour of si, a horseman came to the north gate, his horse lathered all over, himself covered in dust; he reined in below the gate, craned his neck and shouted that Military Governor Pugu was returning to his command, and Acting Governor Hun was to open the gate to receive him. The soldiers on the gate tower ran down to report. Hun Shizhi was eating in his headquarters; when he had heard it, he put down his bowl, wiped his hands, sent for a few officers, and said to fetch Zhang Shao too.

Zhang Shao was Hun Shizhi's nephew. Hun Shizhi had an elder sister who had married a Han named Zhang in Lingzhou long ago; the Zhang husband died early, the sister died early too, leaving one child, who at eight came into Hun Shizhi's tent and grew up together with Hun Zhen. Hun Zhen shot the bow, and so did he, though not so true as Hun Zhen; Hun Zhen later went off to war with Guo Ziyi, and he stayed in Lingzhou with his uncle. He was about thirty this year, quick of speech and quick of wit; the grain and fodder in the city, the issues of clothing, were all in his charge.

They sat down in the hall. Hun Shizhi spoke first, saying Pugu Huaien wanted to enter the city. At Fenzhou he'd had tens of thousands under him; now he'd come back with only a few hundred, and a few hundred couldn't take Lingzhou, as Huaien knew better than anyone; what he wanted wasn't this city but the few thousand soldiers in it. He said he meant to shut the gates and let Huaien drink wind outside. The officers in the hall looked at one another, each waiting for someone else to speak first.

Zhang Shao spoke. He said: "Uncle, shutting the gates won't do. He's Military Governor, and you're Acting Governor; has the court sent down an edict stripping him of office? No. His seal is still in his hands. If a military governor comes back to his own command and the acting governor shuts the gates on him, when word of it reaches Chang'an, Chang'an will say only one thing: Lingzhou has rebelled. Besides, he has only a few hundred men and the city has a few thousand; settle his men outside the city, let him bring in only a few, and with no troops in his hands he's a military governor with nothing but a seal; once he's in the city, what can he do?" Hun Shizhi, his hands on his knees, looked at a bit of dry grass in a crack between the black bricks before the hall, and only after a good while said: send two men out to see how many men he really has behind him.

The men sent out came back after noon, and said Military Governor Pugu had camped thirty li to the north; not many men, three hundred-odd horse, the horses thin, many of the men wounded; no baggage carts, no siege gear; Military Governor Pugu himself wore no armour, but sat on a stone at the camp gate eating dry flatbread, and when he saw them even had a skin of water sent to them. Hun Shizhi sat a while longer, stood up and said: open the gate; his men are to stay outside the city; he may bring in only twenty horse; tomorrow at the hour of si I'll go to the gate myself to receive him.

That evening Hun Shizhi called Zhang Shao to his room. A brazier was burning; Hun Shizhi sat beside it, with a jug of wine and two bowls before him; he poured two bowls and pushed one across to Zhang Shao. Zhang Shao took it up and drank; Hun Shizhi did not drink his own, but looked at the brazier, and after a while said: you can write; write a letter to Zhen for me.

Zhang Shao took paper and brush, spread them on the low table, ground the ink, and waited with the brush raised. Hun Shizhi frowned, and for a long time could not squeeze out a sentence; he had fought all his life and written few letters. He said: "All's well in the city." Zhang Shao wrote it. He said: "The river's opened." Zhang Shao wrote that too. He thought a long time again, till a piece of charcoal in the brazier fell in, before he said: "That bow you had when you were small, I've still got it, hanging on the wall of the east wing. When the war's over, come back and get it." Zhang Shao wrote it and looked up waiting for the next line; Hun Shizhi waved his hand and said: that's all. Zhang Shao folded the letter and said that first thing tomorrow he'd have the post station send it to Hezhong.

Hun Shizhi nodded, took up his own bowl of wine and drained it at a gulp, poured another, and said: "When your Ma went, you were eight. She took my hand and told me to look after you. I've looked after you twenty-odd years; you're better at accounts than Zhen." He gave a little laugh, drank the second bowl too, stood up and clapped Zhang Shao on the shoulder, and said: go and sleep, we've an early start tomorrow. "Tomorrow in the gateway, you stand at my left hand. You've sharp eyes; watch his men's hands for me." Zhang Shao assented, got up and went out.

At the hour of si the next day, the north gate of Lingzhou opened. The wall at the north gate was very thick, the gateway ten-odd paces long, very dark inside and bright at both ends; standing in the middle, ahead was a blaze of white, and behind a blaze of white too, and the wind poured in from outside the city and through the gateway with a moaning sound. Hun Shizhi stood in the gateway at the city end, in armour, no helmet, twenty of his guards around him, Zhang Shao at his left hand half a pace behind.

The hooves outside the city drew near; first two horsemen came in, then Huaien. Huaien rode a grey horse so thin its ribs showed one by one; he was thinner himself, his face longer than before, his cheeks sunken, his cheekbones the higher for it; he wore an old robe, no armour, a sword at his waist, and behind him a dozen-odd guards, every one grey-faced with dust. He reined in in the middle of the gateway and dismounted, and the two of them stood facing each other ten-odd paces apart. Huaien's long narrow eyes were narrowed, as they always had been, as though something lay between them and the world. He said: "General Hun, you've grown old." Hun Shizhi said: so have you. Huaien smiled, a smile still only at the corners of his mouth, and said his son was dead, killed by his own men, men who'd followed him for years. Hun Shizhi said he had heard.

Huaien walked a few steps forward, up to Hun Shizhi, looked at him, then looked past him once. Hun Shizhi said: "Your men can camp outside the city; I'll have grain and fodder sent out. You'll lodge in the east wing of headquarters; I've had it made ready." Huaien nodded, said good, and came no further.

Hun Shizhi felt a chill under his left ribs. The blade had gone in through a gap in the armour. Between two plates of the armour, a little below the armpit, there was a seam, ordinarily tied with leather thongs; the blade went in slanting, and went in very deep. It was a short knife, a foot or so long, its hilt wrapped in blue cloth. Hun Shizhi turned his head and saw Zhang Shao's face; Zhang Shao's face was very white, his lips pressed tight; his eyes were not looking at him but at something somewhere behind him, and his hand still gripped the hilt. Hun Shizhi put out his hand and seized Zhang Shao by the wrist. His hand was very big, his forearm thick as a length of tree trunk, and once it had hold it did not let go; Zhang Shao pulled back, pulled once and could not move it, pulled again and still could not, and his face went whiter still.

The guards stood stunned; one shouted "General!" and drew his sword, and the dozen-odd horsemen behind Huaien drew theirs too; the gateway was full of the sound of iron leaving scabbards, knocking back and forth between the two walls. Huaien said: nobody move. And not one man moved. Hun Shizhi still gripped Zhang Shao's wrist; he opened his mouth and no words came; his legs gave way, he leaned back against the brick wall of the gateway, and the wall was very cold. He slid down the wall, still gripping, dragging Zhang Shao bent over with him, until he was sitting on the ground. He raised his head and looked at Zhang Shao and said: "Shao." Zhang Shao did not answer. Hun Shizhi's hand let go. He sat against the wall, his head turned toward the end that gave on the open country; that end was white, very bright, and the wind poured in from there, stirring his beard. He looked at that whiteness with open eyes, and afterwards, his beard no longer stirred.

Huaien walked past him, out of the gateway, into the city. When the soldiers in the city heard, some wept, some cursed, some took up their swords and ran for the north gate, and halfway there were stopped by a few officers, who said: the Military Governor is in the city, the Military Governor's troops are outside it—what can you do if you go? That afternoon Huaien sat in the hall of the governor's headquarters and called in Hun Shizhi's officers one by one to talk, and when he had talked with them sent them out; some went out and went back to their camps, and some went out and did not come back again. By evening, the soldiers in the city of Lingzhou were all Huaien's soldiers. Hun Shizhi's body a few old soldiers took away in secret, rolled in a reed mat, carried out of the city, and buried by the river. That night there was another boom on the river.

That day Yisi was at Hezhong. Guo Ziyi's main camp was pitched outside the city of Hezhong, and people came into it every day—some fled from Fenzhou, some from Taiyuan, some from further off; those who came gave their names, were put into companies, drew rations, and lodged in the camp, and the camp grew more crowded day by day.

The news came on the ninth day. The man who brought it was an old Lingzhou soldier who had escaped from Lingzhou, gone by byways, crossed the Yellow River, and changed horses twice on the way, and by the time he reached Hezhong he was worn thin past recognition. Yisi was in the headquarters tent just then, with two newly arrived Tongluo men waiting for a decision, while the administrative officer read Guo Ziyi a grain register at the desk. The old soldier came in, knelt, and began to speak at once; he spoke very confusedly, this sentence before that, some things twice. Guo Ziyi listened throughout and did not interrupt him. When the old soldier had finished, he lay on the ground weeping; Guo Ziyi had him helped out, given food, and told to rest. The administrative officer's grain register, read halfway, stopped at a figure, and he read no further.

Yisi stood in a corner of the tent and remembered that campfire at Fengxiang. That night the fire had nearly died, leaving only a heap of red embers; Hun Shizhi had come out of the dark and squatted by the fire to warm his hands, the half-ear twitching in the firelight, and said that if anyone wagged his tongue again about Yisi preaching in camp, let the lad come to him; and when he had said it he clapped Yisi on the shoulder and left. That clap had been very hard; Yisi's shoulder had hurt half the day. He remembered too the parade ground outside Lingzhou: when the three bouts were done, Hun Shizhi had asked what he did all day following Lord Guo, and he said he saw the road, and Hun Shizhi said then see the road true; one wrong step on your part, and all of us coming after will jump into the pit one by one. When he had said it Hun Shizhi turned and went; Hun Zhen was waiting for him at the edge of the ground, and father and son went back to the camp one behind the other.

That evening the post station brought another letter, from Lingzhou, with Hun Zhen's name on the wrapper; the courier said it had been sent from Lingzhou ten-odd days before. The administrative officer presented the letter; Guo Ziyi looked at the wrapper, did not open it, gave it to Yisi, and said: "Hun Zhen is at the archery ground behind the camp. Take it to him."

Hun Zhen was at the archery ground behind the camp. He knew already: when the old Lingzhou soldier came into camp, it was Hun Zhen he had looked for first. The archery ground was empty, only he alone, standing behind the line a hundred paces from the target, shooting arrow after arrow. He had thirty arrows in his quiver; he shot one, drew one, nocked it, drew, loosed; the bowstring sounded once, the target sounded once.

Yisi went and stood beside him, waited till he had shot all thirty, and only then held out the letter. Hun Zhen put down his bow, took the letter, opened it and read. The letter was very short, and he read it very slowly; when he had read it, he folded it and put it inside his coat, then took up his bow again, drew an arrow, nocked it. Yisi did not know what was written in the letter; he saw only that as Hun Zhen put the letter away, his hand pressed once against his chest.

He walked to the target, pulled the arrows out one by one, gathered them in his fist, walked back and put them in the quiver, and shot thirty more. The arrows in the target were packed in a cluster, some grazing the feathers of the one before. He put down the bow again and started toward the target. Yisi went ahead of him, reached the target, and pulled the arrows out one by one; they were in very deep, and some would not come, so he pressed one hand against the target and with the other gripped the shaft and slowly twisted it out. When he had pulled them all, he gathered them into a bundle, walked back, and held them out to Hun Zhen. Hun Zhen looked at the bundle of arrows, took it, drew one out, and set it to the string.
