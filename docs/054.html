Volume Nine　The Translation Hall

Chapter 54　Narsai

In the autumn of the sixth year of Zhenyuan a carpenter in the West Market began selling crosses. They were whittled from jujube wood, two inches or so long, the four ends rounded off, threaded on a red cord to hang round the neck. The carpenter had set out a small table on the north street of the West Market with several dozen of them in rows, and in front of the table a wooden sign that said: Crosses of the Daqin Monastery. Ward off weapons, ward off plague; children who wear them are not frightened. Twenty coins apiece.

The first to bring word of it was Lingbao. He came back from making up medicine in the West Market, having bought one, and laid it on the table in the scripture room. "Look, Brother—there's a character fu, blessing, carved on the back too. I know that carpenter. He's the son-in-law of the Kang family in the ward. He was baptized at the monastery the year before last, and on the day of his baptism he wept louder than anyone."

Samaan, beside him, said, "This ought to be reported to the ward headman. Using the monastery's name falsely, selling things and cheating people of their money."

"No reporting," said Jingjing. "I'll go and take a look."

Those mornings the character on his board was yang, sheep. What he was reading was the man who had a hundred sheep and lost one, and left the ninety and nine and went into the mountains after the one that was lost. On the third day of reading it he thought of what had happened at the monastery that year. The Kang daughter-in-law, at the offering once in seven, took the bread and did not eat it, but hid it in her sleeve and carried it home to soak in water for her sick child to drink; the child did not get better, and she came again to take more, saying the bread wasn't strong enough, she needed another piece. An old believer from the south of the city whose son was to marry came to the monastery to ask for a cross to hang on the bridal-chamber door, to beg for a son. Two young soldiers of the Shence Army came asking to be washed, and asked whether after the washing blades and spears would no longer touch them in battle. These people were all still among the ninety and nine, but what they were asking for was not the same thing he asked for every morning facing the psalms.

That day he went to the West Market. A few women were gathered round the carpenter's little table. The carpenter knew him; his face went red at once, and he stood and saluted with crossed hands. "Master Jing."

Jingjing told him to sit, and crouched down by the table himself, and picked up a cross to look at. "Well whittled. Jujube is hard; it's not easy to whittle it like this. Don't be afraid; I haven't come to smash your table. You can whittle crosses, and you can sell them. The wood is yours and the craft is yours. Only the words on that sign—I beg you not to write them. Ward off weapons, ward off plague, children who wear them are not frightened: no one at the monastery has ever said such things, and they aren't in the scriptures either."

The carpenter hung his head and said, "Your Reverence, if I don't write those lines, I won't sell a single one."

"If you can't sell them, whittle fewer."

"There are six mouths in my house."

Jingjing crouched there looking at the sign, and after the space of a few breaths said, "Then take off the words Daqin Monastery. The rest, weigh it up yourself." With that he stood up and left.

The next day Lingbao went to the West Market to look. The sign was still up; the words Daqin Monastery had been blacked out with charcoal; the words below were still there. Samaan made a line in the ledger: A carpenter of the West Market, one Kang, sells crosses; the Master went to admonish him; the monastery's name removed; the rest unchanged.

In the ninth month the Kang daughter-in-law's child died. The child was three, and after autumn came had diarrhoea, diarrhoea for more than a month, until there was only a handful of bones left. The little piece of bread the Kang daughter-in-law hid and took home at each offering once in seven she soaked in a bowl of well water until it was soft, and fed it to the child spoonful by spoonful. Four times she fed it.

The day the child died was a day of the offering once in seven. She came in at the monastery gate carrying the child, walked straight into the hall, walked up to the front, laid the child on the floor in the middle of the hall, knelt, and wept at Jingjing.

"Your bread doesn't work!" she cried, cursing. "Three years I believed in you people, took your bread and soaked it for the child to drink, and the child died anyway! You cheat people. That Lord of yours is a cheat!"

The whole hall stood. Someone went to pull her away, and she shook them off. Sabrisho was about to have the guard brothers ask her to leave, but Jingjing waved his hand, came down from the front, knelt beside the child, and took him up in his arms. The child was as light as a cat, and there was still a chill of well water on his body.

"Sister," he said, holding the child, "the bread is not medicine. I ought to have told you that long ago. I saw you take the bread home, and I didn't say so; that was my fault. This bread is so that people will remember the Lord died once for us. Those who take the bread, when they're sick are still sick, and when they die still die. But when those who take the bread die, the Lord knows them."

The Kang daughter-in-law raised her head to look at him, her face all tears. "Then what do I want it for? What I want is my child alive! The Lord knows him—what use is that? I don't know the Lord. I know my child!"

Jingjing could not answer. He knelt in the middle of the hall holding the child, and thought of that bowl of well water with the bread soaked in it. The child had had diarrhoea for more than a month, and what he drank was well water, cold. From childhood one mouthful of cold water had given Jingjing a night of bellyache; he knew what cold water was. The words came to his lips, and he swallowed them back. Pa said what shames a man is a lie. This time he did not lie; he did not speak. How far not speaking stood from lying, kneeling there in the hall, he did not know.

He said only: "The monastery will bury this child. Outside the Kaiyuan Gate, among Elder Narsai's rows of wooden stakes. Every year at Cold Food I'll go and see him."

When the Kang daughter-in-law had wept herself out, she stood up, did not take the child, turned, and went out of the hall door. That afternoon, at the hour of wei, Narsai and Siyuan pushed the cart out and buried the child. On the stake was written: The Kang child, three years, ninth month of the sixth year of Zhenyuan.

That night Jingjing sat in the scripture room with the board on his knees until the clapper sounded, and wrote not one character. The next morning he still wrote the character sheep, and when he had written it, wrote beside it a small character liang, cold, and wiped it off again.

A few days later, in the little room where business was discussed, Sabrisho argued with Jingjing in front of all the elders.

"This thing came of the Master's leniency." His beard jutted up and down. "Bread let carried home, crosses let taken off and sold, those who beg for sons given them, those who beg not to be pierced not turned away either—lenient until in the end the bread has become medicine and the cross a charm, and a child's dead, and what people curse is the Lord. I've kept the rules all my life. This time I want two rules set. The offering bread to be eaten on the spot, not to be carried out of the hall door. Crosses to be given only to those who have been baptized and heard the preaching; not to be given to those who want a charm."

Samaan put in, at his side, "That carpenter in the West Market is still selling, and the sign is still up. The monastery's name is blacked out, but people know just the same that it's the Daqin Monastery's."

Jingjing heard them out and said, "The first, I accept. From the next offering once in seven, the bread is broken in the hall and eaten in the hall. If anyone wants to take it home to someone sick, let him carry the sick one here, or let Lingbao go to his house and give it at the bedside. I was wrong first; this rule should be set. The second, I don't accept. The cross is a sign. Those who want a charm take it away, and what's in their hearts is a charm; but the cross hangs round their necks, and some day they'll think to ask: who is the man on this? And there'll be someone to tell them. If nine of ten take it for a charm, I still want the one who can ask."

"And the nine?" said Sabrisho.

"The nine, I pray for every morning."

Sabrisho looked at him, stroking his beard again and again. "The first, then. The second—I'll remember it. If anything comes of it later, I'll speak again."

Samaan entered it in the ledger: Offering bread not to leave the hall door, agreed by all. Crosses as before; Sabrisho judged this wrong.

In the tenth month of that year Narsai brought a living man back from outside the Chunming Gate.

Narsai was nearly eighty that year, his back badly bent, his brow ridge as high as ever. He had pushed that little cart for the dead fourteen years; the shafts were worn shiny and the wheels had been changed three times. These last years he could no longer push it. The Mi family's youngest son, Siyuan, was a monk at the monastery and went with him every day; Narsai held the shafts in front, and Siyuan pushed behind.

That day they went out of the Chunming Gate and saw a man curled up in the grass by the moat, his clothes rotted to strips, his feet bound in rags with black blood showing through, his face buried in his arm. Siyuan went to pull at his legs. The legs were stiff, but the man stirred, and there was a sound in his mouth. Narsai crouched and put his ear close, and made out that the man was singing in Syriac, very low, broken, one of the psalms: My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me.

In the twelfth month thirty years before, outside the Chunming Gate, a young man in a cangue had gone off down the Lantian road, and what he had sung all the way as he walked was this psalm.

Narsai knelt in the grass and turned the man's face over. It was dark and thin, the whites of the eyes yellow, and round the neck an old ring of scar, worn by a cangue.

"Baaluo," Narsai called.

The man opened his eyes and looked at him, looked a good while, and knew him. "Elder… you're still alive."

They laid him on the cart for the dead and pushed him back to the monastery. Jingjing came out, saw a living man lying on the cart, and saw too that Narsai's hands on the shafts were trembling. Siyuan said it was Baaluo, and Jingjing stood still. The last time he had seen Baaluo was at the Chunming Gate in the twelfth month of the first year of Shangyuan. He had been fifteen, gone with Pa to see him off, and Baaluo had shouted at Pa: taking money to buy the escorts' smiles. He went over, crouched by the cart, felt Baaluo's forehead—it was frighteningly hot—and sent Lingbao to heat water, asked Shiyiniang to cook gruel, and had someone clear out the empty room in the side courtyard.

Baaluo lay in the side courtyard a month. He had been released in the first year of Zhenyuan, when the Son of Heaven's great amnesty at the southern suburb let exiles go home. He walked out of Lingnan, over the Dayu Ridge, fell ill at Hongzhou, scraped hides for people two years in a leather-worker's shop, saved a little money and went on; fell ill again at Xiangzhou, scraped hides another year, went on again; walked five years, and only then reached the outside of Chang'an, reached the Chunming Gate, and his legs gave way, and he fell by the moat. When he told this he did not look at anyone; his eyes were on the roof, as if he were telling of someone else. Shiyiniang brought hot gruel, and he drank it; she brought hot water, and he would not drink it, and had someone draw him cold water from the well. Shiyiniang would not. He held on to the wall and walked to the well himself, drew a ladleful, drank it, drew another and poured it over his head, and when he had poured it stood shivering by the well, reciting scripture. Jingjing watched from the gallery and did not stop him.

When he could get up, he went round the monastery. He could not read. He stood before the stele in the front courtyard with his head tilted back to look, and had a young monk read it to him. The monk read from the beginning: Taizong, then Gaozong, Xuanzong, Suzong, Daizong, the present emperor, until he came to the great benefactor, Grand Master of Splendid Happiness with Golden Seal and Purple Ribbon, Deputy Military Governor of Shuofang, Acting Director of the Palace Administration, granted the purple kasaya, the monk Yisi. Baaluo told him to stop, and asked what a purple kasaya was. The monk said it was granted by the Son of Heaven. Baaluo asked where Master Yi was now. The monk said Master Yi had died in the winter of the first year of Xingyuan and was buried outside the Kaiyuan Gate. Baaluo's feet did not move. He told him to read on, and when it was finished, asked: "On this stele, where is the Lord's name?" The monk pointed it out to him—Aluohe, Mishihe—a few places in all. Baaluo asked again: "The Son of Heaven's names—how many places?" The monk could not count them.

That winter, at the offering once in seven, he came into the hall for the first time and stood in the last row. When they reached the passage of the hymn in Tang, In the highest heavens, deep reverence and praise; the great earth holds the thought of peace for all, the whole hall sang along, and Baaluo did not sing. When the passage was done he suddenly spoke, very loud, his voice hoarse:

"I have something to say. In Lingnan, in the miasma, I burned with fever three days and three nights, and on the third night the Dharma King Baolu stood at the head of my bed with iron chains on his hands and said to me: go back to Chang'an; the monastery in Chang'an has become unclean. I've come back, and I've seen it. This monastery—on its gate hangs the Son of Heaven's plaque, in its courtyard stands the Son of Heaven's stele, its elders wear the Son of Heaven's kasaya, and in its hall they sing the words of Buddhist monks and Daoists. The monastery's people go to treat the wounds of the eunuchs' soldiers, go to fill in registers for the eunuchs' clerks, go to beg the generals of the Shence Army to put in a word. The Dharma King Baolu went into the city of Rome in iron chains, and you go in at the eunuchs' gates in purple kasaya! This monastery isn't the Lord's monastery any more; it's Chang'an's monastery, the Son of Heaven's monastery. It must be made clean, clean from the beginning; if it isn't, the Lord won't come!"

The whole hall turned to look at him, then turned back to look at the front. Sabrisho's beard was trembling. He was past seventy, and his voice was still hard. He was about to speak, but Jingjing raised his hand a little, and Sabrisho held it in.

Jingjing came down from the front and walked all the way to the last row and stood face to face with Baaluo.

"Of what you say, some is so," he said. "On the stele there are many of the Son of Heaven's names and few of the Lord's; I wrote that, and knew it when I wrote it. The monastery's people treat the wounds of Shence Army soldiers—that is so; I decided it, and knew it when I decided it too. The eunuchs' clerks came to register us, and the monastery filled it in; General Luo spoke for the monastery, and the monastery thanked him—all so. For each of these things I can tell you why. But today's worship isn't finished, and I won't speak of it in the hall. When worship is over, if you're willing to listen, I'll tell you in the scripture room. If you're not willing, say what you have to say; I won't stop you."

He turned and said to the whole hall: "What Baaluo said, you have heard; what I said, you have heard too. Who is right, think for yourselves; and when you've thought, decide for yourselves."

He went back to the front and went on leading the worship, taking it up from the passage after the hymn in Tang. Baaluo stood in the last row until the worship was over. When the offering bread was passed to him he did not take it, but turned and went out of the hall door.

He did not go to the scripture room. Sabrisho went to Jingjing and said that by the rules, insulting the elder publicly in the hall merited being struck from the register. Jingjing said, "He's a man come back under amnesty. The patriarch's instructions say that striking from the register must be reported to the Patriarchate, and no one is struck off before the reply. Besides, what he said wasn't all insult." Samaan made a line in the ledger: At the offering once in seven, Baaluo said publicly that the monastery was unclean; the Master did not rebuke him; let the hearers choose. When he had written it he raised his head and said, "Master, this line—someone will bring it up one day."

"Then let them bring it up," said Jingjing.

In the twelfth month of that year Baaluo moved out of the monastery to the waste garden west of the Qujiang Pool in the south of the city, the very ground where rice had been given out in the spring of the first year of Qianyuan. There were a few half-fallen houses in the garden; he patched them with bricks he picked up and moved in. Four households went with him, all from the people of other wards outside the gruel shed in the ward street who couldn't get gruel; he had talked to them one by one beside the pot this month and more. One of them was the Kang family. The Kang daughter-in-law went with him, and in the south of the city Baaluo told people that the Kang child had died of drinking the monastery's bread water.

The night before he moved out, Narsai went to the side courtyard to find him. Siyuan helped Narsai there and waited outside the door, which was not quite shut.

"That year you threw water on the altar at the fire-temple in the Liquan Ward," said Narsai, "I spoke for you all at the monastery. I said your hearts were good, I said the elders weren't clean enough, I said you were right. In those days I was like you. I thought those who could keep the rule should keep it, and those who couldn't should be punished. I recited the law as if I were driving nails."

He drew a breath and went on: "Later I did something. In the summer of the eleventh year of Dali the stones by the well were wet, the scholar-tree flowers had fallen all over the ground, and Elder Yonan slipped. I had the knife for trimming pens in my hand. I went to help him up, and didn't put the knife down. All my life I'd held up the rules, and that one time I reached out to hold up a man, the knife was still in my hand. I've pushed the cart fourteen years and gathered thousands of dead, and only now do I know: with a knife in your hand, even helping a man up can kill him."

Baaluo lay on the bed and did not look at him. "The Elder's old and gone soft. The Elder pushes his cart to redeem his own sin. I don't push a cart. I've killed no one. I suffer for the Lord. My suffering I suffered in Lingnan, under the cangue. What little suffering there is on a cart for the dead isn't suffering."

Narsai did not argue with him. He stood a moment at the door and said, "You're going tomorrow. It's cold on the road. Don't pour cold water over yourself any more." Then he had Siyuan help him back.

Narsai died on the ninth of the first month of the next year. That night it snowed, and he pushed the cart alone out of the Kaiyuan Gate. When Siyuan got up in the morning he could not find him, and the cart was gone too. He went after him and found him in the burial ground. He was sitting in the snow, his back against the cart, the cart empty, in front of him the last stake of the third row, nothing yet written on it, a stick of charcoal gripped in his hand, snow on the charcoal.

When Jingjing got there, Siyuan was still kneeling in the snow, and said, "The Elder meant to write. He didn't finish."

Narsai was buried beside Yisi, in front of his own rows of stakes. Jingjing pulled out the stake with nothing written on it and wrote on it with the charcoal the name Narsai in Tang characters, and beside it in Syriac letters, and set it at the head of his grave. Of all those rows of stakes, it was the first to bear the name of one of the monastery's own. Baaluo came, leading those four households, and stood outside the burial ground under a dead tree, and never came closer. When the people dispersed, he left too.

That evening Siyuan pushed the cart for the dead back from the burial ground to the monastery, pushed it into the back courtyard, set down the shafts, and went to find Jingjing.

"Master, the Elder's cart—from now on I'll push it."

"Alone, can you push it?"

"Alone I can't. I'll find two young fellows from the ward, give them gruel, and have them help push," said Siyuan. "The orphans the Elder has looked after these years too. I've been helping him look after them for a long time already. From now on that's mine too."

Jingjing looked at this youngest son of the Mi family, not tall, but broad in the shoulders, the snow water of the burial ground still on his sleeves. "Good," he said. "From now on it's yours."

The next morning Jingjing read the same passage, the hundred sheep and the one lost. When he came to And when he hath found it, he layeth it on his shoulders, rejoicing, he thought of Narsai that day kneeling in the grass outside the Chunming Gate, turning Baaluo's face over. On the board he wrote the character kang, to carry on the shoulder.
