Volume Nine　The Translation Hall

Chapter 54　Narsai

In the autumn of the sixth year of Zhenyuan, a carpenter in the West Market began selling crosses. The crosses were whittled of jujube wood, two inches or so long, the four ends rounded into knobs, threaded on red cord to hang at the neck; the carpenter set out a small table on the north street of the West Market, several dozen ranged on it, and before the table stood a wooden board on which was written: "Crosses of the Daqin monastery: ward off weapons, ward off plague; children who wear them are not frightened." Twenty coins apiece. The first to tell Jingjing was Lingbao; coming back from making up medicines at the West Market, he bought one and laid it on the table in the scripture room. Jingjing picked it up and looked at it; the cross was not badly whittled, and on its back a character was carved: fu, blessing. Lingbao said he knew the carpenter: the son-in-law of the Kang family of the ward, baptized at the monastery the year before last, who at his baptism wept louder than anyone. Samaan beside them said this should be reported to the ward headman: using the monastery's name falsely, selling things to cheat people of money. Jingjing said he would not report it. He said he would go and look.

Those few mornings the character on his board was yang, sheep. What he read was the passage where the Lord says: a man has a hundred sheep, and loses one, and leaves the ninety and nine and goes into the mountains to seek the one that is gone astray. He read it several days, kept reading this passage, and on the third day thought of the monastery's affairs that year. The Kang daughter-in-law, receiving the bread on the Lord's Day, did not eat it, but hid it in her sleeve and took it home and soaked it in water for her sick child to drink; the child did not get better, and she came again to receive it, saying the bread wasn't strong enough; she wanted another piece. An old believer's son south of the city was to be married, and came to the monastery to ask for a cross, saying it would hang on the door of the bridal room, to pray for a son. Two young soldiers of the Shence Army came asking to be washed, and asked whether once washed, swords and spears would not touch them in battle; Lingbao said there was no such word; the two soldiers said then what's the washing for? and left; and a few days later came again, saying someone in camp had been washed, and a sword struck his arm and it didn't break; was that because he wasn't washed enough? Jingjing thought: these people were all still among the ninety and nine; but what they asked for, and what he asked for that day facing the psalms, were not the same thing. When he had thought so far, he wiped the board and wrote sheep once more, and that day went to the West Market.

A few women stood round the carpenter's little table; the carpenter knew him, and his face went red at once; he stood up and saluted, saying: Great Virtue Jing. Jingjing told him to sit, and himself squatted by the table, picked up a cross to look at, and said it was well whittled; jujube wood is hard; to whittle it like this isn't easy. He said he hadn't come to smash your table; the crosses you may whittle, and sell too; the wood is yours, the craft is yours; but the words on that board, he begged you not to write: ward off weapons, ward off plague, children who wear them are not frightened; these words no one in the monastery has ever said, nor has the scripture. The carpenter bowed his head and said: Great Virtue, without these words not one will sell. Jingjing said: if they don't sell, whittle fewer. The carpenter said he had six mouths at home. Jingjing squatted there looking at the board for the space of a few breaths, and said: then take out the three characters "Daqin monastery"; the rest, weigh it yourself. He stood up and left. The next day Lingbao went to the West Market to look: the board still stood, the three characters "Daqin monastery" blacked out with charcoal, the characters below still there. Samaan heard it and entered a line in the accounts: The carpenter Kang of the West Market sells crosses; the Master went to persuade him; the monastery's name removed; the rest unchanged.

In the ninth month the Kang daughter-in-law's child died. The child was three; after autumn set in it had the flux, and passed for more than a month, till it was only a handful of bones; every Lord's Day the Kang daughter-in-law took home that little piece of bread hidden away, soaked it in a bowl of well water, soaked it soft, and fed it to the child spoonful by spoonful; four times she fed it. The day the child died was a Lord's Day; she came in at the monastery gate carrying the child, walked straight into the hall, to the front, laid the child on the floor in the middle of the hall, knelt, and wept at Jingjing, weeping and cursing: your bread doesn't work; she had believed in you three years, received your bread, soaked it for the child to drink, and the child still died; you cheat people; that Lord of yours is a cheat. The whole hall stood; someone went to pull her away, and she flung him off; Sabrisho was going to have the guard brothers see her out; Jingjing waved his hand, came down from the front, knelt beside the child, and picked the child up; the child was as light as a cat, and there was still a chill of well water on it.

Holding the child, he told the Kang daughter-in-law: the bread is not medicine; he should have told you so long ago; he saw you take the bread home, and did not say it; the fault is his. He said this bread is the Lord's body; it is to make people remember that the Lord died for us once; those who receive the bread, when sick are still sick, and when they die still die; but when those who receive the bread die, the Lord knows them. The Kang daughter-in-law raised her head and looked at him, and said: then what does she want it for? What she wants is her child alive; what use is the Lord knowing him? She doesn't know the Lord; she knows her child. Jingjing could not answer. Holding the child he knelt in the middle of the hall and thought of that bowl of well water with the bread soaked in it: the child had the flux, passed for more than a month, and drank well water, cold; from a child he himself, at one mouthful of cold water, had a night of stomach trouble; he knew what cold water was. These words came to his lips, and he swallowed them back. He thought: Pa said what shames is the lie; this time he wasn't lying; he was not saying; how far apart not saying and lying were, kneeling in the hall, he did not know. He said only: this child the monastery will bury, outside the Kaiyuan Gate, among Elder Narsai's rows of stakes; every year at the Cold Food festival he would go to see him. When the Kang daughter-in-law had wept her fill, she stood up, did not take the child, turned, and went out of the hall door.

That day at the hour of wei, Narsai and Siyuan pushed the cart and buried the child outside the Kaiyuan Gate; on the stake was written: the Kang family's little son, three years old, ninth month of the sixth year of Zhenyuan. That night Jingjing sat in the scripture room with the board on his knees and the charcoal in his hand, sat till the wood was struck, and could not write one character. The next morning he still wrote that character sheep, and when it was written, wrote beside it a small character liang, cold, and wiped it out again.

A few days later Sabrisho contended with Jingjing in the small council room before all the elders. He said this business was the Master's leniency come home to roost: let people take the bread home, let people take the crosses and sell them, give to those asking for sons, and not drive away those asking to be proof against swords either; lenient till in the end the bread became medicine and the cross a charm; a child died, and the one they curse is the Lord. He said he had kept the rule all his life, and this time he would lay down two articles: the offering bread to be eaten on the spot, not to be carried out of the hall door; crosses to be given only to those baptized and instructed, not to those seeking charms. Samaan beside him said that carpenter in the West Market was still selling, the board still standing; the monastery's name was blacked out, but people knew all the same it was the Daqin monastery's. Jingjing heard them out and said: the first article, he accepted; from the next Lord's Day the bread would be broken in the hall and eaten in the hall; whoever wished to take some home to the sick should bring the sick here, or have Lingbao go to his house and give it at the bedside; the fault was his first; this article should be laid down. The second article, he did not accept: the cross is a sign; those seeking charms take it, and in their hearts think it a charm; but with the cross hanging on their necks, one day they may think to ask who the man on it is, and then someone will tell them; he would rather nine out of ten took it for a charm, so that the one could ask. Sabrisho said: and the nine? Jingjing said: for the nine, he would pray every morning. Sabrisho looked at him, stroked his beard and stroked it again, and said: the first article, then; the second he would remember, and if anything happened in future, he'd speak again. Samaan entered in the accounts: The offering bread not to leave the hall; settled by the council. Crosses as before; Sabrisho held it improper.

In the tenth month of that year, outside the Chunming Gate, Narsai brought back a living man.

Narsai was nearly eighty that year, his back badly bent, his brow-bones still as high; that small death-cart he had pushed fourteen years, its handles worn shining, its wheels changed three times. These last few years he could not push it; Siyuan, the younger son of the Mi family, was a monk at the monastery and went with him every day; he walked in front holding the handles, and Siyuan pushed behind. The masterless dead of the city of Chang'an—frozen to death, starved to death, dead of sickness by the roadside—they gathered, carried them outside the Kaiyuan Gate, buried them, set up a stake, and wrote on the stake the name, and where there was no name, which ward the man died in, and which day. That day they went out of the Chunming Gate and saw by the city moat a man curled in the grass, his clothes rotted into strips, his feet wrapped in rags with black blood showing through, his face buried in his arm. Siyuan went to pull at his legs; the legs were stiff; but the man moved, and there was a sound in his mouth. Narsai squatted and put his ear close and heard that what the man was singing was Syriac, very low, broken off and taken up again, a psalm: "My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me." Thirty years before, in the twelfth month, outside the Chunming Gate, a young man in a cangue going off along the road to Lantian had sung this very one as he walked. Narsai knelt in the grass and turned the man's face over; the face was dark and thin, the whites of the eyes yellow, an old scar round the neck, worn by a cangue. Narsai said: Baaluo. The man opened his eyes, looked at him, knew him, and said: Elder, you're still alive.

They laid him on the death-cart and pushed him back to the monastery. Jingjing came out and saw a living man lying on the cart, and saw too that Narsai's hands on the handles were shaking; Siyuan said it was Baaluo; Jingjing stopped. The last time he had seen Baaluo was outside the Chunming Gate in the twelfth month of the first year of Shangyuan; he was fifteen then, and went with his Pa to see him off; Baaluo shouted at his Pa about using money to buy smiles from the escorts. He went over and squatted by the cart and felt Baaluo's forehead; it was frighteningly hot; he sent Lingbao to heat water, had Shiyiniang boil gruel, and had the empty room in the side courtyard made ready.

Baaluo lay a month in the side courtyard. He had been let go in the first year of Zhenyuan when the Son of Heaven, at the southern suburban sacrifice, proclaimed a great amnesty and exiles were released and returned; he walked out of Lingnan, over the Dayu range, fell sick at Hongzhou, scraped hides for two years in a tanner's shop there, saved a little money, walked on, fell sick again at Xiangzhou, scraped hides another year, walked on again, walked five years before he reached the walls of Chang'an, reached the Chunming Gate, and his legs gave way, and he fell by the city moat. When he told these things he did not look at anyone, but looked at the ceiling, as if telling someone else's affairs. Shiyiniang brought hot gruel, and he drank it; brought hot water, and he would not drink it, but had someone draw cold water from the well for him; Shiyiniang would not; he supported himself along the wall to the well, drew a dipper and drank it, drew another and poured it over his head, and when he had poured it, stood by the well shivering, reciting scripture under his breath. Jingjing watched from the gallery, and did not stop him.

When he could get up, he went round the monastery. He could not read; thirty years ago he could not, and still could not; he stood before the stele in the front courtyard, head tilted back, looking at the cross on the stele's top, at the two dragons, at the lines of characters below, and had a young monk read it to him. The monk read from the beginning: Taizong, Lord Fang, Gaozong, Xuanzong, Suzong, Daizong, the present emperor, Guo, Prince of Fenyang, "the great benefactor, Grand Master of Splendid Happiness with Gold and Purple, Assistant Vice-Military Governor of Shuofang, acting Director of the Palace Administration, granted the purple robe, the monk Yisi"; Baaluo had him stop and asked what the purple robe was; the monk said it was granted by the Son of Heaven. Baaluo asked again where Master Yi was now; the monk said Master Yi had died in the winter of the first year of Xingyuan and was buried outside the Kaiyuan Gate. Baaluo heard it; his feet did not move; he had the monk read on, and when it was finished asked: on this stele, where is the Lord's name? The monk pointed: Alaha, Meshiha, so many places in all; Baaluo counted them and asked again: and how many places the Son of Heaven's names? The monk could not count them all.

That winter, on a Lord's Day, he came into the hall for the first time. He stood in the last row; Jingjing led at the front; when they came to the passage of the Tang hymn, "In the highest of the heavens, deep reverence and praise; on the great earth, all again think on peace and concord," the whole hall sang it; Baaluo did not sing; he stood there, waited till the passage was sung, and suddenly spoke, very loud, his voice hoarse, saying he had something to say. The whole hall turned their heads. He said that in Lingnan, in the miasma, he burned with fever three days and three nights, and on the third night the Dharma King Baolu stood at the head of his bed with iron chains on his hands and told him: go back to Chang'an; the monastery in Chang'an is unclean. He said he had come back, and he had seen it: this monastery hangs the Son of Heaven's plaque on its gate, sets up the Son of Heaven's stele in its courtyard; its elders wear the Son of Heaven's robe; in its hall they sing the words of Buddhist monks and Daoists; the monastery's people go to treat the wounds of the eunuchs' soldiers, go to make registers for the eunuchs' clerks, go begging the generals of the Shence Army to put in a word; the Dharma King Baolu went into the city of Rome in iron chains, and you go into the eunuchs' gates in purple robes. He said this monastery is not the Lord's monastery any more; it is Chang'an's monastery, the Son of Heaven's monastery; it must be made pure, made pure from the beginning; if it is not made pure, the Lord will not come.

Everyone in the hall looked toward the front. Sabrisho's beard was trembling; he kept the rule, past seventy, his voice still hard; he was about to open his mouth; Jingjing raised his hand once, and Sabrisho held it in. Jingjing came down from the front, walked all the way to the last row, to Baaluo, and stood face to face with him. He said: of the things you speak of, some are so: on the stele the Son of Heaven's names are many and the Lord's few—that he wrote, and when he wrote it he knew; the monastery's people treat the wounds of the Shence Army's soldiers—that is so; that he decided, and when he decided it he knew too; the eunuchs' clerk came to make the register, the monastery filled it in, General Luo put in a word for the monastery, and the monastery thanked him—all so. He said each of these things he could tell you why, one by one; today worship is not finished; he would not speak of it in the hall; when worship is done, if you are willing to hear, he will tell you in the scripture room; if you are not, say what you have to say, and he will not stop you. He said too: those of you in the hall, you have heard what Baaluo said, and you have heard what he said; who is right, think it over yourselves; when you have thought it over, decide yourselves. When he had said it he went back to the front and went on leading the worship, taking it up from the passage after the Tang hymn. Baaluo stood in the last row and stood till worship was over; when the offering bread was passed to him he did not receive it, but turned and went out of the hall door.

When worship was over, he did not go to the scripture room. Sabrisho went to find Jingjing and said: by the rule, to insult the elders publicly in the hall is to be cut off; Jingjing said he is a man come back under amnesty; by the Patriarchate's rule, cutting off must be reported to the Patriarchate and not done before the reply—the order says so; besides, what he said was not all insult. Samaan entered a line in the accounts: On the Lord's Day, Baaluo said before all that the monastery is unclean; the Master did not rebuke him; the hearers to choose for themselves. When he had written it, he told Jingjing: Master, this line—some day people will take it up and use it. Jingjing said: then let them take it up and use it.

In the twelfth month of that year Baaluo moved out of the monastery, to that overgrown garden west of the Qujiang Pool in the south of the city, the very place where rice was given out in the spring of the first year of Qianyuan; in the garden were a few half-collapsed rooms; he patched them with bricks he picked up and moved in. Four households went with him, all from among those outsiders from other wards who could not get gruel outside the shed in the ward street, whom he had talked round one by one at the edge of the pots in this month and more; one household was the Kangs; the Kang daughter-in-law went with him; Baaluo told people in the south of the city: the Kang family's child died of drinking the monastery's bread-water; what the monastery gives you is false medicine. Every day before light he poured cold water over himself at the well in the garden, and when he had poured it, sang scripture, in Syriac; those who went with him could not understand it, and sang along with him.

The night before he moved out, Narsai went to the side courtyard once to find him. Siyuan helped Narsai there and waited outside the door; the door was not shut tight, and Siyuan heard some of it. Narsai said: that year when you poured water on the altar of the fire-temple in the Liquan Ward, he spoke for you in the monastery, said your hearts were good, said they were not pure enough and you were right; he was then as you are now; he thought those who could keep the rule should keep it and those who could not should be punished; he recited the Law like driving nails. He said that afterward he did a thing: in the summer of the eleventh year of Dali, the stone by the well was wet, locust flowers lay all over the ground, Elder Yonan slipped, and he had his pen-knife in his hand and went to catch him, and did not put the knife down; all his life he had caught at rules, and that once, reaching to catch a man, the knife was still in his hand. He said he had pushed the cart fourteen years and gathered thousands of dead, and only now did he know: with a knife in your hand, catching a man can kill him too. Baaluo heard it and said: the elder is old, gone soft; the elder pushes the cart to atone for his own sin; he doesn't push a cart; he hasn't killed anyone; he suffers for the Lord; his suffering was in Lingnan, under the cangue; the little suffering on a death-cart isn't suffering. Narsai did not argue with him. He stood a moment at the door and said one thing: you go tomorrow; it's cold on the road; don't pour cold water on yourself; and having said it had Siyuan help him back.

Narsai died on the ninth of the first month. That night it snowed; he went out of the Kaiyuan Gate alone pushing his cart; when Siyuan got up in the morning and did not see him, and the cart was gone too, he ran after him and found him in the burial ground, sitting in the snow, his back against the cart, the cart empty; before him was the last stake of the third row, with nothing yet written on it, and in his hand he gripped a stick of charcoal, snow on the charcoal. Siyuan said the elder had meant to write, and had not finished. Jingjing went, and lifted him out of the snow; he was as light as a bundle of firewood.

He was buried outside the Kaiyuan Gate beside Yisi, in front of his own rows of stakes. On the day of the burial Jingjing recited the scripture, and when it was recited had Lingbao pull up that stake with nothing written on it, and wrote on it in charcoal the two characters Naer-sai, in Tang, and beside them in Syriac too, and set it up on his grave. Of all those rows of stakes, this was the first with the name of one of the monastery's people on it. Baaluo came; he stood beneath a dead tree outside the burial ground and did not come over, those four households behind him. When the burial was done and people had dispersed, Jingjing went over; Baaluo looked at him and asked: when the elder died, did he say anything? Jingjing said no; he had the charcoal in his hand; the stake wasn't written yet. Baaluo did not answer him, but opened his mouth and sang, sang that same one, My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me, and when it was sung led those four households off toward the south of the city.

That evening Siyuan pushed the death-cart back from the burial ground to the monastery, pushed it into the back courtyard, set down the handles, and went to find Jingjing. He said Elder Narsai's cart—he'd push it. Jingjing asked whether he could push it. Siyuan said one man couldn't; he'd find two young men of the ward, give them gruel, and have them help push. Jingjing said good, and said too that the orphans—Elder Narsai had had charge of them these years too; from now on they were his as well. Siyuan said he knew; he'd long been helping the elder with them.

The next morning Jingjing wrote on the board. He read that same passage, a hundred sheep, one lost; he came to "when he hath found it, he layeth it on his shoulders, rejoicing," and thought of that day when Narsai knelt in the grass outside the Chunming Gate and turned Baaluo's face over. He wrote the character kang, to shoulder, and when it was written sat under the lamp and waited for the striking of the wood outside.
