Volume Nine　The Translation Hall

Chapter 54　Jingjing

In the thirteenth year of Yuanhe, Jingjing was seventy-two.

At the start of spring that year, it was his legs that failed first. When he got up in the morning his two legs felt borrowed, would not do as they were told, and he had to sit on the edge of the bed a while, holding on, until they acknowledged their master, before he could stand. Standing, he walked slowly too; from the back courtyard to the front hall, where others took twenty steps, he took forty.

The people of the monastery whittled him a stick. Jujube wood, an old branch sawn from the jujube tree in the back courtyard, whittled very smooth, a strip of cloth wound round where the hand held it.

Leaning on his stick, he went every morning as always to lead the morning office in the hall, every afternoon as always to sit a while in the scripture room, and every evening at dark as always to the gate to light the lamp.

The lamp at the gate hung from an iron hook beside the door. A bronze saucer, half a palm across, its wick twisted from hemp. The hook was set a little high; his father had driven it in years ago, and though his father was short, he had set it high, saying that hung high it would light further. Jingjing was a little taller than his father and could just reach it. Now his back was bent and he could not reach it, and every time he had to go up on his toes.

First he took the lamp down, tipped out the dregs of last night's oil, wiped it clean with a cloth, ladled a spoonful of new oil from the jar into it, drew out a little of the wick, and cut it even with scissors. Then he struck the flint, lit it, and hung it back.

He had done this for thirty-four years. While his father lived, his father did it; when his father was gone, he did it. Once it had taken no more than the time it takes to drink a cup of tea. Now it took half a mealtime.

Jingfu wanted to do it for him.

Jingfu was twenty-three that year, could already lead the evening office, could preach, could keep the accounts. He stood beside him watching Jingjing go up on his toes, watching Jingjing's hands shake, shake so that the flint had to be struck three times before it caught. He held out his hand and said: "Master, let me."

Jingjing said: "No need."

So Jingfu drew his hand back and stood by watching. When the lamp was hung up, he helped Jingjing back.

The winter before, Li Su had died.

Li Su lived past seventy, spent his whole life at the Astronomical Bureau, rose very high, and when he died the palace sent men to offer condolence. His sons gave him a funeral, a fine funeral, and many came, some in purple, some in scarlet, most of whom Jingjing did not know.

Jingjing went. He stood at the back of the crowd, leaning on his stick, waiting while the men in purple and scarlet went in party by party and came out party by party, and only then went in.

He stood a while before the coffin. He thought of many years ago, Li Su standing under the lamp at the monastery gate, telling him: looking from here and looking from anywhere else, they're the same stars.

He wanted to say something, and did not. He made the sign of the cross, turned, and came out.

On the way back he said to Jingfu, who was holding his arm: "Of those as old as I, only I am left."

Jingfu did not take him up.

After a while Jingjing said again: "Siyuan doesn't count. Siyuan is younger than I."

Jingfu said: "Brother Siyuan is past fifty too."

"Still younger than I," said Jingjing.

That summer was especially hot.

For months Chang'an had no soaking rain; the wells grew shallow, and the leaves of the locust trees curled at the edges in the sun. The people of the monastery hid in the hall by day and moved out to sleep in the courtyard at night. Jingfu slept in the gallery, snoring, the snores carrying from one end of the gallery to the other.

Jingjing could not sleep. He lay in his room with a thin quilt over him. Others were hot; he was not. His body was always cold, his hands and feet especially, even in summer.

One noon, he asked Jingfu to bring him a bowl of water.

Jingfu went to ladle hot water from the stove. Jingjing called him back: "From the well."

Jingfu stopped and turned to look at him.

"Master," said Jingfu, "cold water."

"I know it's cold water."

Jingfu stood there and did not move. Since he was five he had been in this monastery and heard for a dozen years and more of the Master's fear of cold water. He himself had got up in the night to drink well water, and been caught by the Master more times than he could count. The Master had never once scolded him; only every time frowned, and watched him drink, as if watching someone stuff live coals into his mouth.

"Go," said Jingjing.

Jingfu went. He went to the well under the locust tree, wound up a bucket on the windlass, ladled a bowl, and brought it back. At once a film of fine drops formed on the outside of the bowl.

Jingjing took the bowl, held it in both hands, and looked at it a while.

He thought of Lingzhou. The winter he was ten he had stolen a dipper of cold water in the night, and the next day at table had disgraced himself. He thought of the road to the Patriarchate, his father warning him not to drink cold water on the road, and Uncle Duan beside them laughing aloud. He thought of the gate of Xizhou, Uncle Duan on his grey horse turning his head and saying: don't drink cold water.

All his life, he had been warned, all his life.

He brought the bowl to his lips and drank a mouthful.

Cold. Cold enough to make the roots of his teeth ache, cold all the way down into his belly. He paused, drank another mouthful, and another, and drank the whole bowl.

He handed the empty bowl to Jingfu.

Jingfu took the bowl, looked at him, and waited.

Jingjing waited too.

His stomach did not rumble.

After a while, still it did not rumble.

Jingjing sat there leaning against the wall, and slowly smiled. He smiled without a sound, and the corners of his eyes crowded with lines.

"Afraid of it all my life," he said.

That night he slept very well.

In the autumn, he began to set things in order.

Not all at once. Today he thought of one thing and told it; tomorrow he thought of another and told that. Jingfu followed him about, and for every thing he told, Jingfu wrote it down.

First the accounts. The deeds to the monastery's land and buildings, in which chest; how much rice was used each month for the gruel, from which rice shop it was bought; to whom money was owed, who owed money to the monastery. Jingjing told it item by item, very clearly, not one number wrong. Jingfu wrote it in a register, and when it was written Jingjing asked for it and looked it over, changed two characters, and gave it back.

Then the scriptures. Which cupboard in the scripture room held the Syriac scriptures brought from the Patriarchate, which held the Tang scriptures he had translated himself, which were finished, which were not. The ones not finished, he said: if you can't translate them, leave them for now, until someone can.

"And if there's never anyone who can?" asked Jingfu.

"Then leave them for ever," said Jingjing. "Left alone, they won't spoil."

Then the copy of that decree. The sheet Luo Haoxin had brought thirty-two years before, the sheet Samaan had told him to keep safe. The paper had gone yellow through, its edges so brittle they crumbled at a touch. Jingjing had Jingfu put it in the chest with the land and building deeds.

"A scolding of us is a document too," he said.

Jingfu did not understand, and wrote it down all the same.

Then the three bowls in the cupboard.

Jingjing had Jingfu open the cupboard and take the bowls out one by one and set them on the table. His own, with the little chip in its rim. His father's, which was now the one Jingfu used every day. And one with a notch in its rim, and a pinch of earth in it.

He pointed at the bowl with earth in it and told the story of Du De once through. Told how Du De was of Duling in the capital district, a soldier at sixteen, who changed his father's dressings for twenty days at Changshan; told how Du De played football well; told how Du De held the rear in Hebei, said he'd hold for the time it takes to drink a cup of tea, and held for a cup of tea; told how when Du De died, the hilt of his sword and his hand were glued together with blood and could not be parted, and he was buried with the sword.

"All this my pa told me," said Jingjing. "I never saw him."

Jingfu looked at the pinch of earth. The earth had long since dried right through and caked into one lump, greyish in colour.

"This bowl," said Jingjing. "Don't tip out the earth."

"Mm."

"And don't use it for food."

Jingfu nodded. He thought, and asked again: "And the Master's own?"

Jingjing looked at his own bowl. The little chip in its rim he had knocked in his twenties, and after that he had always drunk his soup from the other side.

"Do as you see fit," he said.

At the end of the year, word went round the city that the palace was going to Fengxiang to welcome the Buddha's bone from the Famen Temple.

It was said that in that pagoda a knucklebone of the Buddha's finger was kept, opened once in thirty years, and when it was opened the realm was at peace. It was said the Son of Heaven had already sent a palace envoy, and in the first month of next year it would be welcomed into the palace and worshipped, and when the palace had worshipped it, it would be sent round the monasteries of the capital in turn for the people to venerate. In the West Market people spoke of it with shining faces, saying that when the time came the whole city would go to see it, some would burn their fingers, some burn their arms, and some had already begun saving money to give to the Buddhist temples.

The people of the monastery heard it, and spoke of it a little at table. An Yan, forty-seven that year, a deacon of the monastery, said: other people's affairs—we don't concern ourselves.

Jingjing sat at the table and listened, and said not a word.

He thought of Fengxiang. Sixty-one years before, his father had been at Fengxiang with Lord Guo, meaning to fight back to Chang'an. He had been in Xizhou then, looking east every night, thinking: Pa is at Fengxiang.

He bowed his head and drank the half bowl of gruel in his bowl.

In the twelfth month, he could not get up.

That afternoon he lay in bed watching the window paper darken bit by bit. When it was so dark he could no longer make out the lattice on the window paper, he knew it was nearly night.

He called Jingfu.

Jingfu came in.

"Bring the lamp," said Jingjing.

Jingfu was taken aback, and went out. After a while he came back holding the bronze lamp from the gate, with a little of last night's oil dregs still in it. He set the lamp on the little stand by the bed, and fetched the oil jar, the cloth and the scissors too, and laid them out one by one.

Jingjing pushed himself up a little, and Jingfu put a pillow behind his back.

He held the lamp in his hands. A bronze saucer, handled for decades until it shone, with a small dent at the edge; what year it had been knocked, he no longer remembered.

He tipped the dregs into a dish and wiped the saucer clean with the cloth. His hands shook badly, and he wiped a long time. He ladled a spoonful of new oil in, and spilled a little on the quilt. He drew out a little of the wick, took up the scissors, cut once, did not cut it even, and cut again.

It was cut even.

He handed the lamp to Jingfu.

"Go and light it," he said.

Jingfu took the lamp and stood by the bed and did not move. He looked at Jingjing as if to ask something.

Jingjing looked at him too, waiting for him to ask.

Jingfu asked nothing. He carried the lamp out.

Jingjing lay back on the pillow and listened to his footsteps cross the courtyard, cross the front hall, reach the monastery gate. Heard the bolt of the gate once. Heard the flint once, and once again. Then could hear no more.

After a while the footsteps came back.

"It's lit," said Jingfu.

"Is it straight?"

"It's straight."

Jingjing nodded.

That night Jingfu did not go back to sleep in the gallery. He spread a straw mat by Jingjing's bed and slept there.

He could not sleep. He listened to Jingjing breathing, breath after breath, very light, very slow, sometimes stopping, stopping so that his heart tightened, and then going on again.

In the middle of the night he heard Jingjing speaking.

The voice was very low. He leaned close to listen, and made out that he was praying. Now in Syriac, now in Tang. In Jingjing's hand was that knotted cord, of twisted wool, black and shining. He held one knot, said one prayer, and turned to the next knot.

"Lord, have mercy."

He turned another.

"Lord, have mercy on Pa."

Another.

"Lord, have mercy on Uncle."

Another.

"Lord, have mercy on Uncle Duan."

Jingfu listened, and counted them one by one. Pa, Uncle, Uncle Duan, Uncle Xu, the elder Ahad, the names of the seven teachers, one by one; some he had heard, some he had not. The elder Yonan. Lord Guo. Du De. Lord Mi. Lord Li. General Luo. And the name of an old Buddhist monk that Jingfu could not understand, that sounded Indian.

Towards the end, Jingjing stopped.

He stopped a long time. Jingfu thought he had fallen asleep and was about to lie back down when he heard him say one more.

"Lord, have mercy on her."

Jingfu did not know who her was.

He did not ask.

Near dawn, Jingfu got up and went to the gate to blow out the lamp.

The lamp was still burning, the flame shrunk to the size of a bean, the oil almost gone. He went up on his toes, then remembered he did not need to, took the lamp down, blew it out, and hung it back.

When he got back to the room it was already growing light. The window paper was greyish white.

Jingjing lay back on the pillow, his eyes closed, the knotted cord still clutched in his hand. His thumb and forefinger pinched one knot, pinched it very tight.

Jingfu stood by the bed a while.

Then he knelt down.

The funeral was held in the first month.

Not many came. The people of the monastery, a few old believers from the ward, a few hu merchants from the West Market, and people of the Mi family and the Luo family. Someone came from the Li family too, a grandson of Li Su's, very young, standing at the back with his head bowed all the while. The city was busy welcoming the Buddha's bone, the streets a sea of people, and they carried the coffin out of the city squeezing through the gaps in the crowd. As they squeezed through, no one turned to look at them.

Siyuan recited the prayers. When he had finished, he came over to Jingfu and said: "The Master told me long ago. From today, you are in charge."

Jingfu said: "Brother, you're older than me."

Siyuan said: "I have charge of the orphans."

When he had said it, he turned and went to help fill in the earth.

When they came back from burying Jingjing, Jingfu went to clear his room.

There were not many things in the room. A few robes, a pile of drafts, two very old rolls of journal, the paper yellowed, written on the road the year he was ten. And against the head of the bed leaned a wooden board, written all over in charcoal, written and wiped and written again, one layer pressed on another.

Jingfu picked it up and looked.

It was all the same character.

He looked a long time, set the board back at the head of the bed, and did not wipe it.

That evening, Jingfu went to light the lamp.

He took the lamp down, tipped out the dregs, wiped it clean, added new oil, drew out a little of the wick, and cut it even. He did it very slowly, one thing after another, as if afraid of leaving something out.

He struck the flint once, and it caught.

The hook was set high. The Master had always had to go up on his toes.

Jingfu did not need to. He hung the lamp up.
