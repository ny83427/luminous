Volume Three　The Pamir Road

Chapter 11　Ahad

The elder Ahad's village lay five days' journey north of Ctesiphon.

Jingjing's uncle let them go, and sent a young deacon of the Patriarchate to show the way. Old Duan said he was going too; the boy was his to bring, and he would not leave him by a single step. Xu Jing stayed at the Patriarchate to look after the camels and the baggage.

They hired a boat and went up the river. It went slowly against the current, the boatmen hauling on the towrope from the bank, edging it forward step by step. On both banks were date palms, reeds, field after field of wheat; the wheat had been harvested, and only stubble was left.

The elder Ahad sat in the bow the whole way.

He did not pray or turn his cord; he only sat and watched the banks. When he saw a mud house he looked at it a long time; when he saw a woman washing clothes at the river's edge he looked at her a long time too. Sometimes he would suddenly point at the bank and open his mouth as if to say something, and not say it, and lower his hand.

On the fourth day he pointed at a big rock jutting out from the bank and said: "When I was little I fell on that rock and knocked out half a front tooth."

He opened his mouth to show Jingjing. Half a front tooth was indeed missing; Jingjing had never noticed it the whole way.

"Then you're not far from your village," said Old Duan.

"Not far," said the elder. "One more day."

That night he did not sleep at all.

The village was even smaller than he had said.

A few dozen households, mud walls, flat roofs, and on the roofs dates drying, figs drying, purple and yellow, patch after patch. At the edge of the village there was a little church with a cross carved on its door, and a few old men sat outside it in the sun.

The elder Ahad stood at the edge of the village a long time.

He went into the village. He walked very slowly, looking as he went. At the mouth of a lane he stopped, glanced down the lane, walked on, went a few steps, came back, and turned into the lane.

At the end of the lane there was a house. It was newly built, the walls freshly plastered with mud, the door new too. Outside the door there was a tree.

A fig tree.

It was very old, its trunk too thick for one man to put his arms round, and half of it was dead, the bark peeling, the grey-white wood showing underneath. The other half was still alive; its branches reached out over the top of the wall, the leaves big and thick, and under the leaves still hung a few figs nobody had picked, purple almost to black.

The elder stood under the tree and put out his hand and touched the dead half of the trunk.

The door opened, and a young woman came out carrying a child. She saw a crowd of strangers standing at her door and started, and called into the house. Out came a man of thirty or so, a sickle still in his hand.

The elder said a few words to him. At first the man's face was guarded; as he listened it changed, and he turned and called into the house again. Out came an old woman leaning on a stick, peering at the elder through narrowed eyes.

Only later did Jingjing learn that the man was the grandson of the elder's younger brother. The elder's brother had died more than ten years before, and the elder's father and mother long before that. The man had rebuilt the house a few years ago; the old house had fallen down, and only the tree was left.

The old woman was the elder's brother's wife. When the elder left, she had not yet married into the family.

She looked at the elder, looked a long time, and said something.

The elder did not put it into words for Jingjing. But Jingjing could see that it made him laugh all at once, laugh till the tears came.

Later on the boat Jingjing asked him what the old grandmother had said.

The elder said: "She said there was always a story in the family, that long ago there was a great-uncle who went off to the east, and year after year he never came back. She said: so it was true."

They stayed two days in the village.

The grandson of the elder's brother killed a sheep and invited half the village to eat. The villagers crowded round the elder asking about the east: what was the Tang like, how big was the city where the emperor lived, did the Tang people eat mutton? The elder answered each thing, and by the end his voice was hoarse and he was still answering.

The priest of the village church came too, a young man in his twenties who blushed when he spoke. He asked the elder whether many people in the east believed.

The elder thought and said: not many. In a city, a few dozen households, a few hundred.

The young priest looked a little disappointed. The elder said: but every one of those households came in by itself, one household at a time.

The young priest listened, blushed, and nodded.

On the afternoon of the second day the elder went to the church alone. Jingjing followed and saw him kneeling at the very back of the sanctuary, in the place where at seven years old, having lit the lamps, he had knelt to listen to the praises. He knelt a long time, and when he got up his knees were stiff again. Jingjing went to help him, and he leaned on Jingjing's shoulder and got up bit by bit.

"The lamps are the same ones," he said.

On the morning they left, the old woman took a string of dried figs down from the roof, wrapped it in a cloth, and pushed it into the elder's arms.

The elder held the bundle of figs, looked back once more at the tree, and only then got on the boat.

Going down with the current the boat went much faster than it had coming up. The elder opened the bundle, took out a fig, broke it in two, gave half to Jingjing and ate the other half himself.

Jingjing ate it. It was very sweet, a little tough, and as he chewed his mouth filled with seeds.

"No mould this time," said the elder.

He laughed, and wrapped the bundle up again and put it inside his robe. All that day he did not speak again, but Jingjing felt he was not the same as when he came. Coming, he had sat in the bow as if waiting for something. Going back, he leaned against the side of the boat and dozed off, and slept very deeply, and snored.

On the day the caravan set out, Jingjing's uncle did not come to see them off.

Jingjing, on his camel, looked back once at the walls of the Patriarchate. The walls were very low, and on top of them stood a man, thin, tall, his right sleeve empty, fluttering in the wind.

Jingjing raised his hand and waved.

The man did not wave. He stood on top of the wall, stood and stood, until the caravan was far off; and when Jingjing looked back again he could still see him standing there, like a stake driven into the wall.

The caravan was several times larger than when they came. Seven teachers, twenty-four monk-soldiers, Old Duan, Xu Jing, the elder Ahad, Jingjing, and several camel drivers hired by the Patriarchate; thirty-odd camels and twenty-odd horses. The camels carried scriptures, medicines, vessels for the services, rations, tents, and several heavy chests wrapped in oilcloth; Jingjing did not know what was in them.

The elder Ahad rode his old camel in the middle of the caravan. He looked back once at Ctesiphon, once at the yellow river, then turned round, and never looked back again.

From Ctesiphon east, it was more than two months to Balkh.

In those two months the elder Ahad taught the seven teachers Tang speech.

Every evening when they had made camp and eaten, the seven sat round the fire in a ring, the elder in the middle and Jingjing beside him. The elder said a sentence of Tang speech, and the seven said it after him.

What the elder taught them was all things they would need on the road. "Thank you." "Please sit down." "How much?" "Where is the water?" "We have come from Daqin."

He taught them Tang manners too. How to bow to an official, what to call an old person, and when someone invites you to eat, the first bowl you must push away a little first; you can't just pick it up and eat.

"How many times do I push?" Ishaq asked.

"Once is enough," said the elder. "Push too much and they'll think you really don't want it, and take it away."

Ishaq thought about it and said very seriously: "Then I won't push at all."

All seven laughed.

But there was one word the elder taught wrong.

When he taught the word for rice, fan, he said wan. He had said it for forty years and never noticed anything amiss. The seven repeated after him—"wan, wan, eat wan"—all together, very neatly. Jingjing, sitting beside him, held it in a long time and then could not, and said in a small voice: "Elder, it's fan, not wan."

The elder stopped short. "I am saying fan."

"You're saying wan."

The elder said it again: "Wan."

Jingjing put his hand over his mouth and laughed. Old Duan, on the other side of the fire, laughed till he slapped his thighs. "Elder, you've been saying wan for forty years. Everybody in the Lingzhou camp knows it."

The elder laughed too. When he had finished laughing, he said: "Then you teach that word."

From that day Jingjing taught alongside the elder. The elder taught the speaking, Jingjing the sounds. When a word the elder said sounded wrong to Jingjing, he corrected it in a small voice. After he had been corrected a good many times the elder was not put out; sometimes he would even say one wrong on purpose and wait for Jingjing to correct him, and when he had been corrected he would nod and say: "Yes, you're right. When you get to Xizhou, you'll be teaching alone."

It took Jingjing more than a month to know all seven teachers apart.

Ishaq was the fattest. He was too fat to ride a horse and could only ride a camel, and after carrying him all day the camel would pant half the night. He loved to laugh, and when he laughed his eyes narrowed to slits. Whenever anyone in the caravan quarrelled, he was the one who went to make peace. His way of making peace was very simple: first he gave each of the two a piece of dry flatbread, and once their mouths were full they could not quarrel.

Babai was the thinnest. His fingers were long and fine, and there was always black under his nails: ink. He had charge of all the scriptures in the caravan, and every evening he opened the chests that held them and counted the rolls one by one, and when he had counted them wrapped them in oilcloth again. One night a great wind blew, sand rattling against the tents, and Jingjing woke and saw Babai lying across the scripture chests, holding the oilcloth down with his own body, all night.

Sabrisho's beard was the thickest, thick as a layer of felt, covering his whole face so that only his two eyes showed. He kept the rules of the caravan. When to rise, when to go, when to rest, who kept watch, who cooked—he decided all of it. In the first days he had a quarrel with Old Duan about where to camp. Sabrisho wanted to camp by the water and Old Duan on high ground, and neither could speak the other's language; one gestured, the other gestured, and they gestured till their faces went red. In the end the elder Ahad went over and said a few words to each side and settled it: draw water by the water, sleep on the slope. Both men snorted, and both did as he said.

Haddad's skin was the darkest, and his eyes were black and shining. In his youth he had sailed on merchant ships to many places and could speak several languages. When the caravan came to a city, it was he who went to buy grain, buy water, find lodgings. He could haggle, he could speak sweetly, and he could speak hard too. Old Duan watched him a few times and said to Xu Jing that if this man had been born in the Tang he'd have made a fine travelling merchant.

The left side of Gabriel's face had been burned, the skin wrinkled, his left eye no more than a slit. The first time Jingjing saw him he shrank back in fright. Gabriel was not offended. He said with a smile that when he was little the house caught fire and he went in to save his sister; his sister was saved, his face was not. His voice was very beautiful and very gentle, and in the evenings after the Tang lessons he would sit by the fire and tell stories from the scriptures. He told of Noah, of Abraham, of Joseph sold into Egypt by his brothers. When he told of Joseph, one of the young monk-soldiers wept.

Narsai did not talk much, nor laugh much. His brow ridge was very high, his nose very straight, his eyes like two stones. When Gabriel told stories in the evening he never went to listen, but sat alone in his tent saying his prayers. Once Jingjing passed the door of his tent and heard him reciting a passage of the law, very fast, one word jumping out at a time, like a man driving nails.

At the Tang lessons he learned slowest of all, and tried hardest. When the elder said a sentence, the others repeated it once; he repeated it three times. If he did not get it right he frowned and said it over and over to himself until he did.

Yonan was the easiest to know, and the hardest.

He looked the youngest. There was not a hair of beard on his face, his skin was tight, his eyes bright, and he walked with his back very straight; from behind he looked no different from the monk-soldiers of thirty or so. Jingjing asked Ishaq how old Yonan was. Ishaq thought and said: past fifty, I'd say.

Jingjing did not believe it.

Ishaq laughed. If you don't believe me, he said, go and look at his hands.

So Jingjing went and looked. Yonan's hands were very thin, the tendons standing out one by one on their backs, the skin covered in fine lines, like a dried-up riverbed. They were certainly the hands of a man past fifty.

Yonan had many strange habits. He washed his hands three times a day, morning, noon and evening—with water if there was water, rubbing them with sand if there was none. He ate very little, only half a flatbread at a meal. He slept very little; when Jingjing woke at night he often saw him sitting outside the tent, looking east, not moving.

At meals the flatbread was in a basket and whoever wanted some took it. Sometimes Yonan sat near the basket and broke the bread and passed it piece by piece to those next to him. Everyone took it. Only Narsai did not. He reached round Yonan's hand and took a piece from the basket himself.

Yonan said nothing either; he put that piece back in the basket and went on passing.

Jingjing saw it twice. He did not know what it meant, and did not ask.

Yonan also often looked at Jingjing.

When Jingjing on his camel looked back, he would see Yonan behind, looking at him. When Jingjing squatted by the fire writing and looked up, he would see Yonan on the other side of the fire, looking at him. As soon as he looked back, Yonan would move his eyes away, very quickly.

Once Jingjing could stand it no longer, went up to Yonan, and asked him: "Why do you keep looking at me?"

Yonan stopped short.

He looked at Jingjing, and after a while smiled a little and said: "You write your characters with such care. I watch you write, and I forget myself."

Jingjing felt a little embarrassed.

Yonan picked up a twig from the ground and wrote a character in the sand. It was a Tang character, written very rawly, the strokes crooked; but Jingjing knew it.

It was 心, xin, the heart.

"This character," said Yonan, "am I saying it right? Xin."

"Yes," said Jingjing.

"You can write Tang characters?"

"Only this one," said Yonan. "Someone taught it to me, many years ago."

The monk-soldiers rose every day before light to drill.

Twenty-four men in four squads of six. Their leader was called Sargis, forty or so, tall and strong. They used a shoulder-high wooden staff, iron-shod at both ends, and in the other hand a small round shield.

The first time Old Duan watched them drill, he watched for half an hour without a word.

Six men stood in a line, shield against shield, staves thrust out through the gaps between the shields. Sargis shouted, and all six stepped forward together and thrust together. He shouted again, and all six stepped back together and raised their shields together. He shouted again, and all six turned together and formed a ring, back to back, shields outward, staves outward.

They moved very slowly, but very together. Together as one man.

When the drill was over Old Duan went to find Sargis and asked him by gestures: is this for fighting, or for keeping a gate?

Haddad, beside them, put their words across. Sargis listened and laughed, and said: "For holding."

Old Duan came back and said to Xu Jing: "It's a holding art. If they really met horsemen charging a line, that wouldn't last long."

Xu Jing said: "They're not here to fight rebels."

Old Duan thought and said: "True." But the next day before light Jingjing saw him squatting at the edge of the drill ground, watching again for half an hour.

In the twelfth month they reached Balkh.

The same little church, the same mulberry tree. Its leaves had all fallen, and under the bare branches sat the old deacon who mended shoes.

He saw the caravan and at first did not know it. He peered a long while, then saw the elder Ahad on his old camel, and the shoe in his hands fell to the ground.

The elder Ahad got down from his camel, went over, and said one thing to him.

When the old deacon had heard it he stared at him blankly, then at the long string of camels behind him, the men in grey robes, as if he did not believe it. The elder Ahad said it again.

All at once the old deacon seized the elder's hand and gripped it, gripped it very tight, and said several things in a rush, fast and urgent, and as he said them he began to cry.

The elder Ahad did not try to comfort him. He only let him grip his hand and let him cry. When he had cried enough the elder bent down, picked up the shoe from the ground, and put it back in his hands.

"What did he say?" Jingjing asked.

The elder said: "He said: from now on, when he mends shoes, he'll have someone to talk to."

That day was the day of the seventh-day offering.

The elder Ahad had the old deacon spread the word in the city that this time seven teachers had come from the Patriarchate of Daqin, and all the believers in the city were asked to come.

Twenty-odd people came, twice as many as last time. Some had heard and come in from outside the city. The sanctuary could not hold them; they stood out into the courtyard, under the mulberry tree.

The elder Ahad did not lead the service himself. He asked Sabrisho to lead it, Gabriel to preach, Narsai to read the law. He himself stood at the door of the sanctuary beside the old deacon, and watched.

Sabrisho led the service without a hair out of place, line by line, step by step, the way he kept the rules of the caravan. Gabriel preached on the prodigal son: how the son spent all his money abroad and was so hungry he fought the pigs for husks, and then came home, and his father saw him a long way off and ran to him and embraced him. His voice was very gentle, and when he came to the father running, an old man in the courtyard sobbed aloud.

Narsai read the law as fast and as hard as ever, one word jumping out at a time. But when he had finished he closed the roll and stood there looking at the twenty-odd people before him a while, and said something in Syriac.

Jingjing asked the elder what the elder Narsai had said.

The elder said: "He said: it has not been easy, keeping the faith here."

At the offering bread the seven teachers stood in a row, broke the bread, and put it piece by piece into the believers' hands. The elder Ahad and the old deacon stood at the very end and held out their hands too, and took a piece.

Jingjing, beside the elder, saw him take the bread and bow his head and look at it a while before he put it in his mouth.

He ate very slowly.

When the service was over the believers did not go.

They knew the caravan would leave first thing next morning. Some went home and brought dried fruit, some brought flatbread, some a jar of sheep's milk, and pressed them into the monk-soldiers' hands. The old woman who had wept holding her bread last time brought a pair of new felt boots and would have them go to Jingjing, saying it would be cold on the road. Jingjing pushed them away once, remembered what the elder had taught—once is enough—and took them.

After dark the elder called Jingjing to the foot of the mulberry tree.

The old deacon had lit an oil lamp under the tree and sat beside it mending shoes. The elder sat down beside him and had Jingjing sit too.

"Get out that paper of yours," said the elder.

Jingjing took out the sheet he had written at the Patriarchate—the church at Lingzhou, the keys, the rafter, Auntie Kang, the old groom, the apricot tree. The elder had him read it through. When Jingjing had finished, the elder thought and added one more thing:

"Tell your pa that I'm looking after his father's and mother's graves for him. Every seventh-day offering I'll take people to the graves and pray there once. Tell him not to worry."

Jingjing wrote it down word by word.

When he had finished, the elder untied the knotted cord from his wrist and laid it in Jingjing's palm. It was twisted from wool, and turned by his fingers for decades until it was black and shining.

"This is for praying," said the elder. "One knot, one prayer. When you don't know what to say, turn a knot and say Lord, have mercy. When you've said it, turn the next."

Jingjing held the cord and could not speak.

"Then what will you use?" he asked.

The elder laughed and pointed at the old deacon beside him. "He can twist one. He says he'll twist me a new one tomorrow."

The old deacon did not understand Tang speech, but seeing them both looking at him, he smiled at them, and bent his head to his shoe again.

The elder stroked Jingjing's head.

"Forty years in the east," he said, "and at the very end, I've walked back. I felt guilty at heart, as if I'd abandoned Lingzhou and abandoned your pa. But you were right: they need someone here too. The Luminous Lord told me to go east, and I went; now he tells me to stay here, and I stay here. Where is it that isn't a place to preach?"

As he said it his face was not at all as it had been in those days at the Patriarchate. He was smiling, smiling so his beard jerked up and down, just as on the day they came down from the Pamirs and he heard the old donkey driver say his grace.

"Don't drink cold water on the road," said the elder.

Jingjing burst into tears.

Early next morning the caravan left Balkh.

Jingjing, on his camel, looked back.

Twenty-odd people stood at the church gate waving. At the very front stood two old men, one short and one shorter, one in that old sheepskin robe, the other still holding a shoe he had not finished mending.

The short one kept on waving. Waving very hard, his whole arm raised, again and again, as if he meant to fling something up into the sky.

Jingjing waved too. He waved till his arm ached, and went on waving.

The caravan went round a rise of earth, and the church gate could not be seen.

Jingjing put his hand inside his coat and found the knotted cord. He turned one knot.

"Lord, have mercy on the elder Ahad," he said under his breath.

He thought, and turned another.

"Lord, have mercy on the old grandfather who mends shoes."

Old Duan, riding beside him, heard it, and said nothing. After a while he took off his own felt cap and pulled it down over Jingjing's head.

Ahead were the Pamirs.
