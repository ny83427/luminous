Chapter 61　The Quota

On the first seventh day after Jingjing was buried, while the sky was still dark, Jingfu went to stand in the middle of the courtyard as his Master had bidden him. He thought he was alone; only once he had stood still did he sense there were people behind him; he looked back: Lingbao stood under the jujube tree, Siyuan by the well curb, An Yan by a pillar of the colonnade, and further back, under the colonnade, at the hall door, beneath the eaves of the west wing, standing, squatting, leaning against the wall, a dark mass of them, some telling knotted cords in their hands, one knot, one line. When the sky greyed he counted them: including the old believers come from the ward, forty-one in all. While his Master lived, the morning office in the hall belonged to everyone, and this standing in the middle of the courtyard outside the hall belonged to him alone—stand a while before light, tell the knotted cord once through, go into the scripture room and read a passage, write a character on that old board from Xizhou; the monastery's people knew, and no one followed. Now his Master was dead, everyone came; Jingfu could not say why, and Lingbao did not say either; only from that day on, every day before light, there were people standing in the courtyard.

Jingfu was twenty-six that year and had been leader a little over a month. His leading was not like his Master's. While his Master lived, the ward headman would clasp his hands on seeing him, the clerk of the Jingzhao prefecture would call him Great Worthy Jing, and when a petty clerk of the Commissioners of Merit's inspectorate came to the monastery, the first thing he asked on sitting down was who had written the characters on the stele; when Jingfu went to the inspectorate to hand in a petition, the clerk did not ask him to sit, and asked him: and that old monk of yours who wrote the stele? Jingfu said he had passed away, and the clerk said: oh, took the petition, put it under a stack of documents, and told him to come back in a few days. His Syriac was what it had been; at the offering once in seven he could not keep up with what the elders chanted; the sounds he made up himself the old believers of the ward could not tell, the few old brothers from the Patriarchate could, and though they could, they said nothing, only bowed their heads. He could not break the bread; the bread had to be broken by Lingbao; in the monastery now only Lingbao could consecrate; Jingfu had not even had hands laid on him as deacon; a deacon must be twenty-five, and the year his Master died he was only twenty-three, two years short; in the eight years his Master was bishop he laid hands on An Yan as deacon and on two old brothers as priests, and both of those died before his Master; with his Master gone, there was no one left in Chang'an who could lay on hands. What he could do was the gruel, the lamp, the weddings and funerals of the ward, the corpse cart, talking with the ward headman, with the inspectorate, with the hu merchants of the West Market; his Tang was good and the people of the city knew him; this was why his Master had chosen him, and he knew it himself: his Master had chosen not his abilities, but this face of his that nobody minded.

In the first month of the fifteenth year of Yuanhe the Son of Heaven died suddenly in the palace; it was rumoured in the city that the eunuchs had done it; people said all sorts of things; the ward headman had every household shut its gate and forbade discussion in the street. The new Son of Heaven took the throne, and the next year the era was changed to Changqing. In the spring of the year of the change, a dispatch came from the Commissioners of Merit's inspectorate, saying that by order of the commissioners' office the monk registers of the monasteries of the capital were to be made anew; each monastery was to list its monks by name according to its quota and report; monks within the quota were exempt from tax and labour and might live in the monastery; those beyond it were ordered to return to lay life, be entered on the household registers, and pay the Two Taxes. The dispatch was brought by an inspectorate officer, forty-odd, unsmiling, who came in at the gate, handed it to Jingfu, and said: your monastery—what your quota is, you know yourselves; within ten days list the names and report to the inspectorate; past the time, the inspectorate will send men to count.

The Daqin Monastery's quota had been fixed in the Zhenguan years; the stele had carved on it that edict of the twelfth year of Zhenguan—the office concerned at once built a monastery in the Yining Ward of the capital and ordained twenty-one monks; then the monastery was still called the Persian Monastery; only in the Tianbao years was the name changed; the name was changed, but that number twenty-one had not changed in a hundred and eighty years. In all these years the monastery's people had never been counted by twenty-one: those come from the Patriarchate, those come from the Western Regions, those who had left home in the ward, those come back from the south of the city—everyone living, eating and working in the monastery—Elder Samaan in his day had entered in the accounts, not one missing; but on the official register they had always reported twenty-one, by the quota; the extra ones the government did not ask about, and the monastery did not bring up. The Commissioners of Merit's office had changed hands several times these years; some were careful, some not; while Jingjing lived the inspectorate's men had come to count twice, and when they had counted, drunk a bowl of broth and looked at the stele, they left. This time the officer said it plainly: those beyond the quota must either return to lay life or leave the monastery; for one name too many on the register, the monastery's leader would answer for it.

That night Lingbao carried the account books into the small room in the back courtyard and turned them page by page, and when he had finished, told Jingfu: monks living in the monastery, the two of them included, were thirty in all. He read them out one by one: over sixty, fourteen: old brothers from the Patriarchate and the Western Regions—Elder Hanan, blind eight years, who had come to Chang'an from Xizhou with a Sogdian caravan in the second year of Baoying, when he was only nineteen; and Tang men who had left home in the ward decades ago—Old Cao, who had drawn water at the well curb forty years and at seventy-two was still splitting firewood. Then himself, fifty-four; Siyuan, a little past fifty, who had pushed the corpse cart thirty years; An Yan, fifty, deacon; Jingfu, twenty-six; Joseph and Elijah, thirty-odd, the men of Merv who had come by sea with Elder David in the fourth year of Yuanhe. The remaining ten were all young men, Tang and Sogdian, the eldest under thirty, the youngest seventeen; Guangzheng, the Shi brothers, the Luo family's Twelfth, and Salang, grandson of old Sargis who had guarded the monastery, were all among them. Jingfu reckoned it through and said: thirty; report twenty-one; nine have to go out. Lingbao said yes, and said: ten days' limit, and today one is already gone.

They debated it three evenings. The first evening Jingfu said the ones to go out should be the old. When he said it his own face went red first, but he said it all the same. He said the monastery from now on must rely on the young: the scripture room needed people to copy scriptures, the gruel shed needed people to carry rice, the corpse cart needed people to push it, the offering once in seven needed people to sing; if the young went, the monastery would be left with a houseful of old men, and the old men would die one by one, and when they had all died, the monastery would be empty. He said if the old men left the monastery, the monastery would keep them just the same, send them food every day, look after them when sick; on the register they wouldn't be monks, but they'd still be the monastery's people.

Lingbao heard him out and did not refute him; first he had him work out an account. He said: once a man leaves the monastery and goes on the household register, first he must have a household; a household needs a house; living in the monastery doesn't count as a household; the inspectorate will come and check; once there's a household, there are the Two Taxes, and the Two Taxes are set by property; an old brother has nothing on him but a robe; the tax isn't much, but the corvée has to be answered—repairing walls, digging canals, hauling things for the government; past sixty some is excused, not all. He said that was for the Tang men; those old brothers from the Patriarchate and the Western Regions had no native place; returned to lay life, where would they be registered? The Jingzhao prefecture would ask what country they were from, and having asked, would send them back to their native place, and their native place was ten thousand li away; send a man of seventy-odd back, and he'd be gone halfway. He said Elder Hanan's eyes couldn't see; away from this monastery he couldn't even find his way to the well. He said if the young went out their life would be harder, but they had hands and feet; they could carry rice, copy books, keep accounts for hu merchants in the West Market; Chang'an was where they were born; they had native places, relatives; they wouldn't starve; if the old went out, they'd die. Jingfu said: then if the young go out, will they come back? Lingbao said that he didn't know; he knew only that the old, going out, wouldn't come back.

The second evening, An Yan spoke. He was fifty; in his early twenties he had gone with Baaluo to the south of the city and lived a dozen-odd years in the straw sheds west of the Qujiang Pool, frozen off half a toe, and it was Jingjing who had trodden through the snow to bring back those seven households. He said Lingbao was right; the old couldn't go out. He said too that Jingfu was right as well; he himself had gone out once in his day, and once out, his heart ran wild; he had been brought back; not everyone had someone to bring him back. He said: or let him go out; he was a deacon, hands laid on him; take him off the register and the hands were still on him; he could still come and help pass the bowls. Lingbao said: you going out alone is only one; that leaves eight short. An Yan said: then let him go out first, alone, and they'd debate the rest. Jingfu would not agree, and said: let the deacon go out, and the ward would say the monastery couldn't even feed its deacon.

The next day Jingfu went alone to the inspectorate again. What he had in mind was a way: the old brothers could not leave the monastery; if their names came off the register, could they count as lodgers—lodging in the monastery, eating the monastery's food, counted neither as monks nor as households; when the inspectorate counted, it could take them for a few old sick men the monastery was keeping. He waited under the inspectorate's colonnade till past noon before the officer came out, heard him out, and still did not smile, and said: lodging—every monastery in the capital wants to lodge; the Jianfu Monastery has lodged over a hundred, the Great Xingshan Monastery over two hundred, all of them old and sick, they say; the inspectorate goes to count, and the old and sick go out the back gate one after another carrying sacks of rice on their shoulders; the commissioners' office no longer recognizes the word lodging. Jingfu said the fourteen in their monastery were truly old; one had been blind eight years; he could take the officer to see. The officer said he wouldn't go and see; seeing, it would be the same words: the quota was fixed in the Zhenguan years; twenty-one is twenty-one; if you think it too few, go and tell Taizong. When he had said it he turned and went in; Jingfu stood under the colonnade; there were people from other monasteries there handing in petitions, and a young novice who knew him came over and asked: Great Worthy, does your monastery have extras too? Jingfu said it had; the novice said theirs had forty extra, and the abbot had already sent men to put up sheds on their estate outside the city; take the names off the register, put the people out on the estate, and the inspectorate had no say over estates. Jingfu went back to the monastery and told Lingbao; Lingbao said the monastery had no estate; that piece of land outside the Kaiyuan Gate was for burying people.

The third evening, Elder Samaan's account books were spread on the table, the lamp-wick sparked several times, and the three of them sat till the second watch and still settled nothing. An Yan went back to the west wing; Lingbao leaned against the wall with his eyes shut; Jingfu got up to go to the back courtyard to relieve himself, and passing the row of rooms on the east side where the old brothers lived, saw Elder Hanan's door open and the man standing in the doorway, one hand on the doorframe, one hand stretched out, feeling the air. Jingfu went over and called him; Hanan recognized his voice and said: it's the leader; he wanted to go to the well; he was thirsty at night, and had drunk the water in his pot; usually Old Cao filled it for him; tonight Old Cao was sleeping like the dead; he'd go himself, but out of the door, he couldn't find the pillars of the colonnade. Jingfu held him up and walked him from the colonnade to the well curb, a dozen-odd steps; Hanan walked very slowly, feeling his way step by step with his toes, felt the stone that stuck up by the edge of the well curb, stopped, and said: here we are; he remembered this stone; when his eyes were still good this stone was here; every morning he washed his face here. Jingfu drew water and filled his pot; Hanan did not go at once, but stood by the well curb and asked him in Syriac: these few nights the lamp's been lit in the small room; he'd heard; was the monastery going to drive people out? Jingfu said no one was driving anyone out; the government was going to count the register. Hanan said he knew; he'd heard "go out" several times; he didn't understand much Tang, but those two words he understood. He said: Leader, he came in the second year of Baoying, nineteen the year he came, and had washed his face at this well sixty years; if he must go out, he asked the leader one thing: have someone lead him outside the Kaiyuan Gate, to those rows of stakes, and he'd sit there himself; the monastery needn't send food; he'd sit a while, and go to see them. Jingfu did not answer this, held him up back to his room, set the pot at the head of his bed, took his hand and let it feel where the pot was, and only when he had found it came out. He went back to the small room; Lingbao opened his eyes and asked where he'd been; he said: to the well; and sat down, and sat on till the third watch, and Lingbao sat with him till the third watch, and neither of them mentioned Hanan.

Early on the fourth day, when morning office broke up, Guangzheng came to find Jingfu, followed by the Shi brothers and the Luo Twelfth—ten in all, the ten young men, not one missing. Guangzheng was twenty-eight, a Tang man from Buzheng Ward; his father had died early, his mother did mending for people in the ward; he had entered the monastery at fourteen and learned Syriac letters from Jingjing, and of the young men he wrote Syriac best; Jingjing had said in his lifetime that this one could take over the scripture room one day. He said that yesterday they had gone to the Commissioners of Merit's inspectorate, the ten of them, and drawn up their own petition, applying to leave the monastery and return to lay life; the officer had taken the petition and said: it's voluntary, that's easy; report the register as twenty; the quota is twenty-one; report twenty, and the inspectorate doesn't care.

Jingfu stood there, his face flushed scarlet, and the first thing out of him was: who told you to go? Guangzheng said no one had told them; the ten of them had talked it over themselves the night before last in the west wing. He said that the first evening, when Uncle An Yan came back to the west wing, he looked bad; they asked, and Uncle An Yan wouldn't say; so the second evening they went and stood under the window of the small room and listened; the account Uncle Lingbao gave, they'd worked out too; work it out any way you liked, the ones to go out could only be them. He said it wasn't that all ten were willing at once; the Shi family's Second was only seventeen; the two brothers were left at the monastery gate in the famine year at the end of Zhenyuan, and had not a single relative in Chang'an; out of this gate, he didn't know where to go; he cried at night; it was his brother who said: we've got no home; the West Market has plenty of storerooms; and then he nodded. He said once they left the monastery their names wouldn't be on the monk register, but they'd still be here: they'd still come to morning office, come before light; still carry rice for the gruel shed, still push the corpse cart, still copy scriptures in the scripture room; only at night they wouldn't sleep in the monastery, and at the offering once in seven they'd stand at the back, among the old believers of the ward. The Shi family's eldest put in a word beside him: the two brothers had already arranged with a hu merchant in the West Market to keep his accounts, two meals provided, and at night to sleep in the storeroom behind the shop; the Luo Twelfth said he'd go home; his home was in Liquan Ward; his Ma would be only too glad. Guangzheng said he'd go back to Buzheng Ward and live with his Ma; his Ma was alone, her eyes had gone bad; just right for him to thread her needles at night.

Jingfu said: and the Two Taxes? and the corvée? Guangzheng said they were young; when the corvée came round they'd go, repair walls, dig canals, go and come back, and come to morning office the same. He said the ten of them had talked it over: whether their names were on the roll or not, Aluohe knew them.

Lingbao had stood by all along; when Guangzheng had finished, he asked him: report twenty on the register, one left empty—have you worked that out? The empty one—who is it kept for? Guangzheng said they had worked it out; the empty one would be kept. He said that of the ten of them, not one ought to take that place; that place was for when the Patriarchate sent someone, for when someone who could lay on hands came from the west; when he came, there'd be room for him on the register. Lingbao heard him out, did not praise him, nodded, and turned and went back to the small room.

Of the ten days' limit, on the ninth Jingfu took the roll to the inspectorate. The roll was written by Lingbao: twenty names, Tang names first and hu names after, set out one by one: Lingbao, Siyuan, An Yan, Jingfu, Joseph, Elijah, Hanan, Old Cao, and the names of twelve more old men; several of the old men's Tang names Jingjing had given them after they entered the monastery; copying them out, Jingfu remembered that when his Master gave a name he always went through the scriptures first, till the whole table was covered in paper. The officer took it, counted it once—twenty—and asked: the quota's twenty-one; why report twenty? Jingfu said: those beyond the quota went out, ten; within the quota stay twenty; one left empty. The officer said: what's it empty for? Jingfu said: waiting for someone. The officer looked at him once, did not ask whom, put his mark on the roll, and said: next time the inspectorate comes to count, it'll count by this; one more, one less, and there'll be questions.

The ten moved out that day. They had little: each a bundle and a quilt; some took a bundle of half-copied scriptures, saying they'd light a lamp at night and go on copying, and bring them back when finished. The Shi brothers' bundle was the smallest; the eldest said the hu merchant's storeroom had plenty of hemp sacks; spread at night to sleep on, they'd be warmer than the monastery's straw mats. The Luo Twelfth's mother came to fetch him, a plump Sogdian woman; she stood at the monastery gate, saw her son come out with his bundle on his back, and beamed all over; at the gate she turned back again and bowed to Jingfu, and said: Great Worthy, he'll come; he'll come every day; just let him dare not come—she'll beat him. Salang left empty-handed; he took nothing; he said the Uighurs' warehouse in the West Market was short of porters and gave lodging; his grandfather had guarded the gate of this monastery all his life; he'd go and guard goods for the Uighurs, and when the goods were guarded, come back before light and stand the same as ever. Guangzheng left last; he swept his own place in the west wing clean, put the broom back where it belonged, and going out at the gate stopped a moment before the lamp to the left of the gate; it was full day, the lamp long since blown out; he squatted down and looked at the ring on the stone block soaked black with oil, rose, and went.

That day Old Cao split no firewood. He sat on the well curb watching those young men go out of the gate one by one with their bundles on their backs, and told Lingbao: he was seventy-two, and had taken a young man's place; his heart couldn't get over it. Lingbao said: you'll have to get over it; if you went out, where would you go? Old Cao said he was a man of the ward; his grandnephew still lived at the east end of the ward. Lingbao said: your grandnephew's family has nine mouths in three rooms; if you went, where would you sleep—on top of the stove? Old Cao laid his axe on the woodpile, got up, and went back to splitting firewood, and split louder than usual.

That night Lingbao put the account books in order in the small room. Jingjing had bidden at the last that Samaan's nine account books be kept with that sheet Luo Haoxin had copied; Jingfu had been busy these days and had never got to it, so Lingbao saw to it himself. The account books were bound in sheepskin; the oldest was worn furry at the edges—Elder Samaan had begun it in the second year of Baoying, entry by entry, in Syriac, small and close, down to the Zhenyuan years; in the later books the hand shook more and more; the last was only half filled, and the back half was in Lingbao's hand. He turned through them book by book; some pages he knew: the costs of the day of Yisi's funeral, the stonemason father and sons' meals for forty days the year the stele was raised, "Master Lü would not accept," "'what is sought' changed to 'what is thanked'"; some pages he had never seen, things Samaan had entered and never shown anyone, all scraps: such a month, such a day, such-and-such old woman of such-and-such ward came for gruel and got none; such a month, such a day, those from outside the ward at the gruel shed were given from the brethren's own portions; and after came a few names, the names of those people from outside the ward; Samaan had asked each one and entered each one, written crookedly, and after one was written: six years old. When Lingbao turned to this page he paused, and remembered the gruel shed in the ward street that year, remembered his brother the first day pouring his own bowl out for a child held by an old woman, and the second day he poured his out too; he was in his early twenties then, and having poured it out went hungry to run errands in the West Market; what that child looked like, he no longer remembered. He smoothed that page, closed the account book, stacked the nine square, wrapped them in a piece of old cloth, and put them on the lowest shelf of the scripture cabinet, on top of Luo Haoxin's sheet and Jingjing's slip with its wrong shi. Then he spread out the draft of the roll reported to the inspectorate that day, and below the empty line at the end added a line in small characters with his brush: "Quota twenty-one; present twenty; one remaining, kept for one who is to come."

The next day, while the sky was still dark, Jingfu went to stand in the middle of the courtyard, and behind him people came one after another, some out of the west wing, some in at the monastery gate; those who came in brought the chill of the ward street on them, stamping their feet, rubbing their hands, and went to stand under the colonnade, to stand among the old believers. When the sky greyed he counted them: forty-one, not one fewer. Guangzheng stood in the last row, by the gate; the lamp outside the gate, which Jingfu had lit the night before, was still burning, its flame shrunk to the size of a bean. Jingfu went into the scripture room, opened the scriptures, read a passage, took up the charcoal, and wrote a character on that old board from Xizhou; what he wrote was liu, to stay.
