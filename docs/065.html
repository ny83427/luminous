Volume Ten　Morning Praises

Chapter 65　Qiniang

At the opening of spring in the fourth year of Dahe, morning devotion numbered twenty-nine. The winter they came back from Luoyang two more had gone: one was the old felt-maker at the west end of the ward, who lay down at night and did not get up in the morning; the other was Old He, He Siniang's Pa, seventy-eight, who fell behind the shop in the twelfth month, lay a dozen-odd days, and at the last had his daughter help him up and sat facing east, and sitting, his head drooped. Old He was buried outside the Kaiyuan Gate; the stake was written by Jingfu; He Siniang stood on one side, her younger brother He Qi on the other, a pit between them; as they filled in the earth, He Qi shovelled a spade and looked once at his sister.

The first event in the monastery that year was the marriage of Qiniang of the Mi family. The Mis were old believers of the monastery, of the line of Mi Jifen; Mi Jifen's eldest son had been a general in the Shence Army and was dead some years; the grandson was in the Shence Army too, forty-odd, risen to yaya, an officer of the guard; Qiniang, Seventh Daughter, was this grandson's youngest daughter, seventeen. Siyuan was her great-uncle; from a child she had run about the monastery after Siyuan; at four or five, when Siyuan pushed the corpse cart out of the city, she wanted to go along, and Siyuan wouldn't let her, so she sat on the cart's shafts and would not get off; when she was a little older Siyuan taught her the Syriac letters; she learned for half a year, knew seven or eight, and then would learn no more, saying the letters looked like crawling insects. She sang the Tang hymns well, her voice bright; that one, "In the highest of the heavens, deep reverence and praise," the women of the ward sang after her at the offering once in seven; she raised it, the women followed; wherever she sang, the voices in the hall followed her there. On the days the gruel pot was opened, she stood by the pot passing the bowls, and passed them fast; the old women who came for gruel all knew her and called her the little Mi lady.

She was promised to the Zheng family. The Zhengs were Tang, a distant branch of the Zhengs of Xingyang, so distant that one had to turn several pages of the genealogy to find them; the family had produced two county sheriffs and a registrar; the Zheng Second whom Qiniang was to marry was twenty-two, had twice failed the classics examination, and by virtue of his ancestors' protection held the name of an attendant at the Imperial Ancestral Temple, which he went to a few times a year. The Mi officer valued the Zhengs' lineage, the Zhengs valued the Mis' money; neither family said so; the matchmaker called it a match made in heaven.

After the betrothal, Qiniang came once to find Lingbao. That day when the offering once in seven broke up she stayed in the hall, waited till everyone had gone, went up to Lingbao, and asked him one thing. She said in the main hall of the Zhengs' house stood the spirit tablets of generations of Zheng ancestors; a new bride, on the third day after entering, must bow to her husband's parents and to the spirit tablets, and only when she had bowed was she counted a Zheng. She said she had asked her great-uncle, and her great-uncle said he wouldn't concern himself with this and told her to ask Uncle Lingbao. She asked: if she bowed to those wooden tablets, did it count as turning her back on the Lord?

Lingbao was sixty-three that year, his eyes already a little dim; to look at someone he had to lean his head forward. He thought, and said that long ago his Pa, in Lingzhou, in the Shuofang camp, when Lord Guo's family offered to their ancestors and all the officers of the camp had to go and bow—his Pa went too, knelt, knocked his head, rose, went back to the monastery, and broke the bread as ever. He said: when the Tang bow to their ancestors, they bow to those who bore and raised them; it's reverence for the old folk, a different thing from worshipping a god; when you kneel, know in your heart whose ancestors you're bowing to, say in your heart Lord, have mercy, and that's enough. Qiniang asked again: once married, could she still come to the offering once in seven? Lingbao said: if you can come, come; if the Zhengs won't let you, don't fight them over it; wear your cross; where you are, the Lord is. Qiniang nodded, and asked again: her great-uncle said he wouldn't concern himself with this—was he angry with her? Lingbao said: your great-uncle is sixty and has pushed the corpse cart half his life; what he won't concern himself with is what he can't bear to part with.

Qiniang went out of her family's gate on the sixteenth of the third month. The night before, the Mis invited Lingbao, Siyuan and Jingfu to the house; by the monastery's old custom, before a bride left her home a priest blessed her in the house. The Mis had a great house in Buzheng Ward, three courtyards deep, horses tethered in the front court, the officer's bow hanging under the colonnade. Qiniang knelt in the hall in the blue wedding dress she would wear tomorrow, her face not yet made up; Lingbao laid his hand on her head and recited in Syriac, and when he had finished recited it again in Tang; Siyuan stood by, leaning on a jujube-wood stick; Qiniang, kneeling, looked up at her great-uncle; Siyuan turned his eyes elsewhere.

When the blessing was done, Qiniang's mother drew Lingbao into a side room and opened a dressing case to show him. At the bottom of the case lay a layer of red silk, and under the silk was a wooden cross, of jujube wood, two inches or so long, its four ends whittled round, strung on a faded red cord. Her mother said this was Qiniang's great-grandfather's: when Lord Mi was in the Shence camp, before those fourteen brothers went to Huaixi, the Great Worthy Jingjing gave each man a wooden cross, and Lord Mi kept one himself and wore it all his life; when he died it still hung at his neck; afterwards the family put it away. Qiniang had found it herself and put it at the bottom of her dowry, saying that if the Zhengs wouldn't let her come to the monastery, she'd carry this. Her mother said the child was bold, and she didn't dare stop her; she asked the Great Worthy: put away like this—what if the Zhengs turn it up? Lingbao said: the Zhengs won't turn up the bottom of the dressing case; a bride's dressing case is the bride's own.

The next day was the day itself. The Zhengs came to fetch the bride; the Mis followed the Tang rites—urging the bride to her toilet, blocking the carriage, scattering over the bed curtains—not one left out; at dusk the bride got into the carriage and went to the Zhengs'. The Zhengs lived in Chongyi Ward; the house was smaller than the Mis'; two old locust trees at the gate, their bark all cracked; the gate was old, its lacquer peeling in several places; the Zhengs said it had been built in the Kaiyuan years when an ancestor was a county magistrate. On the Mi side, those who went to escort the bride were the officer's household and a few Mi relatives; Siyuan said his legs were bad and he would not go; Lingbao had not meant to go either, but the officer insisted, saying the Great Worthy had watched Qiniang grow up from a child; if the Great Worthy weren't there when Qiniang left home, Qiniang's heart wouldn't be easy.

The wedding feast was laid in the Zhengs' front hall, the men in one place and the women in another; on the men's side, twenty-odd food tables, half taken by Zheng uncles and brothers, half by the Mi side; Lingbao sat in the lowest seat on the Mi side in a black robe washed old, drinking no wine, a bowl of noodle soup before him, of which he ate a few mouthfuls. When the wine had gone round three times, a Zheng uncle had drunk too much—fifty-odd, who had once managed a granary in the Jingzhao prefecture—his face flushed red; he stood up with his wine cup and went table by table toasting; when he reached the Mi side he toasted one and said a line—the Mis are lucky—and reached the officer and said: the officer's in the Shence Army; in future when the Zhengs have business, we'll be relying on him; and reached the lowest seat, saw Lingbao, and stopped.

He asked who this was. The officer said: this is the Great Worthy Lingbao of the Daqin Monastery in Yining Ward; Qiniang grew up in the monastery from a child. The Zheng uncle held his wine cup, his head cocked, looking at Lingbao, at his eyes, at his nose, at the lines on his face, looked several times, turned, and spoke to the Zheng nephews behind him, his voice neither loud nor low, so that half the front hall heard. He said he'd thought the Mis had invited a hu monk, but it turned out to be this sort of man—call him hu and he isn't hu, call him Tang and he isn't Tang, the eyes a hu's, the nose a Tang man's; a man like this—what do they call him in the West Market? A mongrel. When he had said it he laughed first himself, and the nephews behind him laughed too; one, halfway through laughing, saw the officer's face and took his laugh back.

The officer stood up. He was an officer of the guard in the Shence Army, forty-odd, with several hundred men under him; he stood up, and the squad leader who attended him and stood behind him took a step forward with him—a man of thirty-five or -six, a scar across his face from below his left ear all the way to his chin, his hand on his waist; the wine cups on the food table rocked and one fell over, and the wine ran to the edge of the table and dripped down. No one in the front hall was laughing now. Lingbao put out his hand and pressed the officer's wrist. His hand was an old man's hand; pressed on, it had no strength in it; the officer looked down at that hand, and then at Lingbao. Lingbao said: Officer, sit down; today is Qiniang's good day. He himself stood up, clasped his hands toward the Zheng uncle, bowed, and said he had eaten his fill; there was evening office at the monastery; he would take his leave first. He walked out of the front hall, across the courtyard; a Zheng servant opened the gate for him; the two old locust trees at the gate were black in the dusk; he walked out between the two locust trees into the ward street; the ward gate was about to close, and the drum had already beaten several rounds.

Chongyi Ward was no short way from Yining Ward; one had to cross most of the city; he walked slowly, and by the time he reached the foot of the Imperial City walls the drum had finished, the ward gates were shut, and a Jinwu guardsman came riding along the street and asked where he was from, walking the street at this hour. He said he was a monk of the Daqin Monastery in Yining Ward, coming back from a wedding feast at the Zhengs' in Chongyi Ward, and had missed the hour. The guardsman held up his lantern to his face, shone it, and said: a hu monk, eh? you hu monks, instead of chanting your scriptures in your monastery at night, running out to drink at weddings; told him to go, then called him back and asked whether he could speak Tang. Lingbao said he could. The guardsman said: then say a couple of lines for me. Lingbao said he was born in Chang'an, grew up in Chang'an, sixty-three years, and had never gone outside the Guannei circuit. The guardsman laughed and said: your Tang's good enough; go on.

He reached Yining Ward; the ward gate was shut; he knocked, and the ward watchman, who knew him, opened it, grumbling and cursing. He went in at the monastery gate; the lamp at the gate was still burning, lit by Jingfu at dusk, its flame wavering in the wind. He did not go back to his room, but went to the scripture room, lit a lamp, took from the scripture cabinet the register of baptisms, and turned to that page of the month of Shebat in the second year of Dali, the day of his own baptism; that line was in his brother Jingjing's hand, and read son of Yazdbozid and Martha; sponsor in his father's stead, Adam; his baptismal name was Adam too; two Adams written in one line; his brother's hand had shaken as he wrote it, and one stroke went crooked. He looked at that line, then went to turn Samaan's accounts, turned to the page of the winter of the first year of Xingyuan when Pa died, and looked at Pa's name, Yazdbozid, of Balkh. He closed the account book, put it back, blew out the lamp, and sat in the scripture room till the clapper.

On the third day after she entered, Qiniang bowed to her husband's parents and to the spirit tablets in the Zhengs' hall. The Mis said afterwards that she bowed very properly, knelt, knocked her head three times, rose, without the least mistake, and the Zheng old lady was well satisfied. After that she never came to the offering once in seven. For the first two months the women of the ward still asked why the little Mi lady didn't come any more; after that they stopped asking; that hymn "In the highest of the heavens, deep reverence and praise" was now raised by Old Mother Kang; Old Mother Kang's voice was hoarse, and she pitched it low, and the women of the hall following her sang low too.

He Siniang came to find Jingfu that summer. After Old He died, the He family's silk shop passed to He Qi; He Qi was a little over thirty, shrewd, able to bear hardship; half the year he was on the road—to Liangzhou, to Lingzhou, into the Uighurs' country to buy goods—and left the shop in the keeping of two young Sogdians. He Siniang still lived at her parents' house, in the two rooms behind the shop, keeping the accounts for her brother. He Qi's wife's family and several He uncles had been urging her all year to marry again, saying she had kept widowhood ten years, was nearly forty, and if she didn't marry she'd have no one to look after her when old; they said there was a Tang silk merchant at the south end of the West Market surnamed Wang, whose wife had died, with three children at home who needed someone to raise them; he didn't mind her being hu, didn't mind her having been married; it was a good family. He Siniang would not. The uncles asked why not; she said for no reason; the uncles said: you're keeping faith with that man who died at sea, and not even his corpse came back. He Siniang said it wasn't him she was keeping faith with.

She came to find Jingfu after an offering once in seven had broken up, in the small room in the back courtyard, where Jingfu was reckoning the gruel shed's rice for next month. She sat down and came straight to the point: she had come to ask the leader a favour—to ask the leader to act once as go-between for her. Jingfu asked: between you and whom? She said: Uncle Lingbao.

The counting rods in Jingfu's hand fell to the floor. He bent to pick them up, and took two tries to pick them up, and raised his head to look at her; she sat there with both hands resting on her knees, her face level, just as when she stood on the women's side singing the hymns. She said she had been thinking about it for a year. She said that the year Guangzheng left, Uncle Lingbao sat by the table entering that sum for her, and when he had entered it read it to her and asked whether it was written right, and she had thought then: this man, keeping accounts, keeps account of people. She said that all these years she had come to every offering once in seven, watched him break the bread, his hands shaking more each year; watched him cough, cough at night so that half the monastery heard; the monastery's people were all men, all monks; at night who brought him a bowl of water, who pounded his back for him? She said she knew the monastery's rule; the old people of the ward had all told of Shiyiniang: the Great Worthy Yisi married Shiyiniang in the monastery long ago; those in holy orders may marry; bishops and those who have taken vows don't; Uncle Lingbao was a priest and had never taken vows. She said whether the leader agreed or not was not her concern; she asked only that the leader go and ask him; if he wouldn't, she'd go home, and come to the offering once in seven as before, and stand on that side as before.

Jingfu went to ask. He went at night; Lingbao was checking accounts in the scripture room; he went in, sat down, and told He Siniang's words over once, stammering, and when he had told it his face was red to the ears. Lingbao had kept his head down over the accounts all along; when he had finished, he laid down the brush and asked: she came and said it herself? Jingfu said she came and said it herself. Lingbao said he was sixty-three. Jingfu said she knew. Lingbao said he coughed; every winter he coughed; one winter or other he might cough himself to death. Jingfu said she knew that too; that was exactly what she meant. Lingbao said he was on the monk register; a monk on the register may not marry under the Tang code; if he were to marry he'd have to leave the register, and once off the register he'd be a registered household of the ward, must set up a separate household, pay the Two Taxes, answer miscellaneous corvée—past sixty some is excused, not all; and he couldn't live in the monastery either; living in the monastery doesn't count as a household; the inspectorate would come to check. He said he had lived in this monastery sixty-three years, born here, grown up here; his Pa died here, his Ma died here; out of this gate, where would he go?

Jingfu said: Uncle Lingbao, these things you say—is it that you won't, or that you're afraid? Lingbao closed the account book, stood up, went to the scripture cabinet, opened it, and drew a cloth packet out of the bottom shelf; he opened it; inside was a wooden cross, of jujube wood, very roughly whittled—he had whittled it himself as a child, spoiled it; Pa had not thrown it away but kept it; after Pa died, he turned it up in Pa's cupboard and kept it in turn. He held that wooden cross in his hand and rubbed it a few times, wrapped it again, put it back, closed the cabinet door, and told Jingfu: go and tell her he'll think another seven days.

Those seven days Lingbao still came before light to stand at morning devotion, still checked accounts, still broke the bread; the monastery's people could see no difference in him; only Qushen saw it; Qushen those days read out the accounts for him, and halfway through looked up at him: his eyes were on the account book and he was not listening; Qushen stopped, and he did not notice. On the third night he went alone outside the Kaiyuan Gate, to the burial ground, where Pa's and Ma's graves lay side by side and Shiyiniang's grave on Pa's other side, grass growing on the three mounds; he stood before Ma's grave, and stood till the ward gate was about to close before he came back. On the fifth day he went to Joseph and asked him in Syriac: at the Patriarchate, in Merv—a priest past sixty marrying—was there such a thing? Joseph thought, and said there was: outside the city of Merv there was an old priest, seventy, whose wife died, and he married a widow; the elders in the city said he'd gone foolish with age, the believers in the countryside said he was blessed; after he married he lived nine more years, and when he died that widow closed his eyes. Lingbao heard it and said neither good nor not good; on the sixth day he checked accounts as ever; early on the seventh, when morning devotion broke up, he changed into a clean robe and went himself to the two rooms behind the He family's shop. He Qi was not there; he had gone to Liangzhou. Lingbao sat in the room half a watch, and when he came out He Siniang saw him to the door, and the two of them stood at the door and spoke a few words; the young Sogdian in the shop heard from behind the curtain; what the Great Worthy said was: your name—he'd entered it once already, in the accounts; this time he'd enter it again.

The He uncles knew the next day. The eldest He uncle had kept a shop of incense and drugs in the West Market forty years; he was seventy; he came on his stick to the rooms behind the shop, sat down, and asked his niece whether it was true. He Siniang said it was true. The uncle said: that Wang, the Tang man, has a shop, has three children, is forty-five; you won't marry him, and you'll marry a sixty-three-year-old monk whose eyes are dim and whose coughing half the West Market knows of—how many years has he got left? He Siniang said: however many years he lives, she'll keep him company that many. The uncle said: he's a hu monk; marry a hu monk and people will say no one wanted the He daughter. He Siniang said: he's a priest; the monastery's rule allows him to marry; once he's off the register he's a registered householder, and the Tang code allows him to marry too. The uncle said: the rule allows it, the Tang code allows it—does your Pa under the ground allow it? He Siniang said: when Pa died he sat facing east, and sitting, his head drooped; she was holding him up; whether Pa allows it, she knows. The uncle sat there for half a meal's time, stood up on his stick, and said he'd have nothing more to do with it; when your brother comes back, you tell your brother yourself; going out, he tripped on the threshold, and the young man of the shop caught him.

Lingbao left the register in the seventh month of that year. He went himself to the Commissioners of Merit's inspectorate; the officer had changed—thirty-odd—who heard him out, turned up the roll, found his name, looked at the age, looked up at him, and said: the Great Worthy is sixty-three, returning to lay life to marry—this is the first time he's seen it in the inspectorate. Lingbao said: yes. The officer asked further: if you return to lay life, a place in the monastery's quota comes free; whom will the monastery fill it with? Lingbao said the monastery reported a young man named Qushen, who left the monastery in the first year of Changqing, twenty that year, twenty-nine this, copying books for people in the West Market, never married, standing at the back at the offering once in seven. The officer ticked off Lingbao's name on the roll, added the two characters Qushen beside it, and said: next time we come to count, still twenty on the register—the empty one, is it still empty? Lingbao said it was still empty. The officer looked at him once and said: your monastery has patience, I'll say that.

Qushen came back the next day. He was Tang; what his surname was, the monastery's people hardly remembered; Qushen was the name Jingjing had given him long ago, taken from the Laozi—qu shen, qu she, qu tai, put away excess, put away extravagance, put away extremes; Jingjing said his temper was hasty, and told him to remember these three phrases; he had remembered them a dozen-odd years, and his temper was still hasty. The day he came back he carried a bundle of books on his back, saved from these years of copying books in the West Market—scriptures, histories, a copy of the Laozi thumbed till its corners curled; he put the books on his place in the west wing—still the same one he had had—sat on it, felt the mat, and said: the mat's been changed.

When Lingbao went to the Chang'an county office to set up a household, He Siniang went with him. The clerk in charge of household registers was the very one who years ago had refused Jingfu's plea to excuse the Kangs from labour, a little older now; he knew Lingbao, and asked: setting up a household—the head of household's name? Lingbao said: Lingbao. The clerk said: and the surname? Lingbao stood there and could not answer. He had had no surname all his life; none of the monastery's people had surnames; his Pa was called Yisi—it was the Tang who called him that; Pa's own name was Yazdbozid, his grandfather's Milis; people over in Balkh went not by surname but by whose son one was; his Ma's surname was Pei, but a son can't take his mother's surname. The clerk waited with his brush, and He Siniang stood behind him, waiting too. Lingbao thought, and said: surname Yi; his Pa was called Yisi; the Tang all called him Great Worthy Yi; he'd take the surname Yi. The clerk's brush did not come down; he said: hu entering the registers have always taken their country as surname: from Kang, surname Kang; from An, surname An; from Mi, surname Mi; what country is Yi? Where was your Pa from? Lingbao said: Balkh; the clerk asked where Balkh was; Lingbao said Tokharistan, west of the Pamirs. The clerk said: Tokharians entering the registers all take the surname Luo; you should be Luo. Lingbao said he hadn't come from Tokharistan; he was born in Chang'an, grew up in Chang'an, had never spent a day in Tokharistan; he was Yisi's son; the Tang had called his Pa Great Worthy Yi all his life; his surname was Yi. The clerk looked at him once, shook his head, and wrote in the register: Yi Lingbao, aged sixty-three, Yining Ward, Chang'an county; wife, née He, aged thirty-nine; when he had written it he asked the father's name; Lingbao said: father, Yisi; the clerk added a line of small characters under the father's name—father Yisi, of Balkh in Tokharistan—and asked again: native place? Lingbao said: Chang'an; the clerk looked up at his face, asked nothing more, and wrote the two characters Chang'an.

The wedding was held in the eighth month in the monastery, in the hall itself; not many people: the monastery's people, the ward's old believers; a few He women came; He Qi came back from Liangzhou and stood at the back, his face showing no smile and no lack of one. The Mi officer came; Qiniang did not; the officer said the Zheng old lady was ill, and the bride was attending at her bedside and could not get away; the officer brought two bolts of silk, which he said Qiniang had told him to bring.

The monastery held the rite by the rule of Shiyiniang's year; that year Elder Marya presided, crowned the bridegroom and bride, recited the scripture of blessing, and broke the bread. This time, the only priest in Chang'an who could preside was Lingbao—the bridegroom himself. Joseph could chant the Syriac scripture; he chanted it, and when he came to the place of blessing he stopped and looked at Lingbao, and everyone in the hall looked at Lingbao. Lingbao was beside He Siniang, the two of them kneeling; Jingfu stood to one side holding two crowns, which the He women had woven of willow twigs and wild chrysanthemums. Lingbao, kneeling there, recited the blessing himself in Syriac—the passage he had recited dozens of times for other people's bridegrooms and brides; this time he recited it for himself, very slowly, and at the line "may the Lord give you one heart" his voice stopped a moment, then went on to the end. Jingfu set the crowns on their heads, He Siniang's first and then Lingbao's; Lingbao's hair was more than half white, and when the crown went on, the willow leaves hung down against his ears. Then the bread: Lingbao stood up and went to the table; the bread lay in the glass bowl on the table; he broke it, his hands shaking, and took two tries to break it; he gave He Siniang a piece and himself a piece, and then those in the hall, one by one; when he reached Old Mother Kang, she took it, and this time she looked up at him.

Joseph told Elijah afterwards that in Merv, if a priest who had had hands laid on him married, the elders of the city would frown; the canons allow it, the custom doesn't; he frowned too; but Chang'an had no elders now; and even if it had, he thought they too would have stood here, frowning, and recited that passage to the end.

Lingbao moved out of the monastery on the third day after the wedding. He and He Siniang rented a little courtyard at the south end of Yining Ward, two rooms, not far from the Kangs'; in the courtyard was a well, and by the well a pomegranate tree; He Siniang said pomegranates were good, pomegranates have many seeds, and when she had said it she laughed first herself and said: at her age, what talk of many seeds. Lingbao did not take much out with him: a few robes, a quilt, a little book he carried to keep accounts in; the account books and the key of the scripture cabinet he kept as before; he said he had left the register, but the accounts were still his, the scripture cabinet was still his; give the key to Jingfu and Jingfu wouldn't remember which account book was on which shelf. Jingfu said: Uncle Lingbao, will you still come every day from now on? Lingbao said he'd come every day, come before light; at the offering once in seven he'd still break the bread; what he'd left was the register, not this gate.

The next day, while the sky was still dark, Jingfu went to stand in the middle of the courtyard, and behind him people came one after another, some out of the west wing, some in at the monastery gate. When the sky greyed he looked back and counted, and when he counted to the gate he saw Lingbao come in from outside the monastery gate, bringing the chill of the ward street on him, stamp his feet outside the threshold, rub his hands, step in at the gate, and walk to the foot of the jujube tree, to stand in the place where he had stood for decades. When Jingfu had finished counting, it was thirty—one more than yesterday. He Siniang stood on the women's side, beside Old Mother Kang, holding a little lamp she had lit at home to light the road along the ward street.
