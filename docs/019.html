Volume Four　Xiangji Temple

Chapter 19　Crickets

It was that night that the army surgeon drew Yisi's arrows.

There were too many wounded. There were only a few surgeons, going camp by camp, saving first those about to die, then those who would not. Yisi would not die. He had sat by the canal until the second watch, been half dragged, half carried back to camp by Old Duan, and sat in the tent more than an hour more before the surgeon came.

The surgeon was a thin old man of fifty-odd, his hands all blood, dried and not dried, layer on layer. He came into the tent, looked first at Yisi's face, looked once, and said nothing. Then he went round behind him and looked at the arrow.

"Lie down. On your front."

Yisi lay down.

Old Duan stood beside him holding up the lamp. The lamp was a broken earthenware bowl with oil poured in it and a strip of cloth soaking in the oil; the flame jumped and jumped, and set the shadows in the tent lurching this way and that.

"Hold it steady," said the surgeon.

Old Duan held the lamp steady.

The arrow in his back had a barbed head.

The surgeon used a small knife to cut open the flesh on either side of the wound, cutting very slowly, bit by bit, laying bare the flesh round the barbs. Yisi lay on the felt with a length of stick between his teeth. Old Duan had found the stick; it had been the stock of a horsewhip, and biting on it there was a taste of leather and horse sweat.

He made no sound.

Old Duan stood beside him holding the lamp and watching the surgeon's knife; after a while he turned his face away. After a while he turned it back.

"Old Duan," said Yisi indistinctly round the stick. "The lamp."

Old Duan looked down; the lamp was tilting, the oil about to spill. He hurriedly set it straight.

When the arrowhead came out, a gush of blood came with it. The surgeon threw the arrowhead on the ground, ting. He pressed a cloth on the wound, pressed it a while, and sprinkled a handful of powder on it, yellow, with a smell of burning.

The head in his side was shallower, and came out quickly.

Last was the face.

The surgeon held the lamp close and looked at Yisi's forehead.

The piece over the brow bone had no skin left; red flesh was showing, with sand and bits of grass worked into it. Half his left eyebrow was gone, and the half that was left was stuck into a tuft with blood.

"Where's the skin?" asked the surgeon.

Yisi said: "Tore it off."

"Tore it off?"

"It was in front of my eye."

The surgeon looked at him a while and asked nothing more. He rinsed the wound with clean water, picked out the sand grain by grain, and when he had done that took a needle and a roll of thread from inside his robe.

"Bear it," said the surgeon. "The thread's not good. It won't come out pretty."

"Never mind."

So the surgeon sewed.

As the needle went through skin and flesh there was a very strange sound, very faint, like someone tearing a sheet of paper a long way off. Yisi shut his eyes and listened to that sound, one stitch, and another. He counted. Seven stitches in all.

When it was done the surgeon straightened his back, looked, and shook his head.

"Crooked," he said.

"Never mind," said Yisi.

The surgeon packed up his things and stood to go. At the tent entrance he looked back and said: "Lie still. No riding for a month."

Yisi said: "All right."

The surgeon gave him a look, and what the look meant was: I don't believe you. Then he lifted the curtain and went on to the next tent.

Old Duan set the lamp on the ground and sat down.

He looked at Yisi, looked at him so long that Yisi felt it, and opened his eyes to look back.

"That face of yours," said Old Duan. "It won't be pretty after this."

"It never was."

Old Duan thought, and supposed that was true, and left it. He fished half a flatbread from his coat and broke off a piece for Yisi. Yisi, lying on his side, took it and put it in his mouth. The bread slowly softened in his mouth; he had eaten nothing all day, and halfway through it he found he was hungry, and chewed.

"Your horse," said Yisi. "I've still got it."

"Tied up outside," said Old Duan. "Yours—the groom pulled that arrow out of its haunch. It won't die. It won't run fast any more."

Yisi said "Mm."

"Those two men," said Yisi. "I left them on the canal bank."

"I know," said Old Duan. "I saw when I went to drag you back. Lying side by side, faces wiped."

"They must be buried tomorrow."

"Tomorrow we'll see," said Old Duan. "Tomorrow you won't be getting up."

Yisi did not argue. He lay on his side looking at the flame in the broken earthenware bowl on the ground. It jumped and jumped, very low; the oil was nearly burned away.

After a while Old Duan saw his lips moving. Moving very slightly, with no sound, as if reciting something.

Old Duan waited until he had finished and asked: "What was that? That scripture of yours?"

"Names," said Yisi.

"Whose names?"

"Shi Six. Wei Three," said Yisi. "And Du De."

Old Duan asked no more. He pushed the lamp towards Yisi, a little nearer.

"That Wei Three," said Old Duan. "His ma's twenty li south of the city."

"I know."

"Do you know which village?"

Yisi was silent a while. "No."

Old Duan said nothing either. The two of them, one sitting, one lying, watched that bit of flame. It jumped twice more, and went out. Old Duan felt in the dark, added a little oil to the bowl, and struck a light with his fire-steel.

Someone went past outside, walking fast, talking as he went. He was saying General Pugu had just been to the Marshal's tent, saying the rebels were beaten and would surely flee tonight, and he wanted to take two hundred horse after them and bring back An Shouzhong and Li Guiren. The Marshal had said the men had fought all day and were tired; rest one night and see tomorrow.

The footsteps faded.

Old Duan listened and gave a snort. "That Pugu—the fighting's over and he's still not tired."

He said too that Lord Guo had not gone to bed either. Before dark he'd sent word down: tonight the grain carts weren't to be unhitched, General Hun's battalion would sleep in their clothes with their swords to hand. They said a great body of Li Guiren's picked horse had scattered in the daytime, and where they'd got to no one had made out.

Yisi said nothing.

That night the grand army camped right on the battlefield north of Xiangji Temple. There was no time to put up a palisade; the baggage carts were set end to end in a ring, which served as a wagon laager. The tents of the wounded were pitched in the southwest corner, up against the laager, with only three or four grain carts between.

The camp was very quiet, and very cold.

After a whole day of fighting, the living were all tired, and fell asleep as soon as they lay down. In the wounded tents someone was groaning, groaned a while, went quiet, and after a while groaned again. The men on watch went past and came back, their footsteps dragging.

Yisi could not sleep.

He could only lie on his side. With the wound in his back he could not lie on it; with the wound in his side he could not lie on his front for long. He lay on his side, his face to the tent curtain. The curtain had not been let down close, and there was a gap, and beyond the gap it was dark, and in the dark a few stars.

He listened to what was outside.

Outside, an insect was chirping.

It was on the grain cart outside the tent; the cart was loaded with fodder hauled all the way from Fengxiang, and the insect was probably hiding in a bale. One chirp, a long pause, another chirp. The sound was very thin, very dry, like someone plucking a very fine string with a fingernail.

It was the end of the ninth month, and the nights were thoroughly cold. The insects in the fields had long stopped singing; the few left had all hidden in warm places—in fodder bales, in dung heaps, by the cooking pits. Yisi had been listening ever since he lay down, and there were only a few places in the whole camp with any sound: one on this grain cart outside his tent; outside the laager in the southwest corner, where a great heap of horse dung cleared out in the daytime was piled, two or three; further west, over by the fodder stacks, a few more.

Old Duan was not asleep yet, and heard it too.

"Crickets," he said. "When the cricket sings, the lazy wife takes fright. When my ma was alive, she'd hear one of those and scold my elder sister, saying the winter clothes weren't on the loom yet."

When he had said it he lay down, his back to Yisi.

Yisi listened to the cricket on the cart. One chirp, a long pause, another chirp. He listened and listened, and his eyelids slowly grew heavy.

Then he opened his eyes.

The ones over by the dung heap had stopped.

Yisi held his breath and listened. The one he heard most clearly was the one that sang most often among those by the dung heap. Lying there unable to sleep, he had been counting by it all along.

It had stopped.

He waited a while. When the watch went by, the crickets stopped too, and when the men were far off, they sang again. He waited for the footsteps of the watch, and did not hear them.

After a while, over by the fodder stacks, one stopped too.

Still he did not move. He thought: perhaps it's the cold; perhaps I'm half asleep.

After a while more, by the fodder stacks, another one broke off.

Yisi propped himself on his arm and sat up. The wound in his back pulled, and he clenched his teeth.

He reached out and shook Old Duan.

Old Duan slept with one eye open. Yisi's hand had barely touched him when he was sitting up, his hand already on his sword hilt.

"The southwest corner," said Yisi. "The crickets by the dung heap have stopped. By the fodder stacks, two have broken off. I haven't heard the watch go by."

Old Duan tilted his head and listened a moment. "I can't hear anything."

"They broke off one by one," said Yisi. "From the outside in."

Old Duan looked at him a while. He asked no more, snatched up his sword, lifted the curtain and was gone. He walked fast at any time; this time he was faster still—the curtain was still swaying and he was already out of sight.

Old Duan went to Guo Ziyi.

Afterwards he told Yisi that Guo Ziyi had not been asleep yet, but sitting under the lamp going over the casualties the camps had sent in. When Guo Ziyi had heard him out, he asked only one thing: "Yisi said so?" Old Duan said yes.

First Guo Ziyi sent down an order: no camp was to light a fire; all were to sit in their armour and make no sound.

Then he sent for Shi Jingzhong and had him send men out, feeling their way southwest, to find out for certain and come back and report. And he sent a guard to the Marshal's tent to tell the Prince of Guangping that there might be rebels southwest of the camp, and men had been sent to scout.

Two scouts went out. In less time than it takes to eat a meal, one came back.

The one who came back said that outside the laager, behind the dung heap, a few men were lying flat, looking into the laager. Further southwest, two or three li, at the mouth of a gully by the Jue, there were horses and men; the men had dismounted and left the horses in the gully with someone watching them, and how many could not be seen clearly—a few hundred, at least—as if waiting for those few men to come back. The other scout was still there watching.

The guard sent to the Marshal's tent came back too. He brought His Highness's words: holding the camp comes first; if the rebels come, fight them, but no one is to cross the Jue in pursuit.

Only then did Guo Ziyi send men to Pugu Huai'en's camp and to Hun Shizhi's.

The man sent to Pugu's camp found him gone. Pugu Huai'en had gone back to the Marshal's tent and was still asking for those two hundred horse. The man fetched him back from the Marshal's tent.

Pugu Huai'en took the Shuofang horse, their hooves wrapped in cloth, out of the west of the camp, round south in a great circle, round to the outside of that gully mouth by the Jue. Hun Shizhi took his foot soldiers, bent double, one behind another, and laid them up inside the laager at the southwest corner.

Yisi sat in the tent and listened.

He heard many horses moving at the west of the camp, moving very softly, their hooves wrapped, coming down on the ground—puff, puff, puff. He heard many men moving over by the laager, iron plates touching iron plates, pressed down by hands so that only the least sound leaked out.

Then all these sounds stopped.

Outside the camp there was no sound at all any more. The cricket on the grain cart had stopped too.

Yisi sat there, his hands pressed on his knees.

He thought of that night outside Changshan. He had lain in the graveyard among the tumbled graves and listened like this, listened to the rebel watch changing, to their footsteps going from left to right and coming back from right to left. That night he had been alone, and behind him on the bank of the Mianman lay Old Iron and the others.

Tonight there were men with him. Outside the tent, in the dark, thousands of men just like him were sitting, squatting, gripping their swords, listening.

He heard his own heart beating. Beating very slowly, very heavily, one stroke after another.

He wanted to recite a passage of scripture, opened his mouth, and shut it again. He was afraid of saying it aloud.

So he said it in his heart. What he said was the passage his father had taught him, the one before meals, the shortest of all. When he had finished he said it again from the beginning.

The raiders came from the southwest.

Afterwards, it was got out of the prisoners that they were a body of Li Guiren's horse, several hundred strong. When the main line broke in the daytime they had meant to fall back north into the city, but the Uighurs had cut off their retreat, so they had fled south and crept into the gullies by the Jue, and hidden there all day until dark. They had thought: the Tang army has fought all day, tonight they'll sleep like the dead. Creep into the camp, seize a few carts of grain, lead off a herd of horses, set a fire, and in the confusion slip through the laager and round back to the city.

First they had sent a few men in to spy out the way. Those men crept past the dung heap and up to the fodder stacks, and Hun Shizhi's men pinned two of them; the rest turned and ran.

The men who ran back brought word, and the men at the gully mouth took it that only a few of the Tang watch were awake, and all crept up together.

When they had crept up to the laager, behind the laager, men stood up.

In the tent, the first thing Yisi heard was a roar. That was Hun Shizhi's voice, coarse and hoarse, like a cracked gong. Then the sound of swords, the sound of lances, the sound of men crashing into carts.

The raiders ran back.

They ran back to the gully mouth, and the horses in the gully were gone. Pugu Huai'en's horse had got there a step ahead of them, cut up the party minding the horses and driven all the horses off.

The cloth was torn from the horses' hooves. Several hundred horses broke into a run together, and the sound seemed to heave up all at once from under the ground. Someone was shouting, shouting in Tiele, and Yisi could tell it was Pugu Huai'en's voice, sharp and cold.

The two sides closed.

That fight was not long.

Yisi thought afterwards that it was probably about the time it took Old Duan to dissolve one piece of flatbread. The shouting and killing rose, fell, fell and rose again a while, and then broke up. It broke up into scattered sounds—horses crying, men groaning, someone calling another man's name, calling it several times, with no one answering.

After that there was nothing.

When Old Duan came back it was nearly dawn. His sword had not left its scabbard; he had not gone to fight. He said he had stood at the door of Guo Ziyi's tent, stood all night.

"Some got away," said Old Duan. "Off towards the southern hills. Pugu wanted to go after them, Lord Guo wouldn't let him, said His Highness had given word: not across the Jue. The rest got caught outside the laager."

Yisi nodded.

Old Duan sat down beside him, laid the sword on his knees, and looked at the gap in the tent curtain. The sky beyond the gap turned from black to grey.

"Lord Guo told me to tell you something," said Old Duan.

"What?"

"He said: lie still."

Yisi said: "I am lying still."

And he was. He had lain down on his side again, his face to the curtain.

At daybreak, news came.

Scouts came back from under the walls and said the rebels had abandoned Chang'an in the night and fled east. An Shouzhong, Li Guiren, and all the false officials in the city, with what men and horses they could take, had gone out of the city gates in the night, towards Tong Pass. Only the common people were left in the city.

The camp erupted. Some wept, some laughed, some threw their helmets into the air, and they came down on their own heads, and they clutched their heads and went on laughing.

The raid on the camp went round too. Those who told it said General Pugu had been sure the rebels would come, General Hun's foot had lain up in the laager, the two sides had closed on them and killed nearly all. Some said Lord Guo had foreseen it, and had the men sleep in their clothes before dark.

Old Duan went round the camp, came back, sat down by Yisi and said: "They're saying Lord Guo foresaw it."

"Lord Guo did foresee it," said Yisi.

Old Duan said "Mm," and said nothing more. After a while he stood up.

"Those two," he said. "They're still on the canal bank. I've had men from General Hun's camp watching them."

Yisi said: "Good."

Old Duan said he would go and see whether the cookhouse had any hot broth, lifted the curtain and went out.

Only Yisi was left in the tent.

The sun climbed, and shone on the boards of the grain cart outside the tent. The boards warmed, and in the fodder bale that cricket chirped again.

A long pause.

It chirped again.
