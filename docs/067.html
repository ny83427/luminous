Chapter 67　Three Generations

Early in the spring of the second year of Kaicheng, twenty-five stood at the morning.

Every day, when the sky greyed, Jingfu looked back and counted; he had been counting nineteen years. As he counted he had a map in his mind of who stood where, and he knew it with his eyes shut. Under the jujube tree was Lingbao. By the well curb there had been Siyuan; after Siyuan died the place stood empty a winter, and in spring the younger Shi brother went to stand there; he pushed Siyuan's cart, so he stood in Siyuan's place. By the gallery pillar was An Yan, sixty-six, and the foot that had lost half a toe to the cold made him limp in winter. Joseph and Elijah stood at the hall door, both past fifty, their beards half white. Under the eaves of the west wing stood Qushen, the elder Shi brother and Salang, and the few left of the ten who had left the monastery—some had come back onto the register and some had not; all were thirty or forty now, and when the morning broke up each went to his own work. The rest, in the two rows of the gallery and on the women's side, were all the old believers of the ward. The youngest was He Siniang, forty-six; the oldest was Mother Kang, seventy-three. Kang Da had died the year before last; she came alone, leaning on a bamboo stick, and stood in the last row by the door, in the place where Kang Da used to stand.

The offering once in seven was the same. The years when the hall was full, Jingfu remembered: in the Master's time, the seven households back from the south of the city, people of the ward, of the West Market, of the Shence Army—the hall couldn't hold them, and some stood on the steps outside the door. Now thirty-odd sat in the hall, white heads in the front rows and white heads in the back rows too. Among those drawing gruel at the shed there were a few who had come often these years; they came and were not baptized, sat in the last row, and when the worship had been sung through, did not take the offering bread but sat and watched, and when they had watched, went back to the gruel shed to wait for the pot to open.

Once, as Jingfu was counting the people in the hall, halfway through Qushen leaned to his ear.

"Director, look at this hall of people. Thirty years from now, how many will be left?"

"Thirty years from now you'll have white hair too."

"What I mean," said Qushen, "is whether there'll be any black hair under the white."

Lingbao's eyes began to fail in the winter of the first year of Kaicheng.

First it was reading the accounts: he couldn't make out Samaan's small dense Syriac letters, and had to raise the ledger under the lamp, raise it to the tip of his nose; later, even at the tip of his nose he couldn't make them out, and had He Siniang read them; He Siniang didn't know Syriac letters, so he called Qushen to read. Later still it was seeing people. At the morning he stood under the jujube tree and looked at Jingfu in the middle of the courtyard and could see a dark shape but not make out a face. He said it was as if a mist had risen in his eyes, thicker every year—thick on the left, a little thinner on the right.

Shopkeeper Wang's grandson from the West Market came to look. "It's a cataract, an old man's disease. Tang doctors have no remedy; you can only take medicine, and even taking it doesn't necessarily help," he said. "Monks come from India know a kind of golden lancet: a needle goes in beside the dark of the eye and couches that film down. Couch it well and the eye is bright again; couch it badly and the eye is blind. There's one at the Daxingshan Monastery; I hear he's couched some people well, and blinded some."

He Siniang wanted him to go.

"All your life you've never gone to a Buddhist monastery. Go once this time. You needn't bow to the Buddha, but the eyes have to be treated."

"I can go to a Buddhist monastery," said Lingbao. "It's the needle I'm afraid of."

He sat under the pomegranate tree with his face toward the well. He couldn't see the well; he could see only a patch of brightness.

"When my Pa was young, he went with an old teacher of the law at the monastery who could heal eyes, and treated the eyes of the little princess of the Eastern Palace. In those days people in Chang'an all said the monks of the Daqin Monastery could heal eyes. Pa carried the medicine chest, handed the knife, handed the needle—what he handed was the needle for couching eyes. Pa learned it, watched how the old teacher of the law put the needle in. I was small then; Pa didn't teach me, and I never thought to learn. Later Pa grew old and his hands shook, and that craft was buried with Pa outside the Kaiyuan Gate."

He paused a while.

"A few years ago people from Xichuan said that when Nanzhao broke into Chengdu they carried off a monk of Daqin in the city who healed eyes, carried him off south, dead or alive no one knows. When I heard it I thought about it for several nights. Who that man was, what year he came from the Patriarchate, how many years he lived in Chengdu, I know nothing at all," he said. "Now my eyes need treating, and in Chang'an those who can handle that needle are only the Indian monks in the Buddhist monasteries. Siniang, if I'm pricked blind, I can't read the accounts, can't break the bread, and Chang'an won't even have a blind priest."

He did not go. That winter he had Qushen read him the accounts every day, and when they were read, kept them in his head, kept them more clearly than before: which entry was which year's, how many names were on which page; if Qushen read a word wrong he said, "Wrong. Turn back, read it again."

At one offering once in seven in the winter of the second year of Kaicheng, Lingbao dropped the bread on the floor.

It was snowing that day, and there were not many in the hall; several old believers had not come. Lingbao stood before the table, the bread in the glass bowl. He finished reciting the words of consecration and reached for the bread. His eyes could not see clearly and his hands shook. He lifted it, broke it in two, and as he broke it his hand slipped, and the bread slid off the rim of the bowl, fell to the floor before the table, and broke into three pieces.

Everyone in the hall saw. Of the old believers in the front rows, some opened their mouths and some bowed their heads. Mother Kang, sitting in the last row with her bamboo stick against her knee, stood up, and sat down again.

Lingbao stood there looking down at the floor. He could not see clearly, only a few white things on the floor. Slowly he bent; his knees were bad and he could not bend far. Jingfu came across to pick them up for him.

"No need. I'll do it."

He held the edge of the table and lowered himself bit by bit to a crouch, and once down, groped on the floor with his hand. He found one piece, picked it up, put it back in the bowl; groped again, found the second piece, put it back in the bowl. The third had rolled under the foot of the table, and he groped several times without finding it. Jingfu crouched, took the piece of bread from under the table's foot, and laid it in his palm. He gripped that piece of bread, held the edge of the table, and stood. When he was steady he put the three pieces together and recited the words of consecration once more, from the beginning. When he had recited them he broke the bread and handed it out piece by piece.

When it broke up that day, Jingfu was clearing up in the hall. Lingbao sat on the steps at the hall door, snow falling on his cap, and did not brush it off. Jingfu went over and sat beside him.

"Fu'er, from now on when I break the bread, stand beside me and hold the bowl steady for me."

"Good."

"Not only hold the bowl. When I recite the words of consecration, recite them along with me, recite in your heart, recite until you know them by heart, without one word wrong."

"Even if I know them by heart it won't count. Hands haven't been laid on me."

"I know it won't count," said Lingbao. "It won't count, and still learn them by heart. When some day a bishop comes, recite them for him, and when he's heard, he'll know how the bread was consecrated in Chang'an, know that in all these years in Chang'an not one offering once in seven broke up empty-handed. If a bishop never comes and I die, Chang'an's offering once in seven will have no bread. Then you recite. You recite and it doesn't count, and everyone knows it doesn't count; but listening, everyone will know how it was consecrated before. Keep it in mind—keep it until one day someone comes and it can count."

Joseph was by the steps that day too, heard it, and came over.

"Brother Lingbao, at Merv, at the Patriarchate, when the country churches have no priest, worship is done just the same and the scriptures recited just the same; when they reach the offering bread, they stop, and everyone kneels, and doesn't receive. It's called the empty-handed worship; there are country people who've done it for decades," he said. "Today you reached out and picked up the bread. Consecrated bread fallen on the floor—pick it up and eat it; that's right; it mustn't be thrown away. That the Director's hand touched it doesn't matter either. What matters is that hereafter the Director doesn't take touching for consecrating. If Chang'an comes to that day, let it do the empty-handed worship. The scriptures can be recited; the bread can't be broken. If the Director recited the words of consecration and everyone took the bread, that would be false—worse than empty hands."

"I didn't tell Fu'er to break the bread," said Lingbao. "I told him to remember."

"Remember—I've nothing against that," said Joseph. "Remembering is good."

From then on, at every offering once in seven, Jingfu stood beside Lingbao and held the glass bowl steady for him. Lingbao recited, and he recited along in his heart.

Liu'er came in the summer of the third year of Kaicheng.

That spring there was a great drought. The wheat of Guanzhong gave no harvest; people from outside the city poured in, and the price of rice in the city rose three times in a month. The gruel shed opened more often than in any year—two pots a day, sometimes three—and the monastery's rice was not enough; Jingfu went to borrow house by house.

First he went to He Qi in the West Market. He Qi had just come back from Liangzhou; he opened his rice bin to show him. "There's not much rice in the shop either. But my sister's husband's monastery—I can't not help." He gave twenty shi. "Call it a loan; I don't want interest. Director, don't only borrow from hu. The hu of the West Market have had a hard year too. Go to the Tang."

Jingfu went to the Buzheng Ward to the Mi aide-de-camp. The aide-de-camp gave ten shi. "The Shence Army's short of grain too. These ten shi are my own salary rice."

It still was not enough. On the third day he went to the Secretariat and waited at the gate of the Imperial City for Lü Shao. He waited till dusk; Lü Shao came out and heard him out.

"I'm a copyist; there's no rice at my house either," said Lü Shao. "But I know the Public Revenue Department has a sum of relief rice every year for the capital's monasteries to give gruel. The Buddhist monasteries go to draw it every year; no hu temple has ever gone. It's there to be given; it's only that no one goes to ask."

He wrote out a petition for Jingfu, very neatly, and told Jingfu to take it to the Public Revenue Department. Jingfu stood in the department's gallery for three days, and on the third day an assistant came down, read the petition, and looked at him.

"The Daqin Monastery? The Daqin Monastery whose stele says the office concerned at once built a monastery in the Yining Ward of the capital?"

"Yes."

"I'll accept the petition. Whether it can be given, I can't say."

Half a month later the department allotted fifteen shi of old rice, with weevils in it. Jingfu had mats spread in the courtyard and picked it over grain by grain; it took three days, and the weevils picked out went to feed the ward's hens.

The cart for the dead was pushed more often than in any year too, the two Shi brothers pushing it, sometimes two trips a day. Outside the Kaiyuan Gate, that year added a row of stakes.

One day in the fourth month the younger Shi brother pushed the cart out of the Jinguang Gate and, under a willow by the moat, saw a woman sitting against the tree, her head sunk on her breast, already stiff, a bundle in her arms, and the bundle moving. He untied it. Inside was a child two or three months old, so thin there was only skin left, its mouth still moving, looking for milk. He tucked the child into his breast, lifted the woman onto the cart, pushed it back into the city, pushed it to the monastery gate, the child crying in his breast, its cry as thin as a kitten's.

That day was Xiaoman, the Lesser Fullness of Grain, and the wheat was not yet fully yellow. He Siniang took the child. She had no milk, and ran to the south end of the ward to find the Mi family's third daughter. The third daughter had not waited for Rijin after all; she had married a Sogdian carter of the West Market and just had her third, and had plenty of milk. She gave it a mouthful, the child latched on, and stopped crying.

Lingbao came at dusk to see. His eyes could not see clearly; he had He Siniang bring the child to him and put his face close, close to the child's face.

"It's a girl. What's she called?"

"When the Shi Second found her, there was nothing on her. No name."

"Where was she found?"

"Outside the Jinguang Gate, by the moat, under a willow."

"Found under a willow—then call her Liu'er, Little Willow," said Lingbao. "Break off a willow branch, and wherever you stick it in, there it lives."

Liu'er's mother was buried outside the Kaiyuan Gate. The younger Shi brother wrote the stake. His writing was poor; he wrote it three times before he got it right: Liu'er's mother, under a willow outside the Jinguang Gate, fourth month of the third year of Kaicheng. The Mi third daughter nursed the child until she was weaned in the autumn. There were no women at the monastery, so He Siniang took her home to the south end of the ward and raised her herself. Mother Kang came over every day to help mind her. She was seventy-four, her eyes dim too, and sat under the pomegranate tree with the child in her arms humming In the highest heavens, deep reverence and praise, out of tune, and the child, listening, fell asleep.

Every time Jingfu went to Lingbao's house and saw that old one and that little one sitting under the pomegranate tree, he thought of himself as a child. He had come to the monastery at five, when his Pa died in Huaixi and Lord Mi gave him to the Master. The day he came to the monastery, he held a ladle himself at the well and drank a ladleful of cold water, and the Master watched beside him and did not stop him; and all his life after, he had never once been ill from cold water. He thought: when he came to the monastery the Master was fifty-five; now he was forty-three, and Liu'er could not walk yet.

Guangzheng's letter came in the autumn of the fourth year of Kaicheng.

The one who brought it was Li Su's youngest son. Li Su had been dead more than twenty years. Of his sons, one carried on watching the stars at the Astronomical Bureau, one was in the Shence Army, one in the Court of Imperial Sacrifices, and all their names had the character jing in them. The one at the Astronomical Bureau was the youngest, past fifty himself, risen to Gentleman of the Spirit Tower. He did not come to the offering once in seven, but every year on Li Su's death day he came to the monastery once, stood a while at the scripture-room door, talked a few words with Lingbao, and said that his Pa in his day had watched the stars on this roof with Elder Yonan.

That autumn when he came, it was not the death day.

"Great Worthy Lingbao, something has come from Guangzhou." He took a small packet from his breast. "My clan cousin in the foreign quarter of Guangzhou sent it by a hu merchant coming north. My cousin says this thing had been kept with the Li family of the foreign quarter several years. An old sailor on a ship from Basra gave it to the Li family. The old sailor said a Tang man had entrusted it to him, and the Tang man said: the Daqin Monastery, Yining Ward, Chang'an; give it to Lingbao. The Li family kept it and could never find a sure person going to Chang'an. Some years ago they found one, and he died of illness on the road at Hongzhou, and the thing was sent back to Guangzhou; only this year did they find another."

He paused. "My cousin also said that ten-odd years ago a Tang man came from Chang'an with a letter from the He family of the West Market and lodged with the Li family more than a month, waiting for a ship. The Tang man was called Guangzheng. Afterward he boarded a ship bound for Basra and left. Since then the Li family has had no word of him."

The thing was an oiled-cloth packet the size of a palm. The oiled cloth had gone stiff, its edges bleached white by seawater, but it was sewn very close, the stitches crooked in several places. Lingbao held it and felt it, and when he felt those crooked stitches, his hand stopped.

"Guangzheng's mother sewed this."

Guangzheng's mother had died more than ten years before, in those two rooms in the Buzheng Ward. Before she died her eyes had gone completely blind. On the thread board at her bedside was still stuck a row of needles that Guangzheng had threaded for her before he left. She used them several years, one by one, used them till she died, and three were left.

In the oiled-cloth packet was a small piece of parchment, rolled and tied with a fine hemp cord. Lingbao could not see it clearly and gave it to Joseph. Joseph untied the cord and spread the parchment on the table and held the lamp to it. On the parchment were Syriac letters, written very small. The hand was Guangzheng's; Joseph knew it. There were only a few lines. Joseph read them first in Syriac, then said them again in Tang:

"Daqin Monastery, Yining Ward, Chang'an. To the Great Worthy Lingbao and the Director Jingfu. Arrived. The patriarch consents. Waiting for a ship. Guangzheng. In the year of the Greeks one thousand one hundred and forty-four."

Everyone in the room was standing.

"One thousand one hundred and forty-four—what year is that?" asked Jingfu.

Joseph reckoned on his fingers, reckoned twice. "The sixth year of Dahe, or the seventh, round about winter."

"That's six or seven years ago."

"Yes."

Lingbao sat at the table, reached out and took the parchment, brought it close to his eyes. He could not make out the letters, only some dark marks on the parchment. He turned it over; there was nothing on the back. He turned it back again and felt those few lines with his finger. The ink was dry; he could not feel the strokes.

"Arrived—arrived at the Patriarchate?"

"The letter only says arrived."

"The patriarch consents—consents to what? To send someone, or to lay hands on Guangzheng?"

"The letter only says consents," said Joseph. "In these ten-odd years the Patriarchate has changed patriarchs several times. When Guangzheng arrived, the consent wasn't necessarily given that same year; he may have waited several years for it."

"Waiting for a ship—waiting for a ship coming east?"

"The letter only says waiting for a ship."

"When the letter was written it was the sixth year of Dahe. And now? Did he get his ship or not, board it or not, arrive or not, sink or not?"

"The letter doesn't say."

Lingbao asked no more. He rolled up the parchment, tied it with that fine hemp cord, put it back in the oiled-cloth packet, and held the packet gripped in his hand.

"I understand. This is a hope."

"Yes," said Jingfu. "It's a hope."

Li Su's youngest son stood to one side looking from one to another, and only when he saw Lingbao put the oiled-cloth packet in his breast did he speak.

"When my Pa was alive he used to say that what star-watchers fear most is a star whose light you see, but that light has travelled decades to reach your eye, and by the time you see it the star may long since be gone." When he had said it he felt it was the wrong thing to say, and added, "But then again, perhaps it's still there."

"Gentleman of the Spirit Tower, what your Pa said is right," said Lingbao. "Both are right."

He had Qushen bring a brush, opened that small book he carried, turned to the line after He Siniang's page—Ninth month of the third year of Changqing: Guangzheng goes to the Patriarchate, to ask for a bishop—with the empty line below it. He could not see, and felt with his finger the place of the empty line.

"Qushen, beside the empty line, against the edge of the paper, write a line in small characters."

"What shall I write?"

"Write: Autumn of the fourth year of Kaicheng: a letter from Guangzheng arrived. Arrived; the patriarch consents; waiting for a ship."

Qushen wrote it.

"That empty line," said Lingbao, "is still empty."

At the first offering once in seven after that, more people came to the hall than usual. The Li family's Gentleman of the Spirit Tower came; He Qi, back from Liangzhou, came; the Mi aide-de-camp came, and Qiniang came too—the first time she had come in at the monastery gate on a day that was not a funeral. The news had spread from the Li family, to the West Market, to the ward: there was a letter from Guangzheng; the Patriarchate had consented; a bishop was coming. The old believers sat in the hall, some wiping their eyes, some asking the person beside them when the bishop would arrive, and the person beside them said they didn't know; the letter said waiting for a ship.

Jingfu stood beside Lingbao. Lingbao recited the words of consecration, very slowly, word by word, and at the end reached for the bread. Jingfu's left hand held the foot of the glass bowl, and the bowl did not move.
