Epilogue　The Steppe

Between the night Jingfu lit the first dish of mutton fat by that river and the last night of this story lie another hundred and sixty years.

A hundred and sixty years, in Chang'an, is the business of several generations. Huang Chao entered the city; Zhu Wen pulled down the palaces, and the timber of Chang'an floated down the River Wei to build new halls in Luoyang. The Tang fell. The Central Plain passed through five dynasties and a dozen-odd emperors, like the figures on a revolving lantern. In the end there was a Song in the south and a Liao in the north; the two fought for decades, tired of it, and swore a covenant at Chanzhou—one to send silk, one to receive it—and that was the end of that. On the foundations of the monastery in the Yining Ward, who knows how many times houses had been built, how many times people had lived. The stele still lay in the earth, under the hemp and the rammed soil, and no one knew. It had another six hundred years and more to lie there.

These things, the people of the steppe did not much care about.

What the people of the steppe cared about was grass, and snow, and horses, and whether this winter the sheep would freeze to death. They kept years, not reign-names; what they kept was which year the snow was deep, which year the horses sickened, which year which tribe fought which, and whose son did not come back. The names of the emperors in the south, by the time they reached here, had long since gone out of shape, and spoken aloud sounded like the cry of some other kind of bird.

That year, in the north of the steppe, along the river Tula, there was a great tribe. Many people, many horses, many sheep; the tents on both banks of the river, joined together, took most of a day to ride past. The histories written later called this tribe the Kerait.

The king of the Kerait was thirty or so that year, just at the age to ride and shoot his best. When winter set in, he took a dozen men into the hills to hunt. The first day they shot a few gazelles; the second day they chased a deer, a stag, its antlers branched in seven tines, running like a gust of wind. The king's blood was up, and he rode alone at the very front, chased it over a ridge, and chased it into a valley.

He did not catch the deer. The snow came.

It was the kind of snow the steppe has. First wind, from the north, scouring along the skin of the ground, so that a man could not open his eyes; then snow, and the snow did not fall either, but flew sideways and struck the face as if someone were flinging sand into it by the handful. In the time it takes to eat a meal, the sky was black and the ground white, and sky and ground ran together into one, so that there was no telling up from down.

The king turned and called his men, and no one answered.

His men had scattered. He shouted himself hoarse a few times, and the moment the sound left his mouth the wind tore it to shreds, so that even he could not hear it. He thought to blow his horn, but the horn had frozen, and would not sound.

He rode on through the snow, not knowing where he was riding. Nor did the horse know. The horses of the steppe know the road home, but in snow like this, even the horse did not know it. After a long time the horse would go no further, and stood there, head down, the white breath from its nostrils freezing as it left them, a thick layer of snow on its back, as if it carried a small grave.

The king got down from the horse and, pulling it by the reins, went on alone.

He thought: I will probably die here. Every winter on the steppe people died like this. The next spring, when the snow melted, they were found in some gully, at the edge of some wood, man and horse frozen together, hard as two stones. He had seen it several times. One of his father's brothers had been brought home like that, the reins still gripped in his hand.

He walked a while more, and his legs were no longer his own.

And then he saw a point of light.

The light was very small.

Small as a star fallen to the ground. In the snow, one moment it could be seen and the next not. When the wind was strong it bent aside, sank low, as if it would go out; when the wind dropped a little it stood up straight again, still that one point, not large, not going out.

Pulling the horse, the king walked toward that point of light.

That stretch of road, when he thought back on it afterwards, always seemed to him to have taken a whole night. In truth it was probably no longer than a meal. When he reached it, he saw clearly: it was a wooden post, chest-high, planted in the snow. On top of the post sat an earthenware dish, a corner chipped off, and in the dish was mutton fat, and in the mutton fat a wick, burning a small knot of fire. The flame was yellow, with a little blue at its root, trembling in the wind, and round the edge of the dish was a ring of congealed fat.

Behind the post was a tent. The tent was very small, very old, its felt covered with patches, patches piled on patches, half buried in the snow, icicles hanging from its door-flap.

He lifted the door-flap, stooped, and went in.

Inside the tent it was very dark, and very warm. Dried dung was burning in the hearth, and the smoke went out through the skylight at the top. By the hearth sat an old woman, a man of middle years, and two children, the elder a girl, the younger a boy, and the boy held a lamb in his arms. Seeing a man covered all over in snow, ice hanging from his beard, burrow in through the door-flap, they all started. The lamb in the boy's arms gave a maa.

The king said his name.

The man of middle years sprang up at once, his face gone white, not knowing where to put his hands and feet.

The old woman did not get up. She sat by the hearth, raised her eyelids, and looked at him a while, looked at him quite ordinarily, as at a shepherd who had lost his way. Then she had the girl ladle out a bowl of hot milk, and had the man stir up the fire in the hearth, and then go out and lead this man's horse to a place out of the wind and cover it with a felt.

The king sat by the hearth, holding the bowl of hot milk, drinking it mouthful by mouthful. There was salt in the milk, and it burned the mouth. The snow on him melted, melted into water, and ran down his robe and onto the felt. His fingers warmed bit by bit, hurting first, hurting like needles, and only then did feeling come back.

He raised his head, and saw the post in the middle of the tent.

Something hung on the post.

It was a cross made of wood.

Not large, about a hand-span long, tied with a leather thong, hanging on the post at the height of a man's eyes. The wood had long since gone black, black and shining, as if it had been touched by many hands for many years. It was two sticks crossed, a notch cut in each where they crossed, fitted together, needing no nail and no cord. The four ends of the cross were all whittled round, like four leaves.

The king looked at it and asked what it was.

The old woman said: it's for protection.

"Protection from what?"

The old woman thought and said: I don't know. Her mother had said so, and her mother's mother had said so too; it had come down many generations. The family's tent had been changed several times, the sheep had died several times, and this thing had always hung on the post; when they moved, it was the first thing taken down, and when they arrived, the first thing hung up.

She stood, took the cross down from the post, and handed it to the king to look at.

The king took it and turned it over. On the back of the cross a small mark was carved. Carved very shallow, and worn by hands for so many years, it could hardly be made out any longer. It looked like a leaf, and like a flame.

"And what's this?"

The old woman shook her head. No one knew any more.

She sat back down by the hearth and told it slowly. Told how when she was small, her mother's mother was still alive, already blind, and one winter, when the snow was as deep as this, had told her once. Told how long, long ago, a man had come from the south. A very tall man, with a face you couldn't say was hu or Tang, who couldn't talk properly, who studied their speech for years and still got it all back to front, so that the children laughed at him. He couldn't ride well either, and was always sliding off his horse. But he could carve these. In one summer he carved a great many such crosses, and gave one to whoever came asking. And he would sprinkle water on people by the river, pour water on their heads, and as he poured say a few words. When a child was born, it had to be sprinkled once; when someone was dying, he kept watch beside them, and said the words then too. What the words were, no one remembered. By the time it came down, only one line was left.

"Which line?" asked the king.

The old woman took the cross back, held it in her two hands, bowed her head, and said it.

What she said was a few sounds. They sounded like moran, and like etrahan, and trailed off into something more, slurred, that could not be made out. It was not the speech of these parts, nor the speech of any parts the king had heard. The king could not understand it, nor could the old woman. She only knew that when you said these sounds, you had to draw with your hand like this.

She drew it. First the upright, then the crossbar.

She drew it askew.

The king looked at her hand. It was a hand that had herded sheep all its life, big-knuckled, black under the nails, drawing slowly in the firelight one stroke down, one stroke across. He did not know why, but something in him stirred.

"That lamp outside," the king asked, "did he leave that too?"

The old woman nodded.

"On snowy nights we light it," she said. "We always have."

"Light it for who to see?"

The old woman looked at him, looked a while. She seemed to think the question a little foolish, like asking why people drink water.

"For whoever's walking at night," she said.

The king asked no more.

That night he slept in that tent, slept by the hearth, covered with the family's thickest sheepskin. In the middle of the night he woke once, and heard the wind still roaring outside, the felt of the tent bellying in and out. He thought of the wooden post at the door, of the dish of mutton fat on top of the post, and did not know whether it had gone out. He lay there listening a long time, and then slept again.

In the morning the snow had stopped. The sky was clear and blue, blue as a block of ice, and the steppe lay white as far as the eye could see, so bright in the sun one could not keep one's eyes open.

His men found him. A dozen men, their faces purple with cold, calling his name all the way, treading a deep furrow through the snow. They led his horse and stood outside the tent, looking at the wooden post, at the chipped earthenware dish on top of it. The mutton fat in the dish had burned dry, and the wick had burned down to a short black stub.

One of them said that last night they had come this way following that one point of firelight; at first they had taken it for a wolf's eye.

The king mounted and rode off. When he had gone a long way, he looked back once. The tent was small as a stone, buried in the snow, already out of sight. Only the wooden post still stood there, one thin stick, one black point.

What came after, the histories recorded a little of.

A history written by the Syrians says that in that year, on the steppe, the king of a certain tribe lost his way in the snow, and when he was on the point of death, was led out. When he went home he called in the merchants who travelled the steppe and questioned them. Among the merchants were some who believed in this teaching, and they told him of it—told him of a Lord who came from heaven, told him of the cross, told him of baptism. He heard, and said he would believe. So the merchants wrote west for him, to the metropolitan of the city of Merv. The metropolitan of Merv received the letter and wrote further west, to the Patriarch in Baghdad.

Before he wrote, the metropolitan of Merv looked through the old books his predecessors had left in the scripture room, to see whether there had ever been any of their own people on the steppe before. On a page from more than a hundred years earlier, a few lines were written, the ink gone yellow. They said that a cleric from Chang'an had gone with a band of Uighurs over the Tianshan, going north, and afterwards there had been no news of him.

These few lines are not in the histories.

The metropolitan said in his letter that the king of this tribe wished to be baptized together with his people, two hundred thousand of them. And he asked the Patriarch one thing besides: on the steppe they sow no wheat, there is no flour, no vegetables; the people eat only meat and milk. When the Great Fast comes, and by the rule no flesh may be eaten, what are these people to eat?

The Patriarch wrote back: they may drink milk.

Another two hundred years went by.

Everyone on the steppe knew that in the royal tent of the Kerait a cross was kept. When a king's daughter married into another tribe, she took a cross with her; when a king's grandson went to war, in his tent there was someone holding the scriptures, saying those few lines no one could quite understand. Later still, there arose on the steppe a man of great consequence, who joined all the tribes into one, and rode, fighting, all the way to the edge of the sky. That man's daughter-in-law was a princess of the Kerait. In her tent, too, a cross hung.

At that time there was also on the steppe a boy who had come from the south, who grew up in the felt tents, learned to shoot the bow, learned to ride, and swore brotherhood with a prince of the steppe.

That is another story. Someone has written it, and written it very well.

When I was in high school I read that story, the book wrapped in a cover of brown paper on which was written Capital. I read of the steppe, of wind and snow, of those men who drew their bows to shoot the great eagles, and read till I forgot to go to class. Never once in those days did I wonder what hung in those tents.

On the day the king left, the old woman in the tent sat by the hearth a long time.

She hung the cross back on the post, hung it straight, and felt the mark on its back with her hand. The boy, his lamb in his arms, squeezed in beside her and asked whether that man would come again. She said she didn't know. The boy asked again: he's a king, so why would a king get lost? She said: when the snow's deep, anyone gets lost.

Toward evening, the sky clouded over again. The wind rose from the north, and on the steppe the snow began to fly again.

She came out of the tent and went to the wooden post. She took down the earthenware dish, tipped the burnt dregs of the fat onto the snow, dug a fresh lump of mutton fat out of a skin bag, and put it in the dish. Then she pulled a thread from the hem of her robe, rolled it between her palms into a wick, and stuck it in.

She struck the flint; she struck three times before it caught.

The flame rose, blue first, then yellow, bent aside by the wind, and stood up straight again.

She set the dish back on top of the post, looked at it a while, and turned and went back into the tent.
