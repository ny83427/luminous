Volume Ten　Morning Praises

Chapter 67　Three Generations

At the opening of spring in the second year of Kaicheng, morning devotion numbered twenty-five. Every day when the sky greyed Jingfu looked back and counted, and he had counted for nineteen years; as he counted, he had a map in his heart; who stood where, he knew with his eyes shut. Under the jujube tree, Lingbao; by the well curb it used to be Siyuan; after Siyuan died it stood empty a winter, and at the start of spring the Shi Second went to stand there; he pushed Siyuan's cart, so he stood in Siyuan's place. By the pillar of the colonnade, An Yan; An Yan was sixty-six, and the foot that had lost half a toe to frost made him limp in winter. Joseph and Elijah stood at the hall door, both past fifty, their beards half white. Under the eaves of the west wing stood Qushen, the Shi eldest, Salang, the Luo Twelfth, and the few remaining of the ten who had left—some back on the register, some not—all in their thirties and forties now, and when morning devotion broke up each went to his work: carrying goods in the West Market, copying books, keeping accounts, driving carts. The rest, the two rows under the colonnade and the women's side, were all old believers of the ward; the youngest was He Siniang, forty-six; the oldest was Old Mother Kang, seventy-three; Kang Da had died the year before last, and she came alone on a bamboo stick and stood in the last row by the gate, in the place where Kang Da used to stand.

The offering once in seven was the same. Jingfu remembered the years the hall was full: while his Master lived, the seven households come back from the south of the city, those of the ward, of the West Market, of the Shence Army—the hall could not hold them all, and some stood on the steps outside the hall door. Now some thirty sat in the hall, counting those who did not come to morning devotion; the front rows were all white heads, the back rows were white heads too, with a few of middle age among them; of those who drew gruel at the gruel shed, a few had come often these years; they came but were not baptized, sat in the last row, and when the worship had been sung did not receive the offering bread but sat watching, and when they had watched went back to the gruel shed to wait for the pot to open. Once Jingfu counted those in the hall, and halfway through Qushen leaned to his ear and said: Leader, look at this hall of people—in thirty years, how many will be left? Jingfu said: in thirty years you'll have white hair too. Qushen said what he meant was: under the white hair, would there still be any black?

Lingbao's eyes began to fail in the winter of the first year of Kaicheng. First it was reading the accounts: he could not make out Samaan's small, close Syriac letters, and had to hold the account book up to the lamp, up to the tip of his nose; later even at the tip of his nose he could not make them out, and had He Siniang read them to him; He Siniang did not know Syriac letters, so he had Qushen come and read. Later it was seeing people: at morning devotion he stood under the jujube tree and Jingfu stood in the middle of the courtyard, and looking at Jingfu he could see a dark shape and could not make out the face. He said it was as if a mist had risen in his eyes, thicker every year, thicker on the left, thinner on the right. Proprietor Wang of the West Market was long dead; his grandson kept the shop; he looked and said it was a cataract, an old man's illness; Tang physicians had no remedy; one could only take medicine, and even then it might not help; he said too that monks from India knew a golden lancet: a needle put in beside the dark of the eye, pushed in, to couch the film down; couched well, the eye was bright; couched badly, the eye was blind; there was one at the Great Xingshan Monastery; he'd heard he had couched several well and blinded several.

He Siniang wanted him to go. She said: all your life you've never set foot in a Buddhist monastery; go once this time; you needn't bow to the Buddha, but the eyes must be treated. Lingbao said he could go to a Buddhist monastery; what he feared was that needle. He said that when his Pa was young, he went with an old teacher of the law of the monastery who could treat eyes, and treated the eyes of a little princess of the Eastern Palace; in those days everyone in Chang'an said the monks of the Daqin Monastery could heal eyes; Pa carried the medicine chest and handed the knife and handed the needles; in these years those in the monastery who could heal eyes had all died, not one was left; a few years ago people from Xichuan said that when Nanzhao broke into Chengdu, it carried off a Daqin monk of the city who healed eyes, carried him off to the south, alive or dead no one knew; when he heard it, he thought several nights, and what he thought was: who was that man, what year did he come from the Patriarchate, how many years had he lived in Chengdu?—he knew nothing of him at all. He said those few needles Pa handed long ago were the very needles for couching eyes; Pa had learned, had watched how the old teacher of the law put the needle in; he was small then; Pa didn't teach him, and he didn't think to learn; later Pa grew old, his hands shook, and the craft was buried with Pa outside the Kaiyuan Gate. He said now that his eyes needed someone to treat them, the only ones in Chang'an who could hold that needle were the Indian monks in the Buddhist monasteries; he would go to beg an Indian monk in a Buddhist monastery to stick a needle into his eye, and he was afraid it would blind him; then he couldn't read the accounts, couldn't break the bread, and Chang'an wouldn't even have a blind priest.

He did not go. That winter he had Qushen read the accounts every day, and when they were read he remembered them, remembered them more clearly than before—which entry was of which year, how many names were on which page; if Qushen read one character wrong, he said: wrong; go back and read again.

In the winter of the second year of Kaicheng, at one offering once in seven, Lingbao dropped the bread on the floor. That day there were not many in the hall; it was snowing, and several old believers had not come. Lingbao stood before the table, the bread in the glass bowl on the table; he finished reciting the consecration and put out his hand for the bread; his eyes could not see clearly and his hand shook; he lifted it and broke it apart, and as he broke it his hand slipped, and the bread slid over the bowl's rim and fell on the floor before the table and broke into three pieces. Everyone in the hall saw. Of the old believers in the front rows some opened their mouths, some bowed their heads; Old Mother Kang, sitting in the last row with her bamboo stick against her knee, stood up, and sat down again.

Lingbao stood there looking down at the three pieces of bread on the floor. He could not see them clearly; he could see only some white on the floor. He slowly bent down; his knees were bad and he could not bend; Jingfu came over from the side to pick them up for him, and he said no need, he would do it himself. Holding the table's edge he squatted down bit by bit, and once down put out his hand and felt about on the floor, felt one piece, picked it up, put it back in the bowl; felt again, felt the second, put it back in the bowl; the third had rolled under the table's foot; he felt for it several times and did not find it; Jingfu squatted, picked the bread out from under the table's foot, and laid it in his palm. Gripping that piece, he held the table's edge and stood, steadied himself, put the three pieces together, and recited the consecration once more, from the beginning, and when he had finished broke the bread and handed it out piece by piece.

When it broke up that day, Jingfu was tidying the hall, and Lingbao sat on the steps at the hall door, snow falling on his cap, and did not brush it off. Jingfu went and sat down beside him. Lingbao said: from now on, when I break the bread, you stand beside me and hold the bowl for me. Jingfu said: good. Lingbao said: not only hold the bowl; he said: when I recite the consecration, you recite along, recite in your heart, till you know it by heart, not one word wrong. Jingfu said that even knowing it by heart it wouldn't count; he had never had hands laid on him. Lingbao said he knew it wouldn't count. He said: it won't count, but know it by heart all the same; and when the bishop comes one day, recite it for him; he'll hear it and know how the bread was blessed in Chang'an, and know that all these years in Chang'an not one offering once in seven broke up empty-handed. He said: if the bishop never comes and he dies, Chang'an's offering once in seven will have no bread; then you recite it; recited, it won't count, and everyone will know it doesn't count; but listening, everyone will know how it was blessed before, and remember it, remember it till one day someone comes and it can count.

Joseph was by the steps that day too, heard it, came over and said: in Merv, at the Patriarchate, when a country church had no priest, the worship was held all the same and the scriptures read all the same; when they came to the offering bread they stopped, and everyone knelt and did not receive; it was called the empty-handed worship; there were country people who had held such worship for decades. He said the leader had put out his hand today and picked up the bread; consecrated bread fallen on the floor, picked up and eaten—that was right; it must not be thrown away; the leader's hand touching it didn't matter either; what mattered was that the leader must never afterwards take touching for blessing. He said if Chang'an came to that day, let it hold the empty-handed worship; the scriptures may be recited, the bread may not be broken; if the leader recited the consecration and everyone received bread, that would be false, worse than empty hands. Lingbao said he hadn't told Jingfu to break the bread; he'd told Jingfu to remember. Joseph said remembering he didn't object to; remembering was good.

From then on, at every offering once in seven, Jingfu stood beside Lingbao, his left hand holding the glass bowl, his right hand hanging; Lingbao recited, and he recited along in his heart; Lingbao broke the bread, his hands shaking, and Jingfu's left hand held the bottom of the bowl, and the bowl did not move.

Xiaoman came in the summer of the third year of Kaicheng. That spring there was a great drought; Guanzhong's wheat brought in no harvest; people from outside the city poured into it, and the price of rice in the city rose three times in a month. The gruel shed opened oftener than any year, two pots a day, sometimes three; the monastery's rice was not enough, and Jingfu went from house to house to borrow. First he went to He Qi in the West Market; He Qi had just come back from Liangzhou and the shop had little rice either; he opened the rice bin to show Jingfu, and said: his brother-in-law's monastery he couldn't leave to fend for itself; he gave twenty shi, called it a loan, no interest, and said: Leader, don't only borrow from the hu; the hu of the West Market are hard up this year too; go to the Tang. Jingfu went to the Mi officer in Buzheng Ward; the officer gave ten shi, saying the Shence Army was short of grain too; these ten shi were his own stipend rice. It still was not enough; on the third day he went to the Secretariat and waited for Lü Shao at the gate of the Imperial City, waited till dusk; Lü Shao came out and heard him through, and said he was only a copyist and had no rice at home either; but he knew that the Department of Public Revenue had a yearly allowance of relief rice for the monasteries of the capital to give out as gruel; the Buddhist monasteries went to draw it every year; no hu monastery had ever gone; it was given, only no one had gone to ask. He wrote Jingfu a petition, written very upright, and told Jingfu to take it to the Department of Public Revenue. Jingfu took the petition and stood three days under the Department's colonnade; on the third day a judge came down, looked at the petition, looked at him, and said: the Daqin Monastery—the Daqin Monastery whose stele says the office concerned at once built a monastery in the Yining Ward of the capital? Jingfu said yes. The judge said he'd take the petition; whether it could be granted he couldn't say; after half a month the Department sent down fifteen shi of old rice, with worms in it; Jingfu had mats spread in the courtyard and they picked it over grain by grain for three days, and the worms they picked out went to the ward's chickens. The corpse cart went out oftener than any year too; the Shi Second and his brother pushed it together, sometimes two trips a day; that year outside the Kaiyuan Gate another row of stakes was added.

One day in the fourth month, the Shi Second pushed the cart out of the Jinguang Gate, and under a willow by the moat saw a woman sitting against the tree, her head hanging on her breast; she was already stiff, and in her arms she held a bundle, and the bundle was moving. He undid the bundle; inside was a child two or three months old, so thin it was only skin, its mouth still moving, looking for the breast. He tucked the child into his robe, lifted the woman onto the cart, and pushed it back into the city, to the monastery gate; the child cried in his robe, a cry as thin as a kitten's.

That day was the term of Xiaoman, Grain Full. He Siniang took the child; she had no milk; she ran to the south end of the ward to find the Mis' Third Daughter—who in the end had not waited for Rijin but married a Sogdian carter of the West Market, and had just borne her third, and had plenty of milk; she fed it a mouthful, the child took hold and stopped crying. Lingbao came at dusk to look; his eyes could not see clearly; he had He Siniang bring the child to him, and put his face close, close to the child's face, looked, and said: it's a girl; asked what she was called. He Siniang said when the Shi Second picked her up there was nothing on the child, no name. Lingbao said: today is Xiaoman; call her Xiaoman; the wheat didn't fill; call her Little Full; in years to come the harvest must fill once.

Xiaoman's mother was buried outside the Kaiyuan Gate; the stake was written by the Shi Second; he wrote badly, and took three tries to get it right; what he wrote was Mother of Xiaoman, under a willow outside the Jinguang Gate, fourth month of the third year of Kaicheng. The Mis' Third Daughter nursed the child, nursed her till she was weaned in autumn; then she was brought back to the monastery; the monastery had no women, so He Siniang took her home to the south end of the ward to raise her, and Old Mother Kang came over every day to help mind her; she was seventy-four, her eyes dim too; she sat under the pomegranate tree holding the child and humming "In the highest of the heavens, deep reverence and praise," humming it out of tune, and the child listened and fell asleep.

Each time Jingfu went to Lingbao's house and saw that old one and that little one sitting under the pomegranate tree, he thought of his own childhood: he came to the monastery at five; his Pa had died in Huaixi; Lord Mi handed him over to his Master; that day he held a ladle himself at the well and drank a ladleful of cold water; his Master stood by watching and did not stop him; afterwards in all his life cold water never made him ill. He thought: when he came to the monastery his Master was fifty-five; now he was forty-three, and Xiaoman could not yet walk.

Guangzheng's letter came in the autumn of the fourth year of Kaicheng. It was brought by Li Su's youngest son. Li Su had been dead over twenty years; of his sons, one carried on watching the stars for him at the Directorate of Astronomy, one was in the Shence Army, one in the Court of Imperial Sacrifices, all with the character jing in their names. The one at the Directorate was the youngest, past fifty himself, risen to Observatory Gentleman; he did not come to the offering once in seven, but every year on Li Su's death day he came to the monastery once, stood a while at the scripture room door, and spoke a few words with Lingbao, saying that his Pa had once watched the stars on this roof with Elder Yonan. That autumn he came, and it was not the death day; he said something had been sent from over in Guangzhou; a clan cousin of his family in the foreigners' quarter at Guangzhou had entrusted it to a hu merchant coming north; the cousin said this thing had lain several years with the Li family in the foreigners' quarter; an old sailor off a ship from Basra had handed it to the Li family, and the old sailor said a Tang man had entrusted it to him, and the Tang man said: the Daqin Monastery, Yining Ward, Chang'an, to Lingbao. The Li family kept it and never found a reliable man going to Chang'an; some years ago they found one, and he died of sickness on the way at Hongzhou, and the thing was sent back to Guangzhou; only this year had they found another. He said his cousin also said that a dozen-odd years ago a Tang man from Chang'an, carrying a letter from the He family of the West Market, had stayed over a month with the Li family waiting for a ship; the Tang man was called Guangzheng; afterwards he boarded a ship bound for Basra and left, and since then the Li family had had no news of him.

The thing was an oilcloth packet the size of a palm; the oilcloth had gone stiff, its edges bleached white by seawater, but it was sewn very close, the stitches crooked in several places. Lingbao held it and felt it, felt those few crooked stitches, and his hand stopped. He said: Guangzheng's Ma sewed this. Guangzheng's Ma had died over ten years ago, in those two rooms in Buzheng Ward; before she died her eyes were quite blind; on the thread-board at the head of her bed there was still a row of needles, threaded for her by Guangzheng before he left; she used them several years, one by one, used them till she died, and three were left.

Inside the oilcloth packet was a small piece of parchment, rolled, tied with a fine hemp cord. Lingbao could not see it and gave it to Joseph. Joseph untied the cord and spread the parchment on the table and held the lamp to it. On the parchment were Syriac letters, written very small; the hand was Guangzheng's; Joseph knew it; Guangzheng wrote Syriac well; Jingjing had once said that this one could take over the scripture room one day. There were only a few lines. Joseph read them, first in Syriac, then said them over in Tang.

"To the Daqin Monastery, Yining Ward, Chang'an; the Great Worthy Lingbao; the leader Jingfu. Arrived. The Patriarch consents. Waiting for a ship. Guangzheng. In the year of the Greeks one thousand one hundred and forty-four." When Joseph had read it, everyone in the room stood still. Jingfu asked what year one thousand one hundred and forty-four was. Joseph reckoned on his fingers, reckoned twice, and said: the sixth year of Dahe, or the seventh, around winter. Jingfu said: then that's six or seven years ago. Joseph said yes.

Lingbao sat by the table, put out his hand and took the parchment, held it in his hand close to his eyes; he could not see those few letters, only a few dark lines on the parchment. He turned the parchment over; there was nothing on the back. He turned it back again and felt those few lines of writing with his fingers; the ink was dry, and he could not feel the strokes. He asked: arrived—arrived at the Patriarchate? Joseph said the letter only said arrived. He asked: the Patriarch consents—consents to what? to send someone, or to lay hands on Guangzheng? Joseph said the letter only said consents; he said the Patriarchate had changed patriarchs several times these dozen-odd years; Guangzheng had arrived, but word of consent might not have been given that very year; he might have waited several years. He asked: waiting for a ship—a ship coming east? Joseph said the letter only said waiting for a ship. He asked: the letter was written in the sixth year of Dahe—and now? Has he found a ship or not, boarded it or not, has the ship arrived or not, has it sunk or not? Joseph said the letter didn't say.

Lingbao nodded. He rolled up the parchment, tied it with the fine hemp cord, put it back in the oilcloth packet, and gripped the oilcloth packet in his hand, gripped it. He said he understood; he said this was something to hope for. Jingfu said: yes, it's something to hope for. Li Su's youngest son stood by, looking at one and at another, and only when he saw Lingbao tuck the oilcloth packet into his robe did he speak; he said that while his Pa lived he had said: what a star-watcher fears most is a star whose light you see, but the light has travelled decades to reach your eye; when you see it, the star may long since be gone. When he had said it, he felt he had said the wrong thing, and said again: but then again, it may still be there.

Lingbao said: Observatory Gentleman, what your Pa said is right; both are right. He had Qushen bring a brush and open his little book that he carried, to the page after the one recording He Siniang—that line "Ninth month of the third year of Changqing: Guangzheng goes to the Patriarchate to ask for a bishop," with the empty line below it; he could not see, and felt that empty line with his finger, and had Qushen write a line of small characters beside the empty line, close against the paper's edge. Qushen asked what to write. Lingbao said: write, Autumn of the fourth year of Kaicheng: Guangzheng's letter arrives: arrived; the Patriarch consents; waiting for a ship. Qushen wrote it. Lingbao said: that empty line—still empty.

At the first offering once in seven after that, more people came to the hall than usual. The Li family's Observatory Gentleman came; He Qi came back from Liangzhou and came too; the Mi officer came, and Qiniang came too—the first time she had come in at the monastery gate on a day that was not a funeral. The news had gone out from the Li family's side, gone to the West Market, gone to the ward: Guangzheng had sent a letter; the Patriarchate had consented; the bishop was coming. The old believers sat in the hall; some wiped tears, some asked those beside them when the bishop would arrive, and those beside them said they didn't know; the letter said waiting for a ship.

Jingfu stood beside Lingbao. Lingbao recited the consecration, very slowly, word by word, and at the end put out his hand for the bread. Jingfu's left hand held the bottom of the glass bowl, and the bowl did not move.
