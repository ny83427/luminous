Volume Eight　The Daqin Monastery

Chapter 48　Raising the Stele

The masons carved forty days.

There were two masons, one old, one young, father and son, who had come from the northern hills along with the stone. The old one was past sixty, the skin of his hands like old bark; when he carved he brought his eyes very close, so close the tip of his nose was white with stone dust. The young one was twenty or so, and saw to whetting the chisels, sweeping the dust, handing the hammer. The two of them put up a straw shed before the stele, carved by day and slept in the shed at night, saying they were afraid the stone would crack in the frost and must watch over it.

The monastery sent them their meals. Two a day: one of bread, one of soup. The soup was stewed by the elder Ishaq, and after the old mason had drunk it the first time, the next day he asked whether there was more.

The sound of carving rang in the monastery all winter.

Ting, ting, ting. Not hurried, not slow, like someone knocking at a door a long way off. When the people of the monastery said the evening office, in the middle of it they would hear from the front courtyard a ting, and another ting. At first they found it noisy; after ten days or so they were used to it. After ten more days, one night the old mason fell ill and rested the whole night, and the people of the monastery could not sleep for the quiet.

Jingjing went to look once every day.

He said nothing, only stood by the straw shed watching. Watched the old mason make one cut, and under the red character a white line showed; another cut, and the white line deepened; another cut, and a stroke was out. When a character was finished, the young mason swept the stone dust off with a bristle brush, and there the character was on the stone—a blue-black ground, a white groove.

He watched the words he had written turn to stone one by one.

One day the old mason came to 阿羅訶, Alaha, stopped and asked him: "These three characters—is it a man's name?"

Jingjing thought and said: "In a way."

The old mason said "Oh," and carved on. When he had carved them he ran his finger once over the three characters and said: "A man's name should be carved deeper."

Jingjing did not stop him.

Some time after the twentieth of the twelfth month, the carving of the characters was done.

The last carved were the nine great characters at the head of the stele: The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom, three lines of three characters each, beneath the cross. The old mason took five days over those nine characters, two a day, and when he had finished sat in the straw shed and rested a whole day, doing nothing, only looking.

The Syriac letters Yisi had written were carved by the young mason. The young mason could not read them, and traced the vermilion strokes one by one. When he came to Yisi's line he asked Yisi: "What does this line say?"

Yisi said: "It's about my father."

The young mason said "Oh," and carved a little more slowly.

After the New Year, on the fifth of the first month, father and son pulled down the straw shed and swept the stone dust off the ground, swept it three times, and when it was swept, splashed it all over with water. The old mason stood before the stele and looked a long time, and then bowed to the stele with clasped hands.

He had been a mason all his life, and had carved more steles than he could count. Jingjing asked him: do you bow every time you finish? The old mason said he did. Jingjing asked him why.

The old mason thought and said: "When it's finished, it's not mine any more."

On the seventh of the first month, before it was light, the monastery was up.

That day was a great yaosenwen day, a Lord's Day. By the monastery's rule, the Lord's Day service was held in the hall. This time, Azarel said, the service would be held as usual, and when it was over, everyone would go to the front courtyard.

The front courtyard was not large. The stele stood in the very middle, with a ring of empty ground round it, and beyond the empty ground the courtyard wall. By daybreak the courtyard was already full of people.

The people of the monastery stood in front. Elders, priests, deacons, monks, row by row, all in clean white robes. Of the monk-soldiers of those days nine were left, their hair all white, standing at the very edge. Sargis was gone, and his son stood in his old place, taller than his father, so that he had to stoop to come in at the monastery gate.

Behind were the believers. Sogdian merchants, old Persians, Tang people, and a few children of mixed Tang and hu blood, squirming about until a grown-up pressed them still. Xu Jing came too. He minded a shop in the West Market for someone these days, his back a little bent now, his mouth the same mouth; as soon as he was in at the gate he began talking—that he had watched this stone brought in, that he had said years ago in Xizhou this monastery would raise a stele sooner or later—and halfway through he looked round and everyone was looking at the stele and no one was listening to him, so he stopped, and looked too.

Further back were the people of the ward. The vinegar seller, the shoe seller, the man with the hu bread oven, those who came every morning for bread under the locust tree at the gate, standing at the courtyard gate craning to look in. The courtyard could hold no more, and a few half-grown boys had climbed onto the courtyard wall and sat astride the top of it, one here, one there, like a row of sparrows.

Few came in any official capacity. Official Pei came, in an everyday robe, leaning on a stick, standing in the crowd and not pushing forward. Li Su came too, standing at the back; he looked at the sky, then at that line of date on the stele, and nodded.

Lü Xiuyan did not come. He sent word that the characters were written, and were the stele's business now; whether he came or not made no difference.

When the sun showed above the eastern wall of the courtyard, the elder Azarel went up to the stele.

He recited a passage of scripture in Syriac. He was old, his voice not clear; those in front could hear, those at the back heard only a humming. When he had finished he made the sign of the cross. Those in front all made it after him; of those behind, some made it, some did not, and some made it halfway, not knowing whether to touch the left shoulder or the right first, and stopped there.

Then Azarel turned and nodded to Jingjing.

Jingjing went up to the stele.

He did not read the whole text. The whole text was more than seventeen hundred characters; by the time he finished, the sun would be overhead. He read only the hymn at the end. He stood at the foot of the stele, his head tipped back, facing the characters carved in the stone, and read it line by line. He did not really need to look; he had copied those characters four times and knew them by heart long ago. But he read them looking all the same.

"The true Lord is without beginning, deep and still for ever…"

He read very slowly. The people of the ward could not understand, but they could hear it was Tang, could hear it was a fine composition, four characters a line, four characters a line, like someone walking step by step. The boys on the courtyard wall stopped their fidgeting too, every one of them with his mouth open, listening.

At the last few lines Jingjing's voice faltered once.

"…How broad is the Way, how fine its answering! Forced to give it a name, we set forth the Three in One. The lord can act, the subject can record; we raise this great stele to praise the supreme good."

When he had finished, the courtyard was very quiet.

After a while the elder Marya was the first to weep aloud. When he wept, several old believers behind wept with him. One old Persian, his beard white as wool, wept and recited scripture in Syriac at the top of his voice, louder than Azarel. The few Tang believers did not know whether to weep or laugh, and looked at one another; one laughed first, and then several laughed. And laughing, their eyes went red too.

The elder Samaan neither wept nor laughed. He stood there with his head tipped back, looking at the cross at the head of the stele, looking a long time.

Yisi stood at the side of the stele all the while.

He did not stand at the front. Azarel had called him, and he shook his head, and stood at the side, beside the Syriac letters he had written himself. That day he wore an old grey robe, not the purple robe. Jingjing had asked him that morning, and he said the purple robe was kept for the New Year.

He simply stood there looking at the stele.

The passage about him on the stele, he did not go to look at. What he looked at was that line of Syriac at the bottom—his father's name, and Balkh. He looked a long time, and said not a word.

Someone in the crowd said under his breath: that's the Great Virtue Yi, of Lord Director Guo's command. And those beside him stood on tiptoe to look this way, at the scar a little left of the middle of his brow, at his broad shoulders. Yisi seemed not to hear.

Later he walked away from the stele. He went through the crowd to the foot of the courtyard wall, looking for a place with no one to stand a while. He looked up and saw a boy sitting astride the top of the wall.

The boy was ten or so, thin, his face red with cold, both arms round the top of the wall; seeing him look up he shrank in fright, thinking he was going to be scolded.

Yisi looked at him, looked a while. Then he took a hu flatbread from his coat—he had put it there before he came out that morning and had never had a chance to eat it. He broke the bread in two, put one half back in his coat, and reached the other half up.

The boy was taken aback, and took it.

"Slowly," said Yisi.

The boy held the bread, looking at him as if he had not understood. Yisi said nothing more, turned and went.

Towards noon, a man came from the Guo household.

It was an old servant, past sixty, in the blue livery of the Guo household, leading a donkey. He stood at the courtyard gate and asked which was the Great Virtue Yi. Someone called Yisi over.

The old servant bowed to Yisi and said: "The Lord Director is ill and can't come. The Lord Director sent me with one sentence."

Yisi said: "Please say it."

"The Lord Director says," and the old servant cleared his throat, as if afraid of saying it wrong, and recited it word by word, "he's heard the inscription read. One sentence in it is wrong."

The faces of several of the men behind Yisi changed. Jingjing came over too.

"Which sentence?"

"The one that says though he was trusted even in the bedchamber," said the old servant. "The Lord Director says: what bedchamber was there in camp? In the tent there was one folding stool. He slept on it, and everyone else sat on the ground."

The courtyard was silent a moment.

Then Yisi laughed.

He laughed very softly; only the corners of his mouth moved, and the scar on his brow creased with them. Jingjing had been with him more than thirty years, and the times he had seen him laugh like that could be counted on two hands.

"Go back and tell the Lord Director," said Yisi, "the characters are already carved. They can't be changed."

The old servant nodded, bowed again, and went off leading his donkey.

After the stele was raised, a good many people in Chang'an came to see it.

The first month, people came every day. Believers, and those who did not believe; those who could read, standing before the stele reading it character by character; those who could not, walking once round the stele, touching it, and when they had touched it, going. A few scholars came to copy the inscription, and having copied it stood there discussing it, saying the composition, all parallel fours and sixes, was the work of a real hand, only who in the world this Alaha might be, they could not say. And a monk came once, stood before the stele the better part of an hour, and when he had read it, gave a cold laugh and went.

The elder Samaan went every morning to wipe the stele. He took a cloth and wiped it from the bottom up, and where he could not reach he stood on a little stool. He wiped the stele very carefully, more carefully than he kept the rule. The people of the monastery all said: the elder Samaan was the one least willing to raise the stele, and now it's raised, the one who can least bear to part with it is him.

Samaan heard it, and did not argue. He went on wiping it every day.

At the end of the third month, a caravan came from the west.

In the caravan were two men of the Luminous congregation from Baghdad, who had travelled the better part of a year, and the first thing they did on entering Chang'an was come to the monastery to worship. When the service was over they ate in the back courtyard, and as they ate, they spoke of the Patriarchate.

They said the Patriarch Hnanisho had died the year before.

Everyone at the table put down their bowls.

The two men had thought everyone knew already; they looked at this one and that one, grew a little alarmed, and began to stammer. They said the Patriarch Hnanisho had died in the spring of last year. After he died the Patriarchate had quarrelled for months over who should succeed. Later one was elected, called Timothy, who had been a bishop over Mosul way. Some said he had bought it with money, some said he had relied on great officials in Baghdad, some would not accept him, and still would not.

"Spring?" asked Azarel. "Last spring?"

"Last spring."

Azarel said no more. He thought of that letter of leave, which had come last ninth month. The letter had left Baghdad last spring.

No one at the table spoke again. When the two men of the congregation had finished eating, they hastily took their leave.

That night the elders gathered again in the small room in the back courtyard.

Samaan spoke first. He said: the stele says Hnanisho is reigning. But on the day the stele was raised, Hnanisho had been dead nearly a year. What the stele says is wrong.

"If it's wrong, it must be changed," he said. "Grind away those two characters and carve Timothy in their place."

Marya said: "Once it's carved in stone, you can't grind it away. Grind it, and the stele's spoiled."

"Spoiled or not, it must be ground," said Samaan. "What the stele says, people will take as true."

Sabrisho said: "Won't do." He said: this matter of Timothy is still only the word of two merchants. No letter has come from the Patriarchate; it can't be changed. Change it wrong, and it'll look worse.

Babai said Hnanisho was on the stele not only in the Syriac but in the Tang too—at that time the Lord of the Law, the monk Ningshu, oversaw the Luminous congregations of the East—and that was in Lü Xiuyan's hand. To change it, both places must be changed, and Lü Xiuyan must be brought back.

Azarel said nothing all the while.

They talked a while, and all looked at Yisi.

Yisi sat by the door. He sat a long time before he spoke.

"On the day the stele was raised," he said, "the Patriarch we knew of was Hnanisho."

Samaan said: "But he was already dead."

"We didn't know."

"If we didn't know, then what the stele says is wrong."

"It's wrong," said Yisi. "But it isn't a lie."

Samaan looked at him.

"That day we prayed for him," said Yisi. "We truly prayed for him. We wrote his name truly thinking he was there. What we didn't know, the stone doesn't know either."

He paused, and said again: "Whoever looks at this stele after us and sees this name will know how far Chang'an was from Baghdad that year."

For a long time there was no sound in the little room.

Then Azarel said: "We don't change it."

When they had broken up, Yisi did not go back to his room.

He went alone to the front courtyard. The moon had risen and shone on the stele, and the blue-black stone gave off a white sheen. The cross at the head of the stele could be seen very clearly in the moonlight.

He went to the side of the stele, squatted down, and found that line of Syriac. It was in his own hand, written after the date: "In the days of the Father of Fathers, Mar Hnanisho, the Catholicos Patriarch."

The letters were carved very deep, and a little of the day's dust had gathered in the grooves.

Yisi put out his hand and wiped the dust from those letters with his sleeve.
