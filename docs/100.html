Appendix 11　Li Zhizao's "After Reading the Luminous Stele": Translation and Related Studies

After the stele was unearthed, the first Chinese essay to discuss it seriously was Li Zhizao's After Reading the Luminous Stele (讀景教碑書後). It was written in the fifth year of Tianqi (1625) and runs to fewer than fifteen hundred characters. It identifies the "Luminous Teaching" of the stele with the Catholic faith that Matteo Ricci had brought, and so answered, for the Christian literati of the late Ming, the most damaging charge against them: that this teaching had "never been heard of in antiquity." The passage Semedo recalls in this book's Prologue comes from this essay.

This appendix first gives the original with a translation, then discusses where the essay came from, how it argues, what it got right and wrong, and the later scholarship.

Note on the text

  • Base text: The original is taken from the copy appended to Manuel Dias the Younger's A Correct Explanation of the Hymn on the Tang Luminous Stele (唐景教碑頌正詮, seventeenth year of Chongzhen, 1644), checked against the transcriptions on Wikisource and on cct.chinesecs.cn. Both transcriptions follow the Correct Explanation and agree in their wording. The essay is also included in Li Zhizao's own compilation, First Collection of Heavenly Learning (天學初函); Zheng Cheng's edited Collected Works of Li Zhizao (李之藻集, Zhonghua Book Company, 2018) places it in juan 4, "Prefaces, Colophons and Inscriptions," with the note "fifth year of Tianqi" under the title. Neither has been collated here; variant readings remain to be added.
  • Punctuation and paragraphing: The original is broken only with full stops; the present punctuation and paragraphing are this book's.
  • Characters: The original is kept in traditional characters, with variant forms such as 衺, 叅, 纒, 寕, 滛 reproduced as found.

所云"先先無元，後後妙有"，開天地，匠萬物，立初人，衆聖元尊眞主，非皇皇天主，疇能當此？What it says—"before all beginnings, without origin; after all endings, mysteriously existing"—who opened heaven and earth, fashioned the ten thousand things, set up the first man, the true Lord whom all the holy ones revere as first: who but the glorious Lord of Heaven could answer to this?
The stele reads 先先而無元, 後後而妙有; see Appendix 2, section 1.
其云"三一妙身"，卽三位一體也；其云"三一分身"，卽費略降誕也；其云"同人出代"，云"室女誕聖於大秦"，卽以天主性接人性，胎於如德亞國室女瑪利亞而生也。When it says "the wondrous Body of the Three-in-One," that is the Trinity; when it says "the divided Person of the Three-in-One," that is the birth of the Filius; when it says "appearing in the world in the likeness of man" and "a virgin gave birth to the Holy One in Daqin," that is the joining of the nature of the Lord of Heaven to the nature of man, conceived in the womb of the virgin Mary of the land of Judaea and born.
費略 (feilüe): a transliteration of Latin *Filius*, "Son." 如德亞 (Rudeya): Judaea, the usual rendering in late Ming Catholic texts.
"景宿告祥"，異星見也；"覩耀來貢"，三君朝也；"神天宣慶"，天神降也；"亭午升眞"，則救世傳教功行完，而日中上昇也。"A luminous star announced the blessing"—that is the appearing of the strange star; "seeing its brightness they came with tribute"—that is the homage of the three kings; "the heavenly spirits proclaimed the joy"—that is the descent of the angels; "at high noon he ascended to the true"—that is, when the work of saving the world and spreading the teaching was complete, he ascended at midday.
Three kings: the Magi, called "the three kings" in late Ming Catholic texts. Li Zhizao takes 亭午升真 as an ascension at noon. The sentences around this line in the stele speak of the Passion, and later scholars have read it as the noon of the cross.
至於法浴之水、十字之持、七時禮讚、七日一薦，悉與利氏西來傳述規程脗合。As for the water of the washing of the Law (baptism), the holding of the cross, praise at the seven hours, and an offering once in seven days—all agree exactly with the rules Master Ricci brought from the West and handed on to us.
"Praise at the seven hours" and "an offering once in seven days" are the stele's own words. This book's term "offering once in seven" is taken from here.
而今云"陡斯"，碑云"阿羅訶"；今云"大傲魔魁"，碑云"娑殫"：則皆如德亞國古經語。We say today *Dousi*; the stele says *Aluohe*. We say today "the great proud chief of demons"; the stele says *Suodan*. These are all words of the ancient scriptures of the land of Judaea.
陡斯 (Dousi): a transliteration of Latin *Deus*; after Ricci, 天主 "Lord of Heaven" came to be used instead. "The great proud chief of demons": the prince of demons (Lucifer). *Aluohe* comes from Syriac *Alāhā* and *Suodan* from *Sāṭānā*, cognate with the Hebrew. To call them "words of the ancient scriptures of Judaea" is broadly right.
不曰如德亞而曰大秦者：考《唐書》拂菻國，一名大秦，西去中國四萬里；又考《西洋圖誌》，如德亞畿東一道，其名曰秦，道里約略相同。阿羅本輩，殆從此邦來者，故以大秦稱云。That it says not Judaea but Daqin is because: the *Tang History* records the country of Fulin, also called Daqin, forty thousand *li* west of China; and the *Gazetteer of the Western Ocean* records a circuit east of the capital district of Judaea named Qin, at about the same distance. Alopen and his companions probably came from this land, and so it is called Daqin.
The *Old Tang History*, "Treatise on the Western Rong," entry on Fulin: "The country of Fulin, also called Daqin, lies on the Western Sea... forty thousand *li* from the capital." Fulin generally means the Eastern Roman Empire. What the *Gazetteer of the Western Ocean* refers to is unclear—perhaps a book like Giulio Aleni's *Record of the Lands outside the Imperial Domain* (職方外紀), for which Li Zhizao wrote a preface in the third year of Tianqi. No place answering to "a circuit east of the capital district of Judaea named Qin" can be found today. Alopen in fact came from Persia; see Appendices 1 and 5.
其至長安也，以貞觀九年，上遡耶穌降生近六百禩。是時宗徒傳教，殆徧西土；大唐德威遠曁，應有經像，重譯而來。爾乃宰相郊迎，翻經內殿，為造大秦寺於義寕坊，命名景教。景者，大也，炤也，光明也。They reached Chang'an in the ninth year of Zhenguan, nearly six hundred years back from the birth of Jesus. By then the apostles' preaching had spread nearly throughout the West; the virtue and might of the great Tang reached far, and it was natural that scriptures and images should come through many translations. So the chief minister went out to the outskirts to meet them, the scriptures were translated in the inner palace, the Daqin Monastery was built for them in the Yining Ward, and it was named the Luminous Teaching. *Jing*, luminous, means great; means shining; means light.
禩: year. The ninth year of Zhenguan (635) is six hundred and thirty-odd years from the birth of Jesus; "nearly six hundred" is a round figure. The stele says only that the scriptures were translated "in the library"; "named the Luminous Teaching" is the church's own name for itself as the stele records it, not a name granted by the court; the monastery in the Yining Ward was built in the twelfth year of Zhenguan. "*Jing* means great, means shining, means light" is the most quoted sentence of the essay.
大帝時，又勑諸州各置景寺，崇奉之至，顯與儒、釋、玄三教共峙寰宇，非特柔懷異域，昭王會一統之盛而已也。In the time of the Great Emperor it was further ordered that a Luminous monastery be set up in every prefecture. Its veneration was at its height; it plainly stood in the world alongside the three teachings of the Confucians, the Buddhists and the Daoists—not merely a matter of gentleness to distant lands, displaying the splendour of the assembled kings and a unified realm.
The Great Emperor: Tang Gaozong, posthumously titled Heavenly Emperor the Great. The stele reads "a Luminous monastery was set up in every prefecture." The assembly of kings: the "Assembly of Kings" chapter of the *Lost Book of Zhou* records the tribute of the four quarters; it came to mean the homage of all nations.
聖厯則武氏宣滛，先天則太平亂政。貞衺旣相挺迕，水火應必煎烹，用壯相傾，理同盗憎，禍來無鄉，葢千古有同慨焉。In the Shengli years there was the Empress Wu's licentiousness; in the Xiantian years, the Taiping Princess's misrule. Since true and false collide, fire and water must scald each other; they overthrew it by force—the same principle as "the thief hates the master"; disaster comes from no direction. This is a sigh shared through a thousand ages.
The stele itself reads: "In the Shengli years the sons of Śākya used their strength and raised their voices in Eastern Zhou; at the end of Xiantian, lesser scholars laughed aloud and slandered us in Western Hao"—meaning the attacks of Buddhists and Daoists. Li Zhizao turns this into the misrule of the Empress Wu and the Taiping Princess, without naming Buddhists or Daoists, though he still uses the stele's phrase "used their strength." The thief hates: *Zuo Commentary*, Duke Cheng year 15: "The thief hates the master; the people resent those above them."
羅含、及烈重振斯文；佶和再來，渙頒睿劄。玄、肅、代、德四朝，寵賚彌渥。汾陽重廣法堂，依仁施利，修舉哀七端，遂勒此碑，以紀歲月。Luohan and Jilie revived this teaching; Jihe came again, and the emperor's own writing was bestowed. Under the four reigns of Xuanzong, Suzong, Daizong and Dezong, favour and gifts grew ever richer. In the time of the Prince of Fenyang the halls of the Law were enlarged, alms were given according to benevolence, the seven works of mercy were practised; and so this stele was carved, to record the years and months.
Luohan, Jilie and Jihe all appear in the stele (Appendix 4). The emperor's own writing: Xuanzong's inscribing of the monastery's board. "In the time of Fenyang the halls of the Law were enlarged": in the stele the subject of "sometimes keeping the old monasteries, sometimes enlarging the halls of the Law" is Yisi; Guo Ziyi is only the man Yisi served. Li Zhizao's sentence, taken literally, ties the enlarging of the halls to Fenyang's name, and nowhere in the essay is Yisi named. 哀七端: probably the "seven works of mercy" (哀矜七端). Late Ming Catholic catechisms list seven "corporal works of mercy," usually given as feeding the hungry, giving drink to the thirsty, clothing the naked, caring for the sick, sheltering the traveller, ransoming captives and burying the dead (the wording differs slightly between versions; to be checked). Yisi's charity as recorded on the stele ("when the hungry came he fed them, when the cold came he clothed them, the sick he healed and raised, the dead he buried and gave rest") contains four of these, and Li Zhizao calls it by the Catholic name.
其頌中多述唐德，亦具景教大指。所稱"賜良和""懸景日"，明著肇我人類，以及補贖救世之恩。而貞觀所譯，並所留二十七部經文，卽今貝葉藏中，或尚有可檢者。The hymn mostly praises the virtue of the Tang, but it also contains the main points of the Luminous Teaching. What it calls "bestowing good harmony" and "hanging up the luminous sun" plainly set out the grace of the creation of our human kind and of the redemption that saves the world. As for what was translated in Zhenguan, and the twenty-seven scriptures that were left, there may still be some to be found in the Buddhist canon today.
"He bestowed good harmony on them and set them over the sea of change" is in the first section of the preface and speaks of the creation of man; "he hung up the luminous sun to break open the dark realm" speaks of redemption. Both sentences are in fact in the preface, not the hymn; Li Zhizao calls the whole the "hymn." The stele reads "twenty-seven scriptures were left." 貝葉藏, the palm-leaf canon: the Buddhist canon. The Chinese texts of the Tang Church of the East that survive today mostly come from Dunhuang; the *Book of Honoured Ones* lists the titles of thirty-five scriptures—see the note to Chapter 59.
所疑傳教士曷以僧名，則緣彼國無分道俗，男子皆髠，華人强指為僧，渠輩無能自異云爾。卽利氏初入五羊也，亦復數年混跡，後遇瞿太素氏，乃辨非僧，然後蓄髮稱儒，觀光上國。As to the doubt why the preachers were called "monks": it is because in their country there is no distinction of religious and lay, and all the men are shaven; the Chinese insisted on calling them monks, and they had no way to set themselves apart. So too when Master Ricci first entered the City of Five Rams, he passed several years mixed among the monks, until he met Qu Taisu and made it clear that he was not a monk; after that he let his hair grow, called himself a scholar, and came to the capital.
"All the men are shaven" is wrong: the stele itself says "they keep the beard to show outward conduct, they shave the crown to show they have no inward passions"—it is the clergy who shave the crown and keep the beard, not that all men shave. City of Five Rams: Guangzhou, here standing for Guangdong as a whole (in 1583 Ricci first reached Zhaoqing). Qu Taisu: Qu Rukui, who urged Ricci to give up the monk's robe for the scholar's. The Tang called the clergy of the Church of the East "monks," and this book follows that usage; see the entry "The Luminous Church does not leave the household" in the continuation memo.
我神祖禮隆柔遠，賜館多年。於時文武大臣，有能繼房、郭之芳踪，演正眞之絕緒者乎？七千部奧義宏辭，梯航嗣集，開局演譯，良足以增輝冊府，軼古昭來。其如道不虛行，故迄今尚有所待。Our Divine Ancestor honoured the men from afar with great courtesy and granted them lodging for many years. Was there then among the civil and military ministers anyone able to follow in the fragrant steps of Fang and Guo, and carry on the long-broken thread of the true and pure? Seven thousand volumes of deep meaning and great words have come one after another over mountains and seas; were a bureau opened to translate them, it would truly add lustre to the archives of the state, surpass antiquity and shine for those to come. But "the Way does not walk in vain," and so to this day it still waits.
Divine Ancestor: the Ming emperor Shenzong (Wanli), who in 1601 allowed Ricci to reside in the capital and after his death granted him burial ground. Fang and Guo: Fang Xuanling and Guo Ziyi—on the stele, the man who welcomed Alopen and the man Yisi served. Seven thousand volumes: the Western books Nicolas Trigault brought from Europe in 1620, said to number seven thousand. The Way does not walk in vain: *Book of Changes*, "Appended Statements" II: "If it is not the right man, the Way does not walk in vain."
三十餘載以來，我中土紳士習見習聞，於西賢之道行，誰不歎異而敬禮之？然而疑信相叅，詫為新說者，亦繁有焉。詎知九百九十年前，此教流行已久！雖世代之廢興不一，乃上主之景命無渝。For more than thirty years, the gentlemen of our Middle Land have seen and heard of it often; of the Way and conduct of the Western worthies, who has not marvelled and honoured them? Yet those half in doubt and half in faith, who are startled at it as a new doctrine, are also many. How should they know that nine hundred and ninety years ago this teaching had long been spreading! Though the generations rose and fell unevenly, the luminous mandate of the Lord on high has never changed.
More than thirty years: counting from Ricci's entry into China in 1583. Nine hundred and ninety years: from the ninth year of Zhenguan (635) to the fifth year of Tianqi (1625). "Startled at it as a new doctrine" was precisely the main argument of the opponents of the faith from the Nanjing persecution on; see section 3 of this appendix.
是佑諸賢，閒關無阻；更留貞石，忽效其靈。所繇仁覆閔下，不忍令魔錮重封，天路終閼，故多年秘奇厚土，似俟明時。今茲煥啟人文，用章古教，而後乃知克己昭事，俾無忝生而怛死，此學自昔有聞。It is he who protected the worthies, so that through hard and dangerous roads they met no hindrance; and he left this stone of constancy, which suddenly showed its power. It is because his benevolence covers and pities those below, and he could not bear to let the demons lock and seal it over and over, so that the road to heaven would be blocked for good; therefore for many years he hid this wonder in the deep earth, as if waiting for an enlightened time. Now that human culture is brightly opened, it serves to make the ancient teaching manifest; and then men know that the learning of mastering oneself and serving on high with clarity—so as not to shame one's life nor fear one's death—was heard of long ago.
閒關: hard and dangerous roads. 貞石: the stele stone. 昭事: *Book of Odes*, "Greater Odes," "Daming": "with clarity he served the Lord on High"; late Ming Christian literati often used it for the service of God. The character 怛 in 俾無忝生而怛死 is hard to construe; it is tentatively taken here as "not fear death," to be checked.
唐天子尚知莊事，而况我聖朝重熙累洽，河清璽出、儀鳳呈祥之日哉！If the Son of Heaven of the Tang knew to serve it with reverence, how much more our sacred dynasty, light upon light and peace upon peace, in days when the Yellow River runs clear, the seal comes forth, and the phoenix comes in its grace to show good omens!
莊事: serve with reverence. The clear river, the seal, the phoenix: stock phrases for the omens of an age of peace.
碑文贍雅可味，字體亦遒媚不俗，世之不乏欣賞者。要於返而證之六經，諸所言帝言天，是何學術？質諸往聖曩所問官問禮，何隔華夷？The inscription is rich and elegant and repays savouring, and the calligraphy is vigorous and graceful and not vulgar; the world will not lack admirers. But the essential thing is to turn back and verify it by the Six Classics: when they speak of *Di* and of *Tian*, what learning is that? Ask the sages of old who once inquired of offices and of rites—what divide did they make between Chinese and barbarian?
Inquiring of offices and rites: Confucius asked about offices of the Lord of Tan (Tan being a small state of the Eastern Yi) and about rites of Lao Dan. "The *Di* and *Tian* of the Six Classics are the Lord of Heaven" is the "accommodation to Confucianism" argument of Ricci's *The True Meaning of the Lord of Heaven* and after.
卽如西賢九萬里外，繼踵遠來，何以捐軀衛道，九死不悔者，古今一轍。而我輩不出戶庭，坐聞正眞學脈，得了生死大事，不可謂全無福緣者，何以尚生疑阻，悖吾孔孟知天事天之訓，而不慙且驚乎？Thus the Western worthies, from ninety thousand *li* away, come one on the heels of another—why do they give their bodies to defend the Way, and die nine times without regret? Past and present are one track. And we, without leaving our own gates, hear sitting down the pure and true line of learning and can settle the great matter of life and death—we cannot be said to be wholly without blessed fortune. Why then still raise doubts and obstacles, going against our Confucius and Mencius's teaching to know Heaven and serve Heaven, and feel neither shame nor alarm?
Ninety thousand *li*: late Ming Catholic texts put Europe "ninety thousand *li*" from China. Know Heaven, serve Heaven: *Mencius*, "Exhausting the Mind" I: "He who knows his nature knows Heaven... to preserve one's mind and nourish one's nature is the way to serve Heaven." "Settle the great matter of life and death" borrows a Buddhist phrase.
且借碑作砭，明叅細駁，卽欲不祛偽歸眞，祈嚮於一尊而不可得。不然者，無論詭正殉魔，自斲生理，政恐蜉蝣生死，相尋共作僇民。迴望房梁公、郭汾陽王，已為絕德，而況其進焉者乎！Let us take the stele as a stone needle, examine it openly and dispute it closely; then, even if one wished not to cast off the false and return to the true, not to pray toward the One Honoured, one could not. Otherwise—to say nothing of turning from the right, dying for the demons and cutting off one's own life—I fear that, living and dying like the mayfly, one after another we shall all become men under guilt. Looking back at Fang, Duke of Liang, and Guo, Prince of Fenyang, theirs was already peerless virtue—how much more those who go further than they!
砭: a stone needle, a figure for admonition. 一尊: the one true Lord. 詭正: departing from the right Way. 政恐: 正恐, "I truly fear." 僇民: men under guilt. Fang, Duke of Liang: Fang Xuanling, enfeoffed Duke of Liang. The last sentence means: if the Tang ministers who protected the church were already of peerless virtue, how much more those who in later times go further and embrace the faith.
天啟五年，歲在旃蒙赤奮若，日纒參初度。
涼菴居士李我存盥手謹識。		Fifth year of Tianqi, the year being *yichou*, the day the sun reached the first degree of the lodge Shen.
Recorded with reverence, hands washed, by Li Wocun, Layman of Liang'an.
旃蒙赤奮若: the year in the *suiyang*/*suiyin* reckoning, i.e. *yichou* (1625). 日纒參初度: the sun at the first degree of the lodge Shen, roughly early to mid June in the Western calendar; the exact date remains to be checked. Layman of Liang'an: Li Zhizao, baptized Leo, styled Liang'an, courtesy name Wocun.

2. The author and how the essay came to be written

Li Zhizao (c. 1565–1630), courtesy names Zhenzhi and Wocun, style Liang'an, was a native of Renhe in Zhejiang (present-day Hangzhou) and passed the palace examination in the twenty-sixth year of Wanli (1598). He was baptized by Matteo Ricci in the thirty-eighth year of Wanli (1610) with the name Leo, and with Xu Guangqi and Yang Tingyun is counted one of the "Three Pillars of Catholicism in the late Ming." His contribution to the history of exchange between China and the West lies chiefly in translating and introducing works of calendrical mathematics, geography and logic; the First Collection of Heavenly Learning he compiled and printed late in life was the first collected series of Western learning in Chinese.

How the essay came about, its opening says plainly: Zhang Gengyu of Qiyang got hold of a rubbing, suspected it had to do with the teaching Ricci brought, and sent it to Li Zhizao in Hangzhou. Some fifteen years later Manuel Dias the Younger recorded Li Zhizao's response in the preface to his Correct Explanation: he "brightened with joy and said, from now on the scholars of China can no longer blame the holy teaching for coming so late." That is the whole purpose of the essay.

Accounts of Zhang Gengyu differ. Some records say he met Ricci in Beijing and heard him preach; some studies hold that he was not himself a Christian. Li Zhizao calls him 同志, "fellow in the Way," which can mean a like-minded friend without necessarily meaning a fellow believer. The Prologue of this book calls him only "a scholar of Qiyang."

3. The argument of the essay

The essay falls into five layers.

First, doctrinal correspondence. From "before all beginnings, without origin" to "an offering once in seven days," Li Zhizao matches the stele's sentences one by one to the teaching Ricci brought: the true Lord, the Trinity, the Incarnation, the Virgin, the strange star, the three kings, the angels, the Ascension, baptism, the cross, the daily office, the Sunday Mass. This layer is the basis of the whole essay, and the matching is broadly right. On these basic articles the Church of the East and the Roman Church had always agreed, which is why the Christian literati of the late Ming recognized them at a glance.

Second, names. The stele's Aluohe and Suodan differ from the Dousi and "great proud chief of demons" then in use; Li Zhizao explains that these are "words of the ancient scriptures of the land of Judaea." The stele's "Daqin" he explains from the Fulin of the Tang History and from Western maps. In this layer the conclusion (from the West) is right, but the route to it is off: Alopen came from Persia.

Third, history. The welcoming of the scriptures in Zhenguan, Gaozong's founding of monasteries, the favours of the four reigns from Xuanzong on, the enlarging of the halls and the works of mercy in Guo Ziyi's time, and finally the carving of the stele. Li Zhizao uses nothing but the stele's own material, changing it in only two places. The attacks of the Shengli and Xiantian years, which the stele says plainly came from Buddhists and Daoists, he turns into the Empress Wu and the Taiping Princess; and Yisi, who on the stele enlarges the halls and gives alms on every side, he never mentions, putting it all under the name of "Fenyang." The first change can be read as a wish not to offend Buddhists and Daoists in print; the second, probably, because Guo Ziyi's fame far exceeded that of a hu monk on a stele, and Guo Ziyi would carry more weight with readers.

Fourth, answering doubts. Someone asks: if these were Catholic clergy, why does the stele call them "monks"? Li Zhizao explains with the old story of Ricci's first years in Guangdong, mixing among the monks until Qu Taisu prompted him to change into the scholar's robe. What this layer really looks to is the situation of the time: from Ricci on, the Jesuits had gone to great pains to make the Chinese understand that they were not Buddhist monks. A stele full of the word "monk" was exactly the kind of thing opponents could make use of, and Li Zhizao means to explain it first.

Fifth, persuasion. The readers of the second half are not insiders but the officials "half in doubt and half in faith, startled at it as a new doctrine." Li Zhizao uses the "accommodation to Confucianism" argument going back to Ricci: the Di and Tian of the Six Classics are the Lord of Heaven; Confucius inquired of offices and rites without dividing Chinese from barbarian; Mencius teaches knowing Heaven and serving Heaven. The stele becomes a "stone needle": if the Son of Heaven of the Tang served it with reverence, and it was already spreading nine hundred and ninety years ago, will you still call it a new doctrine?

This layer must be read against the Nanjing persecution. In the forty-fourth year of Wanli (1616), Shen Que, Vice Minister of Rites at Nanjing, sent up memorial after memorial attacking the Western clergy for "privately studying the calendar" and "stirring up the people's hearts"; the clergy were escorted out of the country, and the mission was at a standstill for years. One of the reasons the opponents used most was that the teaching was of no known origin and unheard of in antiquity. The stele came out of the ground at just this moment, and for the Christian literati it was as good as evidence sent down from heaven. Phrases in the essay such as "the road to heaven would be blocked for good," "as if waiting for an enlightened time," and "suddenly showed its power" express exactly this.

4. What it got right and wrong

The credit for first identifying the inscription as a Christian text belongs to Li Zhizao. Zhang Gengyu only suspected it; Li Zhizao, having read it, made a positive judgement and set out his reasons point by point. The later studies of Jesuits such as Trigault, Semedo and Dias all built on this judgement. Researchers have pointed out that the missionaries' command of classical Chinese was limited at the time, and that in deciphering the inscription Li Zhizao took the leading role.

Equating the Luminous Teaching directly with Catholicism is the essay's greatest limitation. Li Zhizao did not know that the Church of the East and the Roman Church had gone separate ways as early as the fifth century, nor could he read the Syriac on the sides of the stele; so he matched everything on the stele to Catholicism, and what would not match he explained as "words of the ancient scriptures." The limitation was not his alone: the missionaries at first saw it the same way, and though they later caught hints of the truth from the Syriac, they mostly avoided the subject. Dias's Correct Explanation, which expounds the inscription sentence by sentence as Catholic doctrine, can be said to take Li Zhizao's approach as far as it would go. For the history of the Church of the East and the name "Nestorian," see Appendix 6, The Name of Heresy.

Particular misreadings. - Placing Daqin east of Judaea; - Not distinguishing the hymn from the preface; - "All the men are shaven"; - Reading "at high noon he ascended to the true" as an ascension at noon, which does not quite fit the context in the stele; - Speaking of "named the Luminous Teaching" as if it were a name granted by the court.

These are all minor points. The gloss "Jing means great, means shining, means light," on the other hand, is apt, and most later writers on how the Luminous Teaching got its name quote it.

Leaving out Yisi. For this novel, the most suggestive thing is that there is no Yisi in Li Zhizao's essay. The man given the most space on the stele is reduced, in the essay, to the six characters 汾陽重廣法堂, "in the time of Fenyang the halls of the Law were enlarged." This is not an oversight; it is how the essay is written. It sets out to persuade Ming officials, and it is names like Guo Ziyi and Fang Xuanling that carry weight. The hu monk from Balkh on the stele, in the eyes of the first Chinese to read it a thousand years later, is hidden behind the man he followed. This is, in fact, of a piece with the stele's own way of writing him: "he served the Duke as claws and teeth, and the army as eyes and ears."

5. Later scholarship

  • Late Ming and early Qing:
  • Xu Guangqi wrote a Record of the Stele of the Luminous Church, between the fifth and seventh years of Tianqi.
  • Nicolas Trigault translated the inscription into Latin in the fifth year of Tianqi and sent it back to Europe; French, Italian and other translations followed.
  • Johann Schreck (Terrentius) deciphered the Syriac on the stele in the second year of Chongzhen (1629) and showed it privately within the Society.
  • Dias's A Correct Explanation of the Hymn on the Tang Luminous Stele was printed in the seventeenth year of Chongzhen (1644), with Li Zhizao's essay appended at the end.
  • Athanasius Kircher's China Illustrata (1667) published copperplates of the inscription's Chinese and Syriac characters; through it European readers saw a continuous Chinese text for the first time.
  • Doubt and defence: In the eighteenth century Voltaire and others suspected the stele was a Jesuit forgery. From the nineteenth century on, after repeated study of the stone, the Syriac and the Tang sources by scholars of many countries, there is no longer any doubt that it is a genuine Tang object.
  • Date and place of discovery: The two dates, the third and fifth years of Tianqi, still stand side by side, and the two places, west of Xi'an and Zhouzhi, have long been disputed. William Hung's Against the Theory that the Luminous Stele Was Unearthed at Zhouzhi (1932) argues for Xi'an.
  • On Li Zhizao:
  • Fang Hao's A Study of Li Zhizao was the first systematic biographical study.
  • More recently, Zheng Cheng's edited Collected Works of Li Zhizao (Zhonghua Book Company, 2018) arranges the whole in six juan by genre, with After Reading the Luminous Stele in juan 4, "Prefaces, Colophons and Inscriptions." Zheng Cheng has also written Corrections and Additions to the Family and Life of Li Zhizao, adding new material on his examinations and family.
  • A 2002 article, The Late Ming Christian Official Li Zhizao's Study of the "Luminous Stele," is devoted to Li Zhizao's contribution to the study of the stele, arguing that earlier research leaned heavily toward the missionaries and neglected late Ming Confucian scholars such as Li Zhizao.
  • English translation: The website of the Ricci Institute at Boston College publishes an English translation of Dias's Correct Explanation, whose first appendix is an English translation of this essay.

6. Relation to this book

  • Prologue: Semedo's recollection of Li Zhizao—"Unknown in antiquity? It was already there in Zhenguan"—turns the sense of the essay's fifth layer into a character's thoughts. "Nine hundred and ninety years ago" is taken from the original.
  • Book One: Terms such as "offering once in seven" and "praise at the seven hours" are used in this book's text following the stele and this essay.
  • Yisi: Li Zhizao does not mention Yisi; this book makes Yisi the protagonist of Book One. The "hidden behind another" of the last item in section 4 of this appendix is exactly Yisi's situation all his life, on the stele and in the histories; see Appendix 3, The Stele, III: Yisi.
