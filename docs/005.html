Volume Two　Li Guangbi

Chapter 5　The Jingxing Road

In the second month of the fifteenth year of Tianbao, Li Guangbi led more than ten thousand foot and horse of Shuofang and Hedong out through Jingxing into Hebei.

Li Guangbi was a Khitan; his father had been deputy military governor of Shuofang. He had grown up in the army, and kept his troops under the strictest discipline; the men in camp feared him more than they feared Guo Ziyi. When he entered Hebei, Changshan had just been lost and won back, and once won back was about to be lost again. The rebel general Shi Siming, with more than twenty thousand horse, was turning back from Raoyang to bear down on it. Li Guangbi entered the city of Changshan and shut the gates, and Shi Siming arrived after him and ringed the city so close that not a drop of water could get through.

The siege lasted more than forty days.

In the west, Guo Ziyi was putting his main army in order and at the same time sending messengers to Changshan. Three times he sent letters. The first messenger died on the way; the second was never heard of again; the third got through, and brought back one sentence from Li Guangbi: the city was nearly out of arrows.

At the end of the third month Guo Ziyi was to send another letter. This time he sent twelve riders.

Their leader was Tie Wanquan of the scouts' battalion—the same Old Iron who had backed Yisi to win a bout on the parade ground and spent his winnings treating the men to mutton.

Old Iron had been a scout in the Hedong army for twenty years. He loved to laugh, and when he laughed it was loud enough to be heard two tents away; but everyone in camp knew that laugh as Old Iron might, he never did a sloppy piece of work. He had travelled the Jingxing road at least ten times. Which stretch had a spring beside it, which pass was good for hiding men, under which stone lay last year's horse dung—he could tell you with his eyes shut.

Guo Ziyi called him and Yisi into his tent together.

"There are two copies of the letter," Guo Ziyi said. "Old Iron keeps them. If you split up on the road, each party carries one. What's in the letter, you don't need to know. But every one of you twelve is to remember one sentence for me."

He told them the sentence.

"I come out through Jingxing in the fourth month."

Old Iron said it after him, and so did Yisi.

"If the letters are lost," said Guo Ziyi, "and a man gets through, the words get through. You're twelve. If one of you gets there, that counts as getting there."

Old Iron said, "Orders received."

Out of the tent, Old Iron clapped Yisi on the shoulder and laughed. "Foreign master, what we said last time—you look your way and I'll look mine—here we are, together at last. I know Jingxing like my own hand. In the hills, you listen to me; anything to do with Hu people, I'll listen to you. When we get to Changshan, the mutton's on me again."

Yisi said, "Good. I still owe you for the last mutton."

Old Iron waved a hand. "Owe it. Owing's good. If you owe it, you have to come back alive to pay."

The twelve riders set out from Taiyuan, rode east two days, and reached the western mouth of Jingxing.

Jingxing is a crack in the Taihang mountains: mountains on both sides and a narrow road between, in places no wider than two carts, in places running right beside the river. From the western mouth there were two ways east. One was the official road, following the valley—wide, level, fast, three days to the eastern mouth; but at the eastern mouth stood the Tumen Pass, and the Tumen Pass was now in rebel hands. The other was a track through the hills, swinging round northeast along the Mianman River, over two ridges and then down along the riverbed; it would get round Tumen and come at Changshan from the north. But it was steep, narrow and slow, five or six days' going, without a soul along the way, and if you were caught in a ravine, both ways out were death.

Two roads; either could get you through, and either could get you killed.

Old Iron reined in at the western mouth and looked east a long time, and said: "We split up."

His second, Feng Shouyi, nodded. Feng Shouyi was the ninth son of his family, and the camp called him Feng Nine. He had followed Old Iron eight years; he talked little and thought carefully, and for every sentence Old Iron spoke he could think of three. He knew what Old Iron meant. Twelve men crowded on one road, if they met the rebels, would all be taken in one cast of the net. Split up, and one party could go with banners flying and draw the rebels off, while the other slipped through quietly, and one of the two would get there.

"I'll take the big road," said Old Iron.

Feng Shouyi said, "No."

Old Iron glared at him.

"The big road's the bait," said Feng Shouyi. "Bait gets eaten. You're the leader. If you get eaten, who leads the rest? The little road is the road the letter goes by. You should take the little road."

"The little road's got the foreign master looking at it."

"Then you should take it all the more," said Feng Shouyi. "You two road-lookers—one looks at the hills, one looks at the people."

The two of them argued on horseback a long time, and neither could move the other. The ten soldiers with them did not dare put a word in, but sat their horses and watched. Yisi did not speak either.

In the end Old Iron laughed, and fished a Kaiyuan coin out of his coat and weighed it in his palm.

"Then let Heaven decide," he said. "Characters up, I take the big road. Back up, I take the little road."

Feng Shouyi looked at the coin and said nothing, which meant yes.

Old Iron flicked the coin up into the air. It turned over several times and fell on the frozen ground in front of his horse, bounced once, and lay flat. Back up: the crescent-shaped mark on its back glinted once in the sun.

Old Iron stared at the coin a long time and swore. He jumped down from his horse, picked it up, wiped it on the front of his coat, and pressed it into Feng Shouyi's hand.

"Keep it," he said. "Give it back when you get back."

Feng Shouyi put the coin away inside his coat. Then Old Iron took two oilcloth packets from his own coat and pressed one of them into Feng Shouyi's hands too, and put the other back inside his coat and pressed it with his hand.

Old Iron took Feng Shouyi aside and gave him instructions for a while. Yisi was not far off and heard some of it. Ride by day, Old Iron said, with your banner out, so they see you from a long way off; at night, light more fires, so the rebels think a big party has come; trample the hoofprints about, so nobody can count that you're only six; if you meet the rebels, don't stay to fight, fall back, draw them west, the further the better; and hold out to the fifth day, and then, whatever happens, fall back.

Feng Shouyi nodded at every line.

"If you don't come back," said Old Iron, "I'll never forgive you."

Feng Shouyi said: "Same for you."

The two parties separated at the western mouth of Jingxing. Feng Shouyi took five men under the banner of the Shuofang army and went east along the official road, and the sound of their hooves rang in the valley a long time. Old Iron took Yisi and four others and turned off onto a goat track by the Mianman River.

The first two days on the little road, nothing happened.

It was cold in the hills; in the shady places the snow had not yet melted and creaked under the horses' hooves. The six of them went in single file, slowly. Old Iron rode first, Yisi second. Old Iron's mouth never rested the whole way. Now he talked about things in the Hedong army, now about his son, and in the middle of it he would burst out laughing himself, and the laugh went knocking about the valley and knocked the snow off the trees.

Of the four with them, one was Lu Yuanliang, seventeen, the thirteenth of his family, whom everyone called Thirteen. It was his first errand of this kind, and his face was drawn tight; every time Old Iron laughed he started and looked up at the hills on either side. There was an old soldier called Ma Six who used a long spear and talked even more than Old Iron, and the two of them bickered all the way. Of the other two, one was called Stone, a bowman; one was a groom named Cao Chounu, whom the camp called Chounu, "the ugly one," and he really was ugly, with a face that looked as if someone had kneaded it and never smoothed it out. He seldom talked, and kept close only to the horses.

The first night they rested under a cliff out of the wind. They did not dare light a fire, and huddled together gnawing dry flatbread. As he gnawed, Old Iron got onto his son again. The boy was called Lock, he said, his proper name Tie Qianshan, twenty years old, a head taller than his father, and a fine hand with the crossbow.

Yisi asked: "Which camp is he in?"

"With Governor Li, among the Hedong men," said Old Iron. "Right there in the city of Changshan."

"You're here with Lord Guo, he's over there with Governor Li, father and son don't see each other once a year," said Yisi. "Why not have him follow you?"

"I sent him over there myself." Old Iron broke a flatbread in two, pushed one half into his mouth, and chewed a long time before he spoke. "Father and son in one camp—when it comes to fighting, either you watch me die or I watch you die, or the two of us die in the same pit. I don't want to watch that, and I don't want him to have to." He swallowed the bread. "Besides, Lord Guo's a good man, there's no question. But if you're talking about who can fight, it's Governor Li. Following him, the boy'll live a few years longer."

When he had said it he gave a couple of chuckles himself, as though he had got the better end of some bargain. Yisi did not laugh.

In the afternoon of the second day, Old Iron, riding in front, suddenly reined in.

He dismounted, squatted by the track, and looked at a pile of horse dung there. He pinched some up, rubbed it apart, and held it under his nose.

"Still warm," he said. "Less than half an hour." He looked up at the lie of the hills on either side. "Into the hollow. Off your horses. Hold their muzzles."

The six of them slipped into a stand of pines, dismounted, and each put his arms round his horse's head and held its nose.

After a while a troop of horsemen passed along the ridge opposite, a dozen or so, in no great hurry. Someone on horseback was talking, in a foreign tongue, and the wind brought the voice across the valley in pieces. Old Iron turned his head toward Yisi. Yisi listened with his head cocked for a while, and said under his breath: "Sogdian, with a few words of Turkic in it. Hu soldiers, not Han."

The horsemen were long past before Old Iron let go of his horse's muzzle, and chuckled. "Hu soldiers or Han soldiers, the dung's warm either way."

Toward evening on the third day they came to a village.

It lay on the lee side of a ridge, a dozen households, the houses all of piled stone. The people had long since fled; the doors stood open, and some had been taken off their hinges and burned. It was nearly dark, all six of them were numb with cold in hand and foot, the horses were hungry, and their rations were almost gone. In the middle of the village there was a little shrine to the god of the soil, its door half closed.

Old Iron did not hurry inside.

He had them wait at the edge of the village and went round leading his horse, looking at one house after another. He looked at the stoves: the stoves were cold, the ash old. He looked at the ground: the footprints were all old, crusted with ice. He looked at the water jars: dry, with a white crust of alkali in the bottom. He walked a circle round the village too, to see whether there were any freshly trodden paths on the slopes outside, any horse dung. Which two houses had courtyard walls close together, which lane led out of the village—he took that in as well along the way.

Nothing.

He came back to the edge of the village, but did not say they would rest in the shrine. He stood there with his eyes narrowed, looking into the village a long time. The horses had been restless since they came in; they kept snorting and pawing the frozen ground.

"This village is wrong," said Old Iron.

"Wrong how?" asked Ma Six.

Old Iron could not say. He looked into the village again and shook his head. "Can't say. It's just wrong. We don't rest here tonight. Ten more li, find a hollow."

Nobody moved. Thirteen's lips were purple with cold, his teeth chattering as he said: "Chief, another ten li and it'll be pitch dark, and the horses can't go any further." Ma Six, who liked nothing better than to bicker with Old Iron, did not bicker this time; he only pulled a long face and said: "You looked at every house yourself. The stoves are cold, the footprints are old, there's not one fresh pile of dung. What else is wrong? The horses snorting—that's hunger."

Yisi thought Ma Six had the right of it too. He had ridden behind Old Iron three days and knew how carefully he looked; that round he had just made—if Yisi had made it himself, he would not have seen any more. He said: "Brother Iron, you've looked at everything. Men and horses can't hold out. Rest one night, and go before light."

"No," said Old Iron. There was no laugh on his face. "Twenty years I've been on this road. A wrong you can't name is deadlier than a wrong you can."

They stood at the edge of the village at a deadlock. The light faded bit by bit.

Yisi looked at Old Iron a while and said: "Then I'll go into the shrine and look. When I've looked, if you still say no, we go."

He led his horse into the shrine. It was not large. The clay image on the altar was missing an arm, and in a corner lay a heap of rotten straw. He stood in the shrine a while, and suddenly caught a smell. It was very faint, so faint it was almost not there, mixed in with the mustiness of rotten straw and earth, a thread here and a thread there, like the fragrance some wood leaves after it has burned.

He knew that smell. It was Parthian incense—benzoin.

He walked slowly round the shrine, went behind the altar, squatted down, and saw that behind the base of the clay image there was a hole in the wall, its mouth stopped with a stone. He moved the stone. Inside was a little pottery jar, with a few cypress twigs pressed over its mouth. He brushed the twigs aside. In the jar was a layer of ash, and under the ash, a spot of red.

Live coals. Not out.

Yisi put the cypress twigs back, put the stone back in the hole, walked quickly out of the shrine to Old Iron's side, and said low: "You were right. We can't stay."

Old Iron looked at him.

"They don't light fires and cook here," said Yisi. "They cook somewhere else and leave the fire here. There's a jar of fire behind the altar, with cypress over its mouth, and a little smell of benzoin in the room. They're fire-worshipping Hu soldiers. Fire is their god; it mustn't go out. Rather than let it die they'll risk being seen keeping it. They've gone out, and after dark they'll be back."

Every face changed. Old Iron asked nothing, and swung up onto his horse.

"Mount," he said, not loudly. "Not a sound."

They had only just mounted when a whistle sounded from the slope behind the village.

Then hoofbeats, from both ends of the village at once, a whole drumming of them. In the dusk a dozen riders burst out from behind the stone walls at the north end of the village, and a dozen more from the lane at the south end. Some held torches, some had bows drawn. The leading few had white cloth wound round their heads, and were shouting in a foreign tongue.

"Follow me!" Old Iron roared.

He spurred straight off. He did not charge the lane; there were men blocking the lane. He charged a narrow gap between the courtyard walls of two houses at the east end of the village—the gap he had noted as he went from house to house—and on the far side of it lay a sloping field, and below the field a dry gully. As he charged into the gap the horse's flanks almost scraped the stone walls on either side, and the men behind followed one after another, Yisi last.

Arrows came after them and stuck in the stone walls with a smack, smack.

Out of the gap, down the slope, into the dry gully, whose bed was broken stone that made the horses' hooves slip. The pursuers came down the slope after them. Yisi, at the back, turned in the saddle, took his bow, nocked and shot. He shot three arrows, all at the riders in the lead. One man went over off his horse; one clutched his shoulder and slumped over his horse's neck; a horse was hit and went round in circles on the slope, screaming. The dusk was too thick; he could not see what became of those men afterward.

The pursuit faltered for a moment. In that moment they plunged into a jumble of boulders at the end of the gully, and it was full dark.

Chounu had not come with them.

Stone said that as they burst out of the gap, Chounu's horse had taken an arrow in the neck and gone down and pinned him under it. Stone had looked back once and seen several men with torches close round him.

Six had become five.

That night they hid among the boulders all night. They did not dare light a fire, and did not dare sleep. Old Iron sat on a rock a long time without speaking. Later he said one thing, his voice hoarse: "Twenty years I've been in Jingxing. I couldn't see that jar of fire."

"Neither could I," said Yisi. "You felt it first. If you'd listened to us, all six of us would be lying in that shrine now."

Old Iron did not answer. After a while he said: "Chounu was a man I picked."

Yisi did not know what to say to that, so he said nothing. The two of them sat on the rock until daylight.

The next two days the road got harder and harder.

They had thought there would be few rebels on the little road, but the further east they went, the more rebel patrols there were. There were men on the ridges and men by the river, and twice, just after they had got round a pass, they saw on the snow beyond it fresh hoofprints, close-packed, as if a whole troop had been going back and forth there.

Old Iron lay on a ridge looking for a long time, and when he came down he said to them: "The rebels guessed."

Ma Six asked what they had guessed.

"That we'd split up," said Old Iron. "There's men waiting on the big road, and men waiting on the little road. They've got an Old Iron of their own over there."

When he had said it he gave a great laugh. When he had finished laughing his face was grim. Yisi knew what he was thinking. He was thinking of Feng Shouyi.

On the afternoon of the fifth day, in a pass, they ran head-on into a small troop of rebels. There were only five or six riders, but they could not fight them; once they fought, the shouting and the screaming of horses would bring the others from far off. Old Iron led them back at a run into a side gully. Stone was last, and held the pursuers off a while, and shot two men down; and when the others came out the far end of the side gully, Stone did not come out.

Five had become four.

Early on the sixth day they reached the bank of the Mianman River.

The river was not wide, but the ice had not yet melted through. On the far side was level ground; across the river, one more day east, was the city of Changshan. On the ridge behind them, just then, a line of riders appeared, a dozen or more, coming down toward them at a charge.

There was no other way. They had to cross.

Old Iron reined in at the bank, looked once upstream and once down, and pointed to a stretch of river. "This bit's shallow. In summer a horse can wade it. If we cross, it's here."

Yisi looked at the ice where he pointed. Snow lay on it, and it looked level all over; where it was thick and where thin beneath, nobody could say. But it was not the snow he was looking at; it was the colour of the ice showing through the snow. In some places it was whitish, in some places bluish. The white was old ice, frozen all winter, thick; the blue was new ice, melted a few days ago and frozen again, thin. On the river outside Balkh, every spring, he and his brother had crossed by looking at the ice just like this.

"It's shallow, but the middle part is new ice," said Yisi. "I'll go first. Follow my horse's hoofprints, and don't put a foot wrong."

He rode out onto the ice. He did not go fast. Now left, now right, he made a winding line across the ice. Old Iron followed him, and Ma Six and Thirteen followed Old Iron, each treading in the last one's hoofprints. The ice cracked under the horses' hooves, like someone breaking a dry flatbread. Thirteen was so frightened he shut his eyes, and Ma Six cursed him from behind, cursed him in a voice that shook.

They got across.

The pursuers reached the bank and did not stop. They charged straight out onto the ice. They did not follow the hoofprints; they took whatever way was shortest. The two in front, in iron armour on big horses, reached the bluish stretch in the middle of the river, and the ice gave a crack and split open.

Two horses and their riders went down into the hole together. The one behind could not rein in in time and slid in after them. The rest milled about on the far side, and did not dare set foot on the ice again.

Old Iron stood on the near bank looking back a while, and roared with laughter, laughed till the tears came. He turned his head and held up a thumb to Yisi.

"You foreign hawk!" he said. "That's what I'll call you from now on."

That afternoon they were only the better part of a day's ride from Changshan.

They did not finish it.

Where the river turned east there was a stretch of riverbed, and on the bank lay a great blue-grey boulder as big as half a room, like an ox lying down. They had just got round below the boulder when a troop of stray horsemen appeared on the earth bank to the north, seven or eight of them, as if they had been waiting there. When they saw them they whistled and came charging down.

Old Iron looked back at his horse. It was lathered all over, white foam at its mouth, its legs trembling. The others were the same.

"They can't run any more," said Old Iron. He did not panic. His eyes swept round: over the great boulder, over the soft bog of mud south of it. Beyond the bog was the river, its ice long since broken up, only a ring of rotten ice along the bank. Between the boulder and the bog there was only a strip of dry ground about ten feet wide.

"Everybody off!" Old Iron shouted. "Backs to the rock!"

The four of them jumped down, drove the horses round behind the boulder, and stood with their backs against the stone. To the north was the rock, to the south the bog; to get at them the horsemen could only come in along that ten-foot strip of dry ground from the east end or the west, two horses at a time at most.

Old Iron gave each man his place, fast and short.

"Hawk, you're the only one with a shield. You hold the gap at the east end. Six, you and that spear stand behind him—go for the horses, don't let a horse barge in. Thirteen, take that bow up on top of the rock—shoot the horses, and save your arrows. I'll hold the west end."

Ma Six said he would and took his place behind Yisi, gripping his long spear. Thirteen scrambled up the boulder on hands and feet, lay flat on the top, and stuck his arrows one by one into the cracks of the stone beside him; his hands shook so that it took him two tries to make them stand.

Yisi stood in the gap at the east end with his shield up. He glanced at Old Iron. Old Iron stood at the west end, his sword already drawn, and bared his teeth at him, without a sound of laughter.

When the horsemen came up, at first they took no account of them. Four men on foot with their backs to a rock, their horses blown—plainly the leavings of some band, run to ground. Their leader shouted something, and two riders went into the gap at the east end ahead of the rest.

The first was halfway in when an arrow came down from the top of the boulder and went into his horse's neck; the horse swerved and threw him. The second reached Yisi, and Ma Six's long spear went out over Yisi's shoulder and took the horse square in the chest; the horse reared and pitched its rider to the ground.

The man rolled as he landed, rolled right to Yisi's feet, came up and lunged, his sword cutting at Yisi's legs.

What happened next, Yisi could not remember whole. He remembered only that he did not think. The shield pressed down and pinned the sword, and the mace in his right hand came down after it, came down on the man's head. On the bank of the Yellow River, on the parade ground, he had broken men's wrists, broken the backs of men's hands, and every time, at that last half-inch, his hand had held back of itself. This time it did not hold back. He heard only a sound like a clay jar breaking. The man pitched forward onto his face at Yisi's feet and did not move.

At the west end a rider had come in too. Old Iron did not step back. He turned sideways to let the horse's head go by, cut at the horse's legs, and the horse went down on its knees, and Old Iron followed up with another cut.

Only then did the horsemen rein in.

They did not charge the gaps any more. They circled outside, shooting in as they circled, shouting, trying to draw them out. The arrows struck the boulder with a clatter and a clink; one went past Ma Six's ear and stuck in the rock.

On top of the boulder, Thirteen had only five or six arrows left. Old Iron shouted up to him: "Thirteen! Don't waste them. Wait till they come into the gap!"

Yisi looked at the gap at the east end. The first horse to be shot down lay not far outside it, still kicking. He slung his shield onto his back, ran out bent double, seized the horse's reins, and he and Ma Six dragged and hauled the dying horse until it lay across the middle of the gap.

The gap was half as wide now. Any horse coming in would first have to jump a dead horse.

While they were dragging the horse back, one of the riders came round over the bog to the south. The bog was soft; his horse sank to the knees, so he jumped off and waded through the mud and began climbing the boulder, his sword going for Thirteen where he lay on top.

Thirteen turned and shot, and missed. The man already had hold of the rim of the rock, his sword raised.

Yisi was right below. He had no time to think. He dropped the reins, leapt across in one stride, and brought the mace down on the hand gripping the rim. The man screamed and slid down off the rock into the mud, turned over and started to get up again, and Yisi struck again, and the mace came down on his temple.

As the man went down he cried out. It was in a foreign tongue, and Yisi understood it.

The man had cried out for his mother.

Yisi was still standing there, the mace still raised. On top of the boulder Thirteen gave a cry, tilted sideways, and rolled off the rock. An arrow was in his back below the shoulder, the shaft still quivering.

The horsemen charged once more.

This time from the west end. Ma Six had gone to pull Thirteen clear, and at the west end there was only Old Iron, and three riders bore down on him at once. Old Iron cut down the one in front; the second one's spear was on him, and Old Iron caught the shaft in his fist and was dragged forward several steps by the horse, and the point went into his belly.

He did not fall. With one hand he held the spear shaft, and with the other his sword was still cutting down, and it cut into the thigh of the man on the horse. The man flinched with the pain and let go of the spear, and Old Iron went over backward with it, against the rock.

The third came on. Ma Six left Thirteen and threw himself at it, his spear crosswise, and blocked it, and took an arrow in his own thigh, and went down on one knee, the spear still crosswise.

When Yisi reached the west end, the third rider had already turned and gone.

The one Old Iron had cut down was their leader.

The remaining horsemen gathered in the distance and looked this way a while. Their leader was dead; there were dead horses lying across the gaps, and four or five of their comrades lying under the rock. They had only come to stop a few stragglers carrying letters; it was not worth throwing more lives against this rock. Someone pointed west and shouted something, as if to say more men were coming from that way. They turned their horses and went off north.

There was nothing to the west.

The riverbed went quiet.

Yisi knelt in the mud, breathing hard. He looked down at himself: he was covered in blood and could not tell which was his own and which was other men's. Below his left ribs it burned; at some point a spear point had opened a gash there. His right shoulder had taken a cut too; the leather armour had stopped some of it, but the blade had still gone into the flesh, and the blood was still seeping. He tore a strip from the front of his robe, held one end in his teeth, and tied it tight anyhow.

Ma Six sat on the ground with an arrow in his thigh, his face white as paper, cursing through his teeth. Thirteen lay face down beside him. The arrow in his back was in very deep, and he had fainted, but he was still breathing; his chest rose and fell.

Old Iron leaned against the rock with both hands pressed over his belly, and the blood kept welling out between his fingers. Yisi crawled over and tried to press on the wound, and Old Iron pushed his hands away.

"Can't hold it in now," said Old Iron. His voice was very soft, but there was still a little laugh in it.

Yisi knelt beside him and said nothing.

Old Iron took the oilcloth-wrapped letter out of his coat and pushed it inside Yisi's coat, and pressed it with his hand, pressed hard.

"Remember," he said. "When you get into Changshan, go to Governor Li's camp and find Lock. Tie Qianshan. When you see him, tell him his pa didn't shame him."

"I'll remember."

"Hawk, those eyes of yours," said Old Iron. "Watch him for me after this. He's like me. Laughs like a fool, no sense in his head."

Yisi nodded.

Old Iron's eyes moved aside, to Ma Six and Thirteen, and back. "These two…"

"When I've delivered the letter, I'll bring men back for them," said Yisi.

Old Iron said, "Mm." He took two breaths, and suddenly bared his teeth again. "Foreign master, the mutton… I'll never get to stand you that now. Damn it all, that pot of mutton the other day—it smelled so good."

When he had said it he laughed first himself, laughed twice, and began to cough, and coughed up a mouthful of blood.

He looked at Yisi once more, as if he wanted to say something else. His mouth moved, and nothing came out.

Yisi knelt beside him a long time. Then he reached out and closed Old Iron's eyes. He put his right hand on his breast, bowed his head, and said a passage from the monastery's prayers.

He got up and went to the two men he had killed. The first one's skull was broken, his face buried in the sand. The second, the one who had come round over the bog, lay on his back: a Hu of twenty or so, the beard on his face still soft. Yisi knelt beside him, said a passage for him too, and closed his eyes.

He knelt there a good while before he understood: these two men, he had killed.

The sun had tilted west. Yisi could not linger here.

On the side of the boulder that faced the river there was a cleft that ran back into the rock, overhung with stone, out of the wind, and not visible from the earth bank to the north. Yisi helped Ma Six over first, then carried Thirteen over on his back. Thirteen was not heavy, but as soon as Yisi's right shoulder took the strain the wound burst open, and blood ran down his arm, and when he set Thirteen down he went down on his knees himself and knelt a good while before he could get up.

He broke off the shaft of the arrow in Thirteen's back, leaving the head in the flesh, not daring to pull it out, and bound strips of cloth tight round the wound. The one in Ma Six's leg Ma Six broke off himself, gritting his teeth, cursing a string of curses. Yisi took off his own water skin and Old Iron's and put them by their hands, emptied out all the rations that were left, and laid Ma Six's long spear and Old Iron's sword where Ma Six could reach them. Of the four horses, one was still unhurt. He led it outside the cleft and tied it to a clump of red willow.

"Six," said Yisi, "if Thirteen wakes, make him drink, but not too much at once. No fire at night. This horse is for you two. Ride it only if you really have to run."

Ma Six leaned against the rock, his face a frightening white, and still pulled the corners of his mouth up. "Go on. Old Iron said you read a road true. Don't go losing the road back."

On a flat stone outside the cleft, Yisi piled three stones into a mark, one on top of another, so that unless you looked carefully you would take them for loose stones that had always been on the riverbed. He looked back once more at Old Iron. Old Iron was still leaning against the other side of the boulder; Yisi could not drag him over. He took off Old Iron's cloak and laid it over Old Iron's face.

Then he mounted Old Iron's horse and rode east.

The city of Changshan was ringed like an iron bucket.

Yisi reached the walls in the middle of the night. He lay half the night in a tumbledown graveyard outside the city, waited till the rebel patrols changed, and only then crept up to the foot of the wall. The defenders on the wall very nearly shot him dead. He held up the bronze tally Guo Ziyi had given him and shouted, his voice cracked: "A letter from Lord Guo! For Governor Li!"

A basket was let down from the wall.

When he climbed into the basket his hands could no longer grip the rope. The defenders hauled him up onto the wall and lifted him out of the basket, a tangle of hands. The torchlight fell on his face, and someone gasped.

He took the oilcloth packet from inside his coat and held it out to the officer nearest him. His hands shook so badly the packet almost fell. The officer caught it.

"Lord Guo says," said Yisi, "he comes out through Jingxing in the fourth month."

The officer turned to go, and Yisi caught hold of his sleeve.

"West of the city," said Yisi. "Where the Mianman River turns east. On the riverbed, a great blue-grey boulder like an ox's back. In the cleft behind the rock, on the river side, there are two men. One with an arrow in his leg, one with an arrow in his back. Outside the cleft, on a flat stone, three stones piled one on another. That's the mark."

He said it all in one breath, very slowly, one word at a time, and when he had finished, he said it all again.

The officer looked at him, nodded, and had the man beside him write it down.

He heard the officer turn and say something to the man beside him. He heard the man answer: "Another one. One got in by the big road too, the day before yesterday."

He wanted to ask who it was who had got in, but the words would not come out. His eyes went black, and he fell.
