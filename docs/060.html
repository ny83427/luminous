Volume Nine　The Translation Hall

Chapter 60　Jingjing

In the thirteenth year of Yuanhe, Jingjing was seventy-two. At the opening of spring that year his legs went first: getting up in the morning, his two legs felt borrowed, not much inclined to obey; he had to sit holding the edge of the bed and wait for them to acknowledge their master before he could stand, and when he stood he walked slowly; from the back courtyard to the front hall, where others took twenty steps, he took forty. Jingfu whittled him a stick, from an old branch sawn off the jujube tree in the back courtyard, whittled very smooth, with a strip of cloth wound round where the hand held it. On his stick he still rose every day before the wooden clapper, stood a while in the middle of the courtyard, told his knotted cord once through, went into the scripture room to read a passage, and wrote a character on that old board from Xizhou; still every morning went to the hall to lead morning office, in the afternoon went to sit a while in the scripture room, and at dark went to the gate to light the lamp.

The lamp at the gate stood on the stone block to the left of the gate—a bronze lamp-dish half the size of a palm, its wick twisted from hemp. The monastery had lit this lamp for decades: at the end of Tianbao the old deacon lit it; in the year of the fall it went out; in the second year of Zhide his Pa set it on the steps; in the first year of Qianyuan Yonan moved it onto the stone block, and from then on it had stayed on the stone block. Jingjing first lifted the lamp, tipped out last night's burnt dregs, wiped it clean with a cloth, ladled a spoon of new oil from the oil jar into it, drew out a little length of wick, trimmed it even with the scissors, then struck the flint, lit it, and set it back. He had done this thirty-four years; while his Pa lived his Pa did it; when his Pa was gone he did it; once the whole round took no longer than a cup of tea; now it took half a meal. He could no longer bend; the stone block was low, and every time he had to hold the doorframe and slowly squat down, and once down, hold the doorframe again and slowly rise. Jingfu stood by watching, watched his hands shake, shake so that the flint was struck three times before it caught, and put out his hand and said: Master, let me. Jingjing said: no need. So Jingfu drew his hand back, and when the lamp was lit helped him back.

The character on his board that year was the same for months. Lingbao, coming into the scripture room in the morning, saw the board leaning by the table, and on it the character xin, heart; the next day it was still that character, and the third day still. Lingbao did not ask. That character was the only Tang character Elder Yonan could write in all his life; a woman in the back lane of a cookshop in the West Market had taught it him—the woman whom afterwards both brothers had called Ma.

That summer was especially hot; for months Chang'an had no soaking rain; the wells ran shallow; the leaves of the locust trees curled at the edges in the sun; by day the monastery's people hid in the hall, and at night moved out to sleep in the courtyard; Jingfu slept under the colonnade, snoring, the snores carrying from one end of the colonnade to the other. Jingjing could not sleep; he lay in his room under a thin quilt; others were hot, he was not; his body was always cool, his hands and feet especially, even in summer. One day at the hour of wu he asked Jingfu to fetch him a bowl of water. Jingfu went to the stove to ladle hot water, and Jingjing called him back and said: from the well. Jingfu stopped and turned his head and said: Master—cold water. Jingjing said he knew it was cold water. Jingfu stood there; he had been in this monastery since he was five, had heard for a dozen-odd years about Master's fear of cold water; he himself had got up at midnight to drink well water and been caught by Master who knew how many times, and Master had never scolded him, only every time knit his brows and watched him drink, as if watching someone stuff a live coal into his mouth. Jingjing said: go. Jingfu went to the well under the locust tree, wound up a bucket on the windlass, ladled a bowl, and carried it back; at once a fine layer of beads hung on the outside of the bowl.

Jingjing held the bowl in both hands. He thought of Lingzhou, the winter he was ten, when he had secretly drunk a ladle of cold water at night and made a fool of himself at table the next day; thought of the road to the Patriarchate, his Pa warning him not to drink cold water on the road, and Uncle Duan beside them laughing out loud; thought of the gate of Xizhou, Uncle Duan on horseback turning his head to say: don't drink cold water; thought of the willows by the Kaiyuan Gate, his Pa not waving, saying only: don't drink cold water; thought of Xu Jing stuffing a water-skin into his arms, reciting the rule; thought of Shiyiniang coming out from behind the Pei family's spirit seat with hot water, saying: you can't drink it cold. His whole life, people had been warning him. He brought the bowl to his lips and drank a mouthful; cold—cold so that the roots of his teeth ached, cold all the way down into his belly; he paused, and drank another mouthful, and another, drank the whole bowl, and handed the empty bowl to Jingfu. Jingfu took the bowl and looked at him, waiting; Jingjing waited too. His stomach did not rumble. By evening, it still had not. Jingjing leaned against the wall and slowly smiled; when he smiled he made no sound, and many lines crowded out at the corners of his eyes, and he said: afraid of it all my life. That night he slept very well.

In autumn he began to hand things over. Not all at once: today he thought of one thing and said one thing; tomorrow he thought of one thing and said another. Lingbao and Jingfu stayed beside him; for each thing he said, Lingbao wrote one down.

First the scriptures. Which bark-skin cases in the scripture room held the Syriac scriptures the Patriarchate had sent, which cabinets held the Tang-language scriptures he had translated, which were finished, which half done, where that list of scriptures was kept—and that last line of the list, "not yet translated," he had Lingbao read to Jingfu. He said: the ones not finished, if you can't translate them, leave them for now; when there's someone who can, let them be translated. Jingfu asked: what if there's never anyone who can? Jingjing said: then leave them for ever; left, they won't spoil. He had Lingbao carry out the Tang scriptures in the scripture room roll by roll, and turn them for Jingfu to see the little dots beside them, and said: these dots—in time, if you find characters of your own, change them; and when you change one, rub out its dot; if you can't find any, leave them; left, those who come after will see and know this was borrowed.

Then those few sheets of paper. The one Luo Haoxin had copied long ago, the paper gone yellow through, its edges so brittle that a touch shed flakes; and pressed beside it the little slip he had copied himself, "Jingjing ought to transmit the teaching of Mishihe," where in copying he had written the character shi with the wrong one. He had Lingbao put these two sheets in the scripture cabinet, with Samaan's nine account books, and said: what reviles us is a document too—Elder Samaan said that. Then Official Pei's little register: the front half in Official Pei's upright regular hand, the back half in his own hand slanting right, and a few pages after that in Lingbao's hand. He told Lingbao to go on writing it, and when he had finished, to hand it to Jingfu.

Then the three bowls in the cupboard. He had Jingfu open the cupboard and take the bowls out one by one and set them on the table: his own, with a small chip knocked out of the rim; his Pa's, which Jingfu now used every day; and one more, with a notch in its mouth, holding a pinch of earth. He pointed to the bowl of earth and told once more the story of Du De: that Du De was from Duling in Jingzhao, joined the army at sixteen, changed his Pa's dressings twenty days at Changshan, was good at kickball, held the rear in Hebei, said he'd hold for a cup of tea and held for a cup of tea, and when he died his sword-hilt and his hand were glued together with blood and could not be prised apart, and they buried him with the sword. He said all this his Pa had told him; he had never seen Du De. He said: this bowl—don't tip out the earth, and don't use it for rice. Jingfu nodded, and asked: then Master's own? Jingjing looked at his own bowl, at the little chip in its rim—knocked out when he was in his twenties; after it was chipped he always drank his broth from the other side—and said: you see to it.

Then the matter of the leadership. He told Lingbao that after he died, the leadership of the monastery would go to Jingfu. Lingbao was fifty-one that year; David had laid hands on him; he was priest; the monastery's accounts and keys were in his hands; by age and by standing it should have been his. Lingbao heard it without the least reluctance, and said that if Brother decided so, he'd thought of it long ago himself: he kept the accounts and the scripture room; let Jingfu lead; Jingfu was young, his Tang was good, the people of the city knew him; later, in dealing with the government and with the ward, a young one would be useful. He said: when Pa handed the charity over to Brother, he said, go along with him and learn the road; he too would go along with Jingfu, and learn the road.

Jingfu would not. He was twenty-three that year; he could lead evening office, could preach, and his Tang was better than anyone's in the monastery; but he knelt before Jingjing's bed and said he could not. He said he had studied Syriac a dozen-odd years and still could not do it well; he couldn't chant the scriptures properly; of what was sung at morning and evening office, half was sounds he made up himself; the monastery's old people all knew and all held back their laughter; he said the people of the south of the city still said to this day that the monastery's people were the Son of Heaven's monks; make a Tang mouth the leader as well, and the south of the city would say this monastery wasn't even hu any more; he said too that Uncle Lingbao was a priest, hands laid on by Elder David, while he had not even had hands laid on him as deacon—by what right? As he spoke the rims of his eyes went red, and he said: Master, if you go, he'll keep this monastery, keep that lamp; but the leadership, he can't do it.

Jingjing did not try to persuade him. He lay in bed looking at this child, and thought of fifty years before, when his Pa came back from Hezhong and in the side courtyard handed over to him the charity and the attending at the government offices, and he too had said he could not, and his Pa had said: go along with him, learn the road. He thought: Pa did not try to persuade him then, and he would not try today. He said: then let it be this way: from tomorrow, you do the morning devotion for me; rise before the wooden clapper, stand in the middle of the courtyard, tell the knotted cord once through, go into the scripture room and read a passage, find a character in the scripture, write it on that board, and when it's written bring it to me to see; seven days; do it seven days; after seven days, if you still say you can't do it, he'll hand it to Lingbao.

Jingfu agreed. The first morning he stood in the middle of the courtyard for half a watch, went into the scripture room, opened the Psalms, read a passage, sat, sat till the clapper, and not one character was on the board; he came empty-handed to Jingjing and said he'd stood and thought of nothing, read and couldn't take it in. Jingjing said: go again tomorrow. The second day it was empty again, and the third; the fourth day he wrote a character, and when he had written it rubbed it out; the fifth, the sixth, he came empty-handed; each day Jingfu's head hung lower, and on the sixth evening he told Lingbao: Master has picked the wrong man. Lingbao said: in Xizhou long ago, seven elders took turns coming to your Master's room at night to borrow his oil-dish lamp and learn to write characters; he taught those seven a whole winter, and what came of it was one heart, one lotus, one vow; you've had only six days.

On the seventh morning Jingfu stood in the middle of the courtyard; it was still dark; he told the knotted cord, and halfway through looked back once at the monastery gate; the lamp at the gate he had lit the night before and it was still burning, its flame shrunk to the size of a bean, the oil nearly dry. He looked at that point of fire, stood till the edge of the sky went grey, went into the scripture room, opened the scriptures—not the Psalms; he opened the Gospel according to John—came to "the light shineth in darkness; and the darkness comprehended it not," read it three times, took up the charcoal, and wrote a character on the board; what he wrote was deng, lamp. He brought the board to Jingjing's bed; Jingjing took it and looked; the character was written upright, far more upright than the hearts he had written himself this year, and he said: that'll do. Jingfu asked: do for what? Jingjing said: do for leading. You find one character from the scripture, carry it on your shoulders through a day, carry it till dark, and you'll know what leading is; leading is carrying one character every day; when you can't carry it, there's still Uncle Lingbao, still Brother Siyuan, still the whole monastery. Jingfu, kneeling, said: then this board—he'll take it to use. Jingjing said: the board is from Xizhou; take it; tomorrow morning, write your own character.

At the year's end it was rumoured in the city that the palace was going to Fengxiang, to the Famen Monastery, to welcome the Buddha's bone. It was said that in that pagoda lay a finger-bone of the Buddha, opened once in thirty years, and when it was opened the world was at peace; it was said the Son of Heaven had already sent a palace envoy, and in the first month of next year it would be welcomed into the palace and venerated, and after the veneration sent round the monasteries of the capital in turn for the common people to pay homage. The people of the West Market, speaking of it, were all eyebrows and sparkle: when the time came the whole city would go to see; some would burn their fingers, some burn their arms; some had already begun saving money to give to the Buddhist monasteries. In the monastery too some were moved: two old households of the ward, believers for twenty years, quietly asked Jingfu whether, in the first month when the Buddha's bone passed Yining Ward, they might go and stand in the street and look—just one look. Jingfu came to ask Jingjing; Jingjing, lying in bed, said: let them go; one look does no harm; when they've looked, let them come back; the gate is open; and if they look and don't come back, let them go too. An Yan, who was forty-seven that year and a deacon of the monastery, said at table: other people's business, we don't meddle in it; Jingjing heard and did not take up his words. He thought of Fengxiang: sixty-one years before, his Pa had been at Fengxiang with Lord Guo, meaning to fight back into Chang'an, and he had been in Xizhou, gazing east every night, thinking of his Pa at Fengxiang. He bowed his head and drank the half bowl of gruel in his bowl.

In the twelfth month he could not get up. That morning, before the clapper, he woke and called Jingfu; Jingfu was sleeping on the straw mat by the bed and woke at once. Jingjing said he couldn't get up; you go and stand a while in the middle of the courtyard for him. Jingfu was taken aback a moment, dressed, and went out; Jingjing lay listening to his footsteps go to the middle of the courtyard, stop, stay stopped till the window-paper greyed, and come back again. Jingfu came in holding the board and a stick of charcoal; on the board was the character he had written himself that morning; he rubbed it out and said: Master, today you write. Jingjing had him help him sit up a little, took the board—on it was still that heart; he wiped it with his sleeve, wiped it clean, took up the charcoal; his hand shook badly; he drew on the board several times, and what came out was still that character, more crooked than he used to write it, crooked like a child's. He handed the board to Jingfu and said: keep it.

That evening the window-paper darkened bit by bit, darkened till the lattice on it could no longer be made out, and he knew it was nearly dark; he called Jingfu and said: bring the lamp. Jingfu was taken aback a moment, and went out; when he came back he held in his hands the bronze lamp from the gate, with a little of last night's burnt dregs still in it; he set it on the low stand by the bed, and brought the oil jar, the cloth and the scissors too, and laid them out one by one. Jingjing propped himself up a little, and Jingfu put a pillow behind his back. He took the lamp in his hands: the bronze dish, handled for decades, handled till it shone, with a small dent at the edge—knocked in which year, he no longer remembered. He tipped the dregs into a saucer and wiped the dish clean with the cloth; his hands shook badly, and he wiped a long while; ladled a spoon of new oil into it, and spilled a little on the quilt; drew out a little length of wick, took up the scissors and cut once, not evenly, and cut again, and it was even. He handed the lamp to Jingfu and said: go and light it. Jingfu took the lamp and stood by the bed as if to ask something; Jingjing looked at him, waiting for him to ask; Jingfu asked nothing, and carried the lamp out. Jingjing leaned on the pillow and listened to his footsteps cross the courtyard, cross the front hall, reach the monastery gate; heard the flint strike once, and again, and then could hear nothing. He waited, and the footsteps came back, and Jingfu said: it's lit. Jingjing asked: is it straight? Jingfu said: it's straight. Jingjing nodded.

That night Jingfu did not go back to sleep under the colonnade; he spread a straw mat by Jingjing's bed and slept there. He could not sleep; he listened to Jingjing's breathing, one breath and another, very light, very slow, sometimes pausing, pausing so that his heart tightened, and then going on again. In the middle of the night he heard Jingjing speaking, very low; he leaned close to listen and made out that he was praying, now in Syriac, now in Tang; in Jingjing's hand was clutched that knotted cord, twisted of wool, black and shining; he gripped one knot, said one line, and told on to the next. Lord, have mercy. Another knot: Lord, have mercy on Pa. Another: Lord, have mercy on Uncle. Another: Lord, have mercy on Uncle Duan. Jingfu counted them one by one: Pa, Uncle, Uncle Duan, Uncle Xu, Elder Ahad, the names of the seven teachers of the law one by one, Elder Yonan, Lord Guo, Du De, Official Pei, Shiyiniang, Lord Mi, Lord Li, General Luo, David, Elder Samaan, Elder Narsai, Baaluo. There was also the name of an old monk that Jingfu did not understand—it sounded Indian. Toward the end Jingjing stopped, stopped for several breaths, and Jingfu thought he had fallen asleep and was about to lie back down, when he heard him say one more line: Lord, have mercy on her. Jingfu did not know who she was; he did not ask.

When the sky was nearly light, Jingfu got up and went to the gate to blow out the lamp. The lamp was still burning, its flame shrunk to the size of a bean, the oil nearly burnt dry; he squatted, blew out the lamp, and set it back on the stone block. He went back into the room; the window-paper was greyish white; Jingjing leaned on the pillow, his eyes closed, still clutching the knotted cord in his hand, his thumb and forefinger pinching one knot, pinching it very tight. Jingfu stood by the bed, and then knelt down.

The funeral was held in the first month. Those days the city was busy welcoming the Buddha's bone; the bone was welcomed from Fengxiang into the palace, venerated three days, and sent round the monasteries of the capital; the streets were seas of people, and as the bone passed someone burned his finger in the open street, and a smell of scorching drifted half the length of the street. Not many came to the burial: the monastery's people, a few old believers of the ward, a few hu merchant families of the West Market, the Mi family, the Luo family; the Li family sent one too, a grandson of Li Su's, very young, who stood at the back and kept his head bowed the whole time. Some of those who drew gruel every day at the gruel shed in the ward street came, and stood at the monastery gate, and did not come in. Carrying the coffin out of the city, they had to squeeze through gaps in the crowd, and as they squeezed through no one turned to look at them. Those two old households who had gone into the street to see the Buddha's bone, standing in the crowd, saw the coffin, squeezed out of the crowd, and followed behind it all the way to beyond the Kaiyuan Gate.

Jingjing was buried outside the Kaiyuan Gate, beside his Pa, with Yonan and A-Lian on his other side, a foot apart. Lingbao read the scripture, and when he had read it he went up to Jingfu and said: Brother told me long ago; from today you are the leader. Jingfu said: Uncle Lingbao is older than me. Lingbao said: I keep the accounts. When he had said it he turned to help fill in the earth. Siyuan pushed the corpse cart; on the cart was a new wooden stake he had whittled the night before; Jingfu took it, wrote on it with charcoal the two characters Jingjing, and then wrote a line of small characters; he thought, and what he wrote was: Of the Daqin Monastery, Yining Ward, Chang'an. He who composed the stele.

When they came back from burying Jingjing, Jingfu went to clear his room. There was not much in it: a few robes, a stack of drafts, two very old rolls of diary, the paper yellowed—written on the road in the year he was ten; one roll said Pa's left eyebrow, half missing; one said at the great river crossing met Du Huan of Jingzhao. Leaning at the head of the bed was that old board from Xizhou, and on the board, in charcoal, one character, crooked. Jingfu picked it up to look, took it to show Lingbao, and asked: this character, Master wrote it a whole year—what does it mean? Lingbao took the board and said: it's heart; and said: this character—there was a man who could write only this one Tang character his whole life; a woman taught it him. When he had said it, he gave the board back to Jingfu and said: don't rub it out.

That evening, Jingfu went to light the lamp. He lifted the lamp, tipped out the dregs, wiped it clean, added new oil, drew out a little length of wick, and trimmed it even; he did it very slowly, one thing after another, as if afraid of leaving one out; he struck the flint once, and it caught. The stone block was low; Master had had to hold the doorframe each time to squat down; Jingfu was young, and could reach it with a single bend. He set the lamp back on the stone block, stood up, and stood a while beneath the lamp.
