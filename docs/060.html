Volume Nine　The Translation Hall

Chapter 60　Jingjing

In the thirteenth year of Yuanhe Jingjing was seventy-two. Early that spring his legs went first. When he got up in the morning his two legs were like borrowed ones and would not do as they were told; he had to sit holding the edge of the bed and wait until they acknowledged their master before he could stand, and once standing, he walked slowly: from the back courtyard to the front hall, where others took twenty steps, he took forty. Jingfu whittled him a walking stick from an old branch sawn off the jujube tree in the back courtyard, whittled it smooth, and wound a strip of cloth round the grip. Leaning on the stick, he still rose every day before the clapper, stood a while in the middle of the courtyard, told the knots once, went into the scripture room to read a passage, and wrote one character on that old board from Xizhou; still went every morning to the hall to lead the morning office, sat a while in the scripture room after noon, and at dark went to the gate to light the lamp.

The lamp at the gate stood on the stone block to the left of it, a bronze oil dish half a palm across, the wick twisted of hemp. The monastery had been lighting this lamp for decades. At the end of the Tianbao years the old deacon lit it; in the year of the fall it went out; in the second year of Zhide Pa set it on the steps; in the first year of Qianyuan Yonan moved it to the stone block, and it had been on the stone block ever since. While Pa was alive Pa lit it; when Pa went, he lit it—thirty-four years he had lit it. Before, tipping out the dregs, adding oil, trimming the wick had taken no longer than a cup of tea; now it took half a meal. The stone block was low and he could no longer bend; every time he had to hold the door frame and crouch slowly down, and once down, hold the door frame and stand slowly up. Once the flint took three strikes to catch. Jingfu, beside him, watched his hands tremble and reached out. "Master, I'll do it."

"No need."

So Jingfu drew his hand back, and when the lamp was lit, helped him back in.

The character on his board that year was the same for many months. When Lingbao came into the scripture room in the morning he saw the board leaning by the table, and on it the character xin, heart; the next day it was still that character, and the day after still. Lingbao did not ask.

That summer was especially hot. For months no soaking rain fell on Chang'an; the wells grew shallow, the leaves of the scholar trees curled at the edges in the sun; the monastery's people hid in the hall by day and moved out to sleep in the courtyard at night. Jingfu slept in the gallery and snored, a snore that carried from one end of the gallery to the other. Jingjing could not sleep. He lay in his room under a thin quilt. Others were hot; he was not. His body was always cold, his hands and feet especially, even in summer.

One noon he asked Jingfu to fetch him a bowl of water. Jingfu went to the stove to ladle hot water, and Jingjing called him back. "From the well."

Jingfu stopped and turned his head. "Master, that's cold water."

"I know it's cold water."

Jingfu stood there and didn't move. He had come into this monastery at five or six and heard for a dozen-odd years about the Master being afraid of cold water. He himself got up in the night to drink well water, and the Master had caught him at it more times than he could count, and had never scolded him, only frowned every time and watched him drink, as if watching someone stuff live coals into his mouth.

"Go on," said Jingjing.

Jingfu went to the well under the scholar tree, wound up a bucket on the windlass, ladled out a bowlful, and brought it back. At once a fine sweat of drops gathered on the outside of the bowl.

Jingjing held the bowl in both hands. He remembered Lingzhou, the winter he was ten, when he secretly drank a ladle of cold water in the night and the next day made a show of himself at table; remembered the gate of Xizhou, Uncle Duan on his horse turning his head to say, don't drink cold water; remembered under the willows at the Kaiyuan Gate, Pa not waving, saying only one thing, don't drink cold water; remembered Shiyiniang coming out from behind the Pei spirit seat with hot water and saying, you can't drink cold. All his life he had been warned by people, all his life. He brought the bowl to his lips and drank a mouthful. Cold—cold enough to set his teeth aching at the roots, cold all the way down into his belly. He drank another mouthful, and another, drank the whole bowl, and handed the empty bowl to Jingfu.

Jingfu took the bowl and watched him, waiting, and Jingjing waited too. His belly did not rumble. By evening it still had not. Jingjing leaned against the wall and slowly smiled; he smiled without a sound, and many lines crowded out at the corners of his eyes.

"Afraid all my life," he said.

That night he slept very well.

In the autumn he began to give instructions. Today he remembered one thing and said it; tomorrow he remembered another and said that. Lingbao and Jingfu stayed at his side, and as he said each thing, Lingbao wrote it down.

First the scriptures. He had Lingbao take out that list of the scriptures and read the last line to Jingfu. Lingbao read it: "The great remainder lies in the bark-skin cases, not yet translated."

"The several hundred not translated—if you can't translate them, leave them for now," said Jingjing, "until there's someone who can."

"And if there's never anyone who can?" asked Jingfu.

"Then leave them always. Left there, they won't spoil." He had Lingbao bring out a few rolls of the Tang scriptures too and show Jingfu the little dots beside the characters. "These dots—if one day you find words of your own, change them; change one, and wipe out its dot. If you can't find them, leave them."

Then the papers. The sheet Luo Haoxin had copied out that year, yellowed through now, its edges so brittle they flaked at a touch, and under it the little slip he had copied himself, Jingjing should propagate the teaching of Mishihe, the shi that was 尸, corpse, written as 施, to give. "Put them in the scripture cabinet," he told Lingbao, "with Elder Samaan's nine ledgers. Elder Samaan said: abusing us, and still a document."

Then Official Pei's little book: the first half in Official Pei's upright regular hand, the second half in his own right-slanting characters, and a few pages after that in Lingbao's hand. "Keep writing it, and when you've finished, give it to Jingfu."

Then the bowls in the cupboard. He had Jingfu open the cupboard and set them on the table one by one: his own, with a small chip knocked out of the rim; Pa's, which Jingfu used every day now; and one more, with a notch in its rim and a pinch of earth in it. He pointed to the bowl with the earth in it and said, "This is Du De's. Don't tip out the earth, and don't use it for rice either."

"And Master's own?"

Jingjing looked at his own bowl. The little chip in its rim he had knocked out in his twenties, and after that he had always drunk his soup from the other side. "See to it as you think fit."

Then the matter of who would direct the monastery. That evening, alone in the room with Lingbao, he said, "When I'm dead, the directing of the monastery goes to Jingfu."

Lingbao was fifty-one that year. David had laid hands on him; he was a priest; the monastery's accounts and keys were in his hands; by age, by standing, it should have been his. When he heard it there was not a trace of difficulty in his face. "If Brother decides it so—I thought of it long ago. I keep the accounts and the scripture room. Jingfu is young, his Tang is good, people in the city know him; from now on, for dealings with the government and with the ward, a young one will be useful. When Pa handed you the charity that year, he had me go along with you to learn the way. I'll go along with Jingfu too, and learn the way."

Jingfu would not. He was twenty-three that year. He could lead the evening office and preach, and his Tang was better than anyone's at the monastery, but he knelt before Jingjing's bed and would not hear of it.

"Master, I can't. I've studied Syriac a dozen years and more and still can't get it right. I can't read the scripture properly; half of what's sung at the morning and evening office is sounds I made up myself. The old people at the monastery all know it and all hold back from laughing. The people in the south of the city still say today that the monastery's people are the Son of Heaven's monks. Make a Tang mouth director as well, and they'll say this monastery isn't even hu any more. Uncle Lingbao is a priest; Elder David laid hands on him. Hands haven't even been laid on me as deacon. What standing do I have?" As he spoke his eyes reddened. "Master, if you go, I'll keep this monastery and keep that lamp. But direct it—I can't."

Jingjing did not try to persuade him. He lay in bed looking at this child, and remembered fifty years before, when Pa came back from Hezhong and in the side courtyard handed him the charity, and the going along to the government offices, and he too had said he couldn't, and Pa had not tried to persuade him either.

"Then let's do this," he said. "From tomorrow, you do the morning for me. Get up before the clapper, stand in the middle of the courtyard, tell the knots once, go into the scripture room and read a passage, find a character in the scripture, write it on that board, and when it's written, bring it to show me. Do it seven days. If after seven days you still say you can't, I'll hand it to Uncle Lingbao."

Jingfu agreed. The first morning he stood in the middle of the courtyard half an hour, went into the scripture room, opened the psalms, read a passage, sat, sat until the clapper, and not one character was on the board. He came to Jingjing empty-handed. "I stood, and thought of nothing. I read, and couldn't take it in."

"Go again tomorrow."

The second day it was empty again, and the third. On the fourth he wrote a character, and when he had written it wiped it off again. The fifth, the sixth, he came empty-handed, his head lower each day. On the evening of the sixth he said to Lingbao in the kitchen, "Master has chosen the wrong man."

Lingbao was scrubbing a pot and didn't raise his head. "When your master was at Xizhou, the seven elders took turns coming to his room at night to borrow his oil-dish lamp and learn to write characters. He taught those seven a whole winter, and what came of it was one heart, one lotus, one vow. You've had six days."

On the seventh morning Jingfu stood in the middle of the courtyard while it was still dark, telling the knots. Halfway through he turned his head. The lamp at the monastery gate, which he had lit the night before, was still burning, the flame shrunk to the size of a bean, the oil nearly dry. He looked at that point of fire, stood until the edge of the sky turned grey, went into the scripture room, and did not open the psalms. He opened the Gospel of John, turned to And the light shineth in darkness; and the darkness comprehended it not, read it three times, took up the charcoal, and wrote on the board the character deng, lamp.

He brought the board to Jingjing's bedside. Jingjing took it. The character was written upright, far more upright than the heart characters he had written himself that year.

"That'll do."

"That'll do for what?"

"For directing the monastery," said Jingjing. "You found a character in the scripture. Carry it through a day, carry it till dark, and you'll know what directing the monastery is. Directing the monastery is carrying one character every day. When you can't carry it, there's still Uncle Lingbao, still Brother Siyuan, still the whole monastery."

Jingfu knelt. "Then this board—I'll take it and use it."

"The board is from Xizhou. Take it. Tomorrow morning, write your own character."

At the end of the year it was rumoured in the city that the palace was going to Fengxiang to receive the Buddha's bone from the Famen Monastery. In that pagoda, they said, a finger bone of the Buddha was kept; it was opened once in thirty years, and whenever it was opened the realm was at peace. The Son of Heaven had already sent a palace envoy, and in the first month of next year it would be received into the palace and venerated, and when the venerating was done it would be sent round the monasteries of the capital in turn to be venerated, so the common people could all go and pay their reverence. When the people of the West Market spoke of it their eyebrows danced. Some said they would burn a finger, some said they would burn an arm, some had already begun saving money to give to the Buddhist monasteries.

Two households in the ward who had believed twenty years came quietly to Jingfu and asked whether, in the first month, when the Buddha's bone passed through the Yining Ward, they might go and stand in the street a while. "Just to have a look," said the old man. "Have a look and come back."

Jingfu came to ask Jingjing. Jingjing, lying in bed, said, "Let them go. A look does no harm. When they've looked, they come back; the gate is open. And if they look and don't come back, let them go too."

An Yan was forty-seven that year and a deacon of the monastery. At table that day he said, "Other people's affairs—we don't concern ourselves with them." Jingjing listened and did not take up what he said. He was thinking of Fengxiang. Sixty-one years before, Pa had been at Fengxiang with Lord Guo, about to fight his way back to Chang'an, and he had been at Xizhou, gazing east every night, thinking of Pa at Fengxiang. He bowed his head and drank the half bowl of gruel in his bowl.

In the twelfth month he could no longer get up. That morning, before the clapper, he woke and called Jingfu. Jingfu was asleep on the straw mat by the bed, and woke the moment he was called.

"I can't get up," said Jingjing. "Go and stand in the middle of the courtyard for me."

Jingfu dressed and went out. Jingjing lay listening to his footsteps go to the middle of the courtyard and stop, stop until the window paper turned grey, and then come back. Jingfu came in with the board and a stick of charcoal in his hands. On the board was the character he had written himself that morning; he wiped it off with his sleeve and held it out. "Master, today you write."

Jingjing had him help him sit up a little, took the board, took up the charcoal. His hand shook badly. He drew on the board several times, and what came out was still that character heart, more crooked than those he had written before, crooked as if a child had written it. He handed the board to Jingfu. "Keep it."

That evening the window paper darkened bit by bit, darkened until the lattice on it could not be made out, and he knew it was nearly dark.

"Bring the lamp."

Jingfu stood stock still. After a while he went out, and when he came back he was holding the bronze lamp from the gate in both hands, with a little of last night's burnt dregs of oil still in it. He set the lamp on the small table by the bed, brought the oil jar, the cloth and the scissors too, set them out one by one, and put a pillow behind Jingjing's back.

Jingjing took the lamp in his hands. A bronze dish, rubbed by hands for decades until it shone, with a small dent at the rim—knocked which year, he no longer remembered. He tipped the dregs into a saucer and wiped the dish clean with the cloth; his hands shook, and the wiping took a good while. He ladled in a spoonful of new oil and spilled a little on the quilt. He drew out a little length of wick, took up the scissors and snipped it, did not snip it even, snipped again, and got it even.

"Go and light it."

Jingfu took the lamp and stood by the bed. His mouth moved. Jingjing looked at him, waiting for him to ask; he asked nothing, and carried the lamp out. Jingjing leaned on the pillow and listened to his footsteps cross the courtyard, cross the front hall, reach the monastery gate; heard the flint strike once, and strike again, and then could hear nothing. He waited, and the footsteps came back.

"It's lit."

"Is it straight?"

"It's straight."

That night Jingfu did not go back to sleep in the gallery. He laid a straw mat beside Jingjing's bed and slept there. He could not sleep. He listened to Jingjing's breathing, one breath after another, very light, very slow; sometimes it broke off, broke off so that his heart clenched, and then took up again. In the middle of the night he heard Jingjing speaking, very low, and leaned close to listen, and made out that he was praying, sometimes in Syriac, sometimes in Tang. Jingjing had that string of knots gripped in his hand, twisted of wool, black and shining; he held one knot and said a line, then told on to the next knot.

"Lord, have mercy." Another knot. "Lord, have mercy on Pa." Another. "Lord, have mercy on my uncle." Another. "Lord, have mercy on Uncle Duan."

Jingfu counted them one by one: Pa, his uncle, Uncle Duan, Uncle Xu, Elder Ahad, the names of the seven teachers of the law one by one, Elder Yonan, Lord Guo, Du De, Official Pei, Shiyiniang, Lord Mi, Lord Li, General Luo, David, Elder Samaan, Elder Narsai, Baaluo. There was also an old monk's name that Jingfu couldn't understand; it sounded Indian. Toward the end Jingjing stopped. Several breaths went by, and Jingfu thought he had fallen asleep and was about to lie down again, when he heard him say one more line:

"Lord, have mercy on her."

Jingfu did not know who she was. He did not ask.

Toward dawn Jingfu got up and went to the gate to blow out the lamp. It was still burning, the flame shrunk to the size of a bean, the oil nearly burned dry. He blew it out and set it back on the stone block. When he came back into the room the window paper was greyish white. Jingjing was leaning on the pillow with his eyes closed, still gripping the string of knots, a knot pinched between thumb and forefinger, pinched very tight. Jingfu stood by the bed, and then knelt down.

The funeral was held in the first month. In those days the city was busy receiving the Buddha's bone. The bone was brought from Fengxiang into the palace and venerated three days, then sent round the monasteries of the capital, and the streets were seas of people; some burned their fingers in the street as the bone went by, and a smell of scorching drifted half a street. Carrying the coffin out of the city, they had to squeeze through gaps in the crowd, and as they squeezed through, no one turned to look at them. The two old households of believers who had gone to look at the bone, standing in the crowd, saw the coffin, squeezed out of the crowd, and followed behind it all the way outside the Kaiyuan Gate.

Jingjing was buried outside the Kaiyuan Gate, beside Pa, with Yonan and A-Lian on his other side, a foot apart. Lingbao read the scripture, and when he had read it went up to Jingfu. "Brother told me long ago. From today, you direct the monastery."

"Uncle Lingbao is older than me."

"I keep the accounts." When Lingbao had said it he turned to help fill in the earth.

Siyuan pushed the cart for the dead, and on the cart was a new wooden stake, which he had whittled the night before. Jingfu took it, wrote on it with charcoal the name Jingjing, thought, and added a line in small characters: Daqin Monastery, Yining Ward, Chang'an. He who composed the stele.

When they came back from the burial Jingfu went to clear his room. There were not many things: a few robes, a stack of drafts, two very old rolls of diary, the paper yellowed, written on the road the year he was ten; one roll said Pa's left eyebrow, half missing; one said at the great river crossing met Du Huan of Jingzhao. The old board from Xizhou was still leaning at the head of the bed. That day the Master had told him to keep it, and he had never dared move it; the heart on it was crooked. He took it to show Lingbao.

"Uncle Lingbao, this character—Master wrote it for a year. Who was it written for?"

Lingbao took the board and looked at it a good while. "There was a man who all his life could write only this one Tang character, and a woman from the lane behind an eating house in the West Market taught it to him. That woman was afterwards the one both of us brothers called Ma." He gave the board back to Jingfu. "Don't wipe it off."

That evening Jingfu went to light the lamp. He did it very slowly, one thing after another, as if afraid to leave anything out. The flint struck once and caught. The stone block was low; every time, the Master had had to hold the door frame to crouch down. Jingfu reached it the moment he bent.
