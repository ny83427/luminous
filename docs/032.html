Volume Six　The Shuofang Army

Chapter 32　Washing Off the Dust

In the third month of the second year of Baoying, a caravan from the west reached Chang'an ahead of the rest and brought word: the little deacon of the Yining Ward was on his way back, and had already crossed the Long Mountains.

Yisi heard it, nodded, and went on to see to his gruel shed in the south of the city.

The next day he went to the gruel shed as usual. The day after, too. On the morning of the fourth day he led the chestnut out of the Kaiyuan Gate and went twenty li west, to the Lingao post station.

The Lingao station was the post station at the western edge of Chang'an. Those going west were seen off here; those coming back from the west were welcomed here. There were a few old willows at the station gate, their branches stripped bare by people breaking off twigs.

Yisi stood under the willows all morning.

In the afternoon, a line of yellow dust rose on the road to the west.

The caravan came very slowly. At its head was a grey horse, and the rider sat very straight, very tall, thin, a layer of dark down sprouting on his chin.

Yisi looked a good while before he recognised him as Jingjing.

Jingjing had seen him too. He sat stunned a moment in the saddle, then jumped down, led his horse by the reins, and walked over. Three paces from Yisi he stopped.

The two of them looked at each other.

Jingjing was taller again than when he had left. His face was darker, his lips so dry they were peeling, rings of blue under his eyes. When he walked his left foot dragged a little; his shoe had probably rubbed his foot raw.

"Pa," said Jingjing. "I'm back."

Yisi looked at him.

"And your stomach?" Yisi asked.

Jingjing burst out laughing. And laughing, his eyes went red.

"Below the Pamirs," he said, "I drank snowmelt once."

Yisi nodded.

"Just once," said Jingjing.

"I know," said Yisi.

He put out his hand and pressed it on Jingjing's shoulder. Pressed hard, and when he had pressed, drew his hand back.

Behind the caravan came three men in grey robes.

Jingjing presented them to Yisi one by one.

The first was called Azarel, past sixty, tall, very straight-backed, a white beard hanging to his chest. He walked slowly and spoke slowly; he said a sentence and paused, as if every sentence had first to be weighed in his mouth. He was the first of the three.

The second was called Marya, round-faced, round-bellied, with a voice like a great bell. Jingjing told Yisi later that the elder Marya was the preacher; he had preached for half a year along the road, until in the end, as soon as the camel drivers saw him open his mouth, they went off to feed the camels.

The third was called Samaan, thin and small, his beard sparse and straggly, a leather-bound account book in his arms that he carried wherever he went. He had charge of the rule. When he made his greeting to Yisi, Yisi saw that the account book was already half filled.

The elder Marcus had not come back. He had stayed in the Two Rivers. Jingjing said the elder had found a little room in the back courtyard of the Patriarchate, and sat at its door every day in the sun counting his beads, speaking to no one, sitting there all day long.

The three old believers had all come back. The old medicine trader who had been to Fulin climbed down from his camel's back, his legs gave way, and he knelt on the ground and lay there a long while without getting up. The men beside him went to help him up, and he waved them off: no need, he just wanted to lie here a while.

That night Yisi boiled a pot of water.

He boiled it very hot, poured it into a wooden basin, carried it to Jingjing and set it on the ground.

"Feet," he said.

Jingjing sat on the mat and took off his shoes. There was a hole worn through the sole, and his sock was stuck to the blisters on his foot; as he pulled it off he winced. Both his feet were covered with calluses, his heels cracked in several places, and the little toe on his left foot was swollen and shiny.

He put his feet in the water. The water was very hot, and he drew in his breath.

Yisi squatted by the basin and looked at those two feet. After looking a while he scooped up water from the basin with his hand and poured it over the tops of Jingjing's feet. Poured it once, and again.

Jingjing kept his head down, looking at his father's hands. They were very big, with an old scar across the back of one, left from Changshan. The water ran down between his fingers and back into the basin.

The room was very quiet. The pot on the stove was still steaming.

After a long while Jingjing spoke.

"Pa," he said, "Uncle is gone."

Yisi's hands stopped.

Jingjing told it from the beginning.

He told of the board struck in the mornings at the Patriarchate. Told of his uncle tying that knot every morning with his teeth. Told how his uncle questioned in such detail that if you got one number wrong, next time he pointed it out. Told how his uncle had asked what became of that basin of fire, and he had said it didn't go out, and his uncle had nodded. Told how these nine years at the Patriarchate had not been peaceful, how his uncle never slept two nights running in the same room.

He told of that night, the room beside the storehouse. Told of his uncle's left hand gripping his wrist. Told his uncle's last three sentences.

He did not change one word.

Then he told of Baluma. Told of the lamp oil, the bread money, the riverbank, how he had told Baluma to go. Told how on the morning of the fourth day Baluma had lain outside the gate of the Patriarchate with a pair of new shoes on his feet. Told of the stake with nothing carved on it.

When he had finished, the room was quiet again.

The water in the basin was no longer hot.

Yisi squatted there all the while without moving. His hand still rested on the rim of the basin, and the water dripped from his fingers drop by drop.

After a long while, he asked one thing.

"Was he in pain?"

Jingjing remembered that seven years before, at the Patriarchate, he had wondered the same thing. He had been wondering about his uncle's arm. He had not asked aloud.

"When he spoke," said Jingjing, "he didn't seem in pain."

Yisi nodded.

Jingjing looked at him, and his lips moved several times before he got the question out.

"Pa, when I let Baluma go—was I wrong to let him go?"

Yisi did not answer at once.

He stood up, went to the stove, ladled a dipper of hot water from the pot, came back, and slowly poured it into the basin. The water in the basin steamed again. He put the dipper down and squatted again.

"Your uncle gave you his words to pass on," said Yisi. "You passed them on."

He scooped up water again and poured it over the tops of Jingjing's feet.

Jingjing brought out his uncle's things.

One was a roll, the Patriarch's written instruction, wrapped in oilcloth, sealed with wax, the wax stamped with the Patriarch's seal. This roll was in the keeping of the elder Azarel, to be opened before everyone tomorrow.

The other was only a sheet of paper, folded into a small square, unsealed, which his uncle had written to Yisi. Jingjing said that when his uncle had written it he gave it to him and said: you'll take it with you when you go back. The day he wrote it was less than a month before he died.

Yisi took the paper and opened it.

There were only a few lines on it, in the country speech of Balkh, spelled out in Syriac letters. The letters leaned left, one after another, like men walking into the wind.

Yisi looked at those letters a long time.

When he was small and learning his letters, it was Shapur who taught him. Shapur had held his hand and written stroke by stroke. In those days Shapur's letters leaned right, leaned very handsomely, and Yisi had tried all his life and never learned to make them so.

Now these few lines leaned left.

The paper said:

"I return him to you. A head taller than when he went. His stomach still the same. The instruction is enclosed; do as it says. Take care of yourself. Your brother."

Yisi folded the paper, folded it back into its small square, and put it inside his coat, against his chest.

Jingjing lifted his feet out of the basin. Yisi handed him a cloth, and he dried his feet.

That night the lamp in Yisi's room stayed lit all night. Jingjing, lying in the next room, heard his father praying, in the country speech of Balkh. He prayed all night.

The next day at the hour of si, everyone in the monastery came to the hall.

The seven teachers, twenty-odd monk-soldiers, Xu Jing, old believers and new, Tang and hu, packed the hall full. Official Pei came too, not in his official robe, standing at the back.

The elder Azarel stood under the wooden cross on the east wall, broke the wax seal before everyone, unrolled the instruction and read it sentence by sentence. He read in Syriac, a sentence and a pause, and Jingjing beside him turned it into Tang.

The instruction began with offices.

In Chang'an there was to be one Elder of Teaching, in charge of expounding the scriptures, instruction in the Way, and examination—held by the elder Marya. One Deacon of Relief, in charge of giving gruel, clothing and medicine, gathering the dead, taking in foundlings—held for the present by Jingjing, who would receive the office properly when he came of age. One Warden of the Rule, in charge of the rule, the accounts, the registers, rewards and penalties—held by the elder Samaan. Yisi was to be Superintendent of Chang'an, and all three offices above were under the Superintendent's direction.

When he came to held for the present by Jingjing, a few people in the hall murmured. Jingjing, translating, paused here, went red, and went on. He translated his own name as flatly as anyone else's.

When he came to the Warden of the Rule, Yisi glanced at Sabrisho. All these years Sabrisho had kept the rule at the monastery, kept it without one slip. Under that felt-like beard of his, nothing could be read. When Azarel had finished reading this article, Sabrisho took an old register from inside his robe—the rules he had recorded these seven years—went up to Samaan and handed it to him with both hands. Samaan took it with both hands too.

Then came the plan. Five articles in all.

The first: Do not quarrel with the other teachings. Buddhist, Daoist, Zoroastrian, Manichaean, each has its own law; do not dispute, do not disparage, do not enter their gates to trouble them.

When this article was read, the hall was so quiet you could hear the birds in the locust tree outside. Ba'aluo had been gone more than two years, and of the nine sent back, six were still at the monastery; they stood now at the back of the hall, heads bowed.

Narsai stood at the very edge. Yisi saw his eyes on the floor, kept on the floor all the while, never raised.

The second: Establish trust by relief.

The third: All the faithful are to be in groups of fifty, each group with one man set over it; any matter is to be reported first to the head of the group, and the head of the group reports to the deacon.

When this article was read, several of the old soldiers laughed. The year of the relief at Qujiang, fifty to a group, sitting to eat—this was where it came from. They had never thought this method would travel from Chang'an to the Two Rivers, and come back again written in the Patriarch's instruction.

The fourth: Each year, three men are to be trained who can preach in the Tang tongue.

When Ishaq heard this article he sighed and said: "Three. I've studied six years, and I still say mo pa as 'grind and crawl.'"

Everyone in the hall laughed.

The fifth: In all dealings with the government, go only through Yisi and the official surnamed Pei; no one else is to form connections privately.

Official Pei, standing at the back, was taken aback when Jingjing had finished translating this article. He had never imagined the Patriarch in the Two Rivers would know his name was Pei.

Yisi knew. In that half year, in that little room, Jingjing had been questioned one thing after another by a man. That man's memory was good; he never got a single number wrong.

When Azarel had finished reading, he rolled up the instruction and handed it to Yisi.

Yisi took it, and did not open it. He knew whose hand was in it.

"Good," he said.

That evening the monastery gave a feast to wash off the dust of the road.

The feast was laid in the back courtyard, under the jujube tree. Several long tables were pushed together, and on them were mutton broth, flatbread, steamed buns, stewed turnip, pickles, and a jar of wine. The mutton broth had been stewed by an old believer of the monastery, the way the elder Ahad had stewed it in the old days, stewed most of the day, the turnip so tender it fell apart at the touch of the chopsticks. The steamed buns had been sent from Lord Guo's residence, two great steamers of them; the old servant who brought them said Lord Guo said this was what he ate himself in the mornings, and he didn't know whether the elders from the Two Rivers would find them to their taste.

The elder Azarel tasted one, chewed it a long time, and said something in Syriac. Jingjing translated it: the elder says this bread has no taste, but once you've eaten it you want more.

The old servant went back and repeated it to Guo Ziyi. Later it was heard that when Guo Ziyi heard it, he smiled.

Halfway through the meal, music struck up in a corner of the back courtyard.

It was a few of the monastery's believers. An old Sogdian played the bili, a young Tang man played the pipa, and a Uighur woman held a harp, and when her fingers plucked the strings the sound flowed out like water.

When the bili began, the whole courtyard fell quiet.

Then a few young people stood up and sang a song.

They sang it in Tang. Babai and a few Tang believers who could read had pieced it together character by character over these two years. It was not yet quite smooth; the tones of a few characters did not fit the tune, and the singers had to take a turn to get round them.

"The highest heavens all deeply revere and praise; the great earth longs again for peace and harmony."

The three elders could not understand. They sat there looking at the young people singing, at their mouths opening and closing.

The elder Azarel leaned his head towards Jingjing and asked: what are they singing?

Jingjing listened, and turned it for him line by line. Turned it into Syriac, very slowly. When he had turned one line, the young people had already sung the next. So he chased after it, translating.

As Azarel listened, he raised his hand and wiped his eyes.

He wiped them, and wiped them again.

The elder Marya sat beside him and for once said not a word. The elder Samaan held his account book, closed.

Xu Jing had drunk too much; he leaned over to Yisi and patted his shoulder with that hand missing two fingers and said a great string of things—that the elders were good elders, that the little deacon was a good deacon, that this life of his had been picked up by this monastery—and as he talked he leaned back against the jujube tree and fell asleep.

Yisi sat at one end of the long table and drank little. Before him were two bowls. One was his own; one had a chipped rim, and a pinch of earth in it.

He listened to the song all the way through, until it ended.

When the feast broke up, it was past the second watch.

The guests had all gone, the elders had all gone to rest. Yisi and Jingjing cleared the bowls and dishes from the tables and took them to the well. Jingjing went to draw water to wash them, and Yisi said: wash them tomorrow.

The two of them walked to the front courtyard.

At the monastery gate hung a small lamp. The lamp was the monastery's custom these years: lit at dark, put out only at daybreak, so that anyone at the end of his road in the night would see the lamp and know there was a door here. Its shade was of paper, pasted many layers thick, yellowed by smoke. The wind blew in from the ward, the flame wavered, and stood still again.

Yisi stopped under the lamp.

Jingjing stopped too.

Yisi looked at him. The lamplight fell from above on Jingjing's face, lighting up the layer of dark down on his chin, lighting up those black eyes.

"Is the road still open?" asked Yisi.

Jingjing was taken aback.

He had heard these words. In that little room in Ctesiphon he had heard them twice. The man who said them had used the country speech of Balkh, just as his father was using it now.

He thought. He thought of the snowmelt river below the Pamirs, of the boat going downstream from the ferry on the Tigris, of the room beside the storehouse, of the stake at the edge of the burial ground with nothing carved on it.

"It's open," said Jingjing.

Yisi nodded.

The two of them went in. The room was dark; Yisi felt for the lamp stand and lit the lamp. He went to the cupboard and opened its door.

Two years before, on the day Jingjing left, he had put Jingjing's bowl in this cupboard.

He took that bowl out and set it on the table, beside Du De's bowl.
