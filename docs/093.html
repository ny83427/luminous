Appendix 5　The Stele, V: Guest Ritual and Foreign Religions under Taizong

The stele says that when Alopen reached Chang'an in the ninth year of Zhenguan, Taizong sent his chief minister Fang Xuanling with an honour guard to receive him in the western outskirts, brought him into the palace to translate his scriptures and explain his teaching, and three years later issued an edict allowing him to build a monastery and ordain monks. Was a reception like this ordinary in the early Tang, or exceptional? This appendix first reads the inscription closely, then sets it against other sources, compares it with the norms of Tang guest ritual and with actual cases of foreign religious figures received under Taizong, and finally sums up the Zhenguan court's policy toward foreign religions.

1. Conclusions first

  1. The rank was high, but not unprecedented. By the stele's account, Taizong sent his first minister, Fang Xuanling, with an honour guard to the western outskirts, and Alopen translated scriptures and was questioned in the palace. This is above the routine reception that Tang guest ritual gave an ordinary foreign envoy (usually a welcome by officials of the Court of State Ceremonial at the guest lodge), and close to the honours given to eminent monks and distinguished guests. But in the same period Fang Xuanling also oversaw the translation bureau of the Indian monk Prabhākaramitra, and as regent welcomed Xuanzang home; ministers receiving foreign religious figures were not rare under Taizong.
  2. The truly "special" thing is not the welcome but the edict. The edict of the twelfth year of Zhenguan (638) allowed him to build a monastery and ordain twenty-one monks, and said outright that his teaching "ought to be practised throughout the world." It is independently recorded in the Tang Huiyao and is reliable history. The details of the welcome in the outskirts, by contrast, appear only on a stele raised by the Luminous community itself a hundred and forty-six years later—a single, uncorroborated source.
  3. The stele's description is partly rhetoric. Writing in 781, Jingjing deliberately shaped Alopen into a "man of high virtue" received with great ceremony by Taizong, in order to win the Luminous Religion a place under Dezong. The description may have a factual basis, but its details ("full honour guard," "brought in," "within the palace") should not be taken wholesale.
  4. The reception was a matter of guest ritual. The word bin (賓, "as a guest") in "received him as a guest and brought him in" makes his status plain: Alopen was received as a foreign guest, not as a religious leader. This fits the logic by which the early Tang managed foreign religions under the heading of "guests" (under the Court of State Ceremonial).

The arguments follow point by point.

The Zunjing colophon is worth attention. It says that after reaching China Alopen "presented a memorial in his own tongue," which Fang Xuanling and Wei Zheng "had translated and presented." So Fang Xuanling really was involved with Alopen, but his role looks more like that of the minister responsible for passing on memorials, not necessarily one who went out of the city in person to greet him. The Handbook of Christianity in China, edited by Nicolas Standaert, suggests that Alopen's "own tongue" was Sogdian rather than Persian, since Sogdian was the lingua franca of Central Asia at the time.

3. The routine of Tang guest ritual

To judge whether Alopen's treatment was special, we first need to know what was routine.

3.1 What the ritual codes prescribe

The Tang compiled three ritual codes: the Zhenguan Rites (promulgated 637, chiefly compiled by Fang Xuanling and others), the Xianqing Rites (658) and the Kaiyuan Rites (732). All three contain "guest ritual." Zhu Yi, in his study of the ritual spaces of guest ritual from the Tang to the Northern Song (2014), cites the Zhenguan Rites' summary: guest ritual was for receiving the rulers of the four barbarian peoples and their envoys, and its procedure comprised "sending envoys to welcome and comfort them, assigning lodgings, the presentation of gifts, and the forms of the audience and the banquet."

The procedure in the Kaiyuan Rites is:

  1. Welcome and lodging: the court sends an envoy to greet the guest and arranges his lodging;
  2. Notice of the audience: the guest is told the day he will be received;
  3. Audience: he enters the palace to be received by the emperor;
  4. Banquet: the emperor gives a feast;
  5. Leave-taking: he takes his leave.

3.2 Where, and by whom, the welcome was given

According to Zhu Yi, in the Kaiyuan Rites the first two steps—"welcome and lodging" and "notice of the audience"—took place at the guest lodge of the Court of State Ceremonial, inside the imperial city. The codes say only that the court "sends an envoy," without fixing his rank, and say nothing at all of a chief minister going out of the city to meet anyone.

Audiences also differed in rank: a foreign ruler was received in the Taiji Hall and could ascend the hall and be seated; a foreign envoy was received in whatever hall the emperor was in, standing in the courtyard. Zhu Yi also notes that differences in treatment between states lay mainly in the distinction between "foreign ruler" and "foreign envoy," not in the size of the state.

3.3 The offices responsible

  • The Court of State Ceremonial (鴻臚寺): in charge of guests and of funeral rites. Under it the Office for Receiving Guests handled the reception and supply of foreign visitors.
  • The guest lodge of the Court of State Ceremonial: where foreign envoys stayed.

On the face of it, Alopen was received well above an ordinary foreign envoy.

4. Actual cases under Taizong

But ritual codes and practice often diverged. Comparing several contemporary cases gives a better idea of the weight of Alopen's treatment.

Case 1: the Indian monk Prabhākaramitra (arrived in Chang'an, twelfth month of the ninth year of Wude)

According to Daoxuan's Further Biographies of Eminent Monks, chapter 3, "Biography of Prabhākaramitra":

  • Prabhākaramitra was an eminent monk of kṣatriya birth from central India, who had studied under Śīlabhadra at Nālandā, then travelled north to preach at the court of the Yabghu Qaghan of the Western Turks, who treated him with honour.
  • In the ninth year of Wude (626) the Tang prince of Gaoping, on embassy to the Western Turks, met him. The qaghan would not let him go; the prince reported to the court, the court issued a summons, and Prabhākaramitra came to the Tang with the prince, reaching the capital in the twelfth month.
  • Taizong ordered him lodged at the Daxingshan Monastery. Later he "was brought in for an audience" in the palace and given forty lengths of coloured silk and monk's robes newly made in the palace; the five monks with him were given larger allowances, and he was repeatedly visited—his biography describes the welcome as "beyond the common measure."
  • In the third month of the third year of Zhenguan (629) Taizong ordered a translation bureau founded at the Daxingshan Monastery, gathering nineteen monks of great virtue. He also ordered Fang Xuanling, Pillar of State and Left Vice-Director, and Du Zhenglun, Supervisor of the Household of the Heir Apparent, to "assist in checking," and Xiao Jing, Chief Minister of the Court of the Treasury, to "superintend and protect"; all offices supplied it, and it lacked for nothing.
  • In the sixth year of Zhenguan (632) three works in thirty-five chapters were finished, and Taizong ordered ten copies of each made for circulation throughout the empire, with a preface by Li Baiyao, Mentor of the Heir Apparent.

Comparison: Prabhākaramitra was summoned by the court, received by Taizong in the palace, and given high officials including Fang Xuanling to help with his translations. In pattern this is very close to Alopen's "scriptures translated in the library, his Way inquired into within the palace." The difference is that the biography does not say Fang Xuanling went out of the city to meet him.

Case 2: the return of Xuanzang (645)

According to Huili and Yancong's Biography of the Tripiṭaka Master of the Great Ci'en Monastery, chapters 5 and 6:

  • When Xuanzang returned to Chang'an in the nineteenth year of Zhenguan (645), Taizong was in Luoyang preparing his eastern campaign against Goguryeo. Xuanzang sent a memorial ahead when he reached Shazhou, and Taizong ordered Fang Xuanling, regent of the western capital and Left Vice-Director, to have the offices concerned welcome him.
  • But Xuanzang, anxious to arrive before Taizong set out, travelled day and night and turned up suddenly at Caoshang in the western outskirts of Chang'an. "The officials did not know to receive him and had no time to set out their regalia," while the people who came running to look filled the streets, and he had to spend the night at Caoshang.
  • On the day jingzi of the first month (that is, bingzi; the Tang avoided the character bing and wrote jing), Fang Xuanling sent Houmochen Shi, General-in-Chief of the Right Guards, Li Shushen, Administrator of Yongzhou, and Li Qianyou, magistrate of Chang'an county, among others, to escort him from Caoshang into the city, where he was lodged at the Duting post-station.
  • The next day the monasteries of Chang'an held a great procession south of the Vermilion Bird Avenue to welcome the scriptures and images.
  • Xuanzang then hurried to Luoyang and was received by Taizong in the Yiluan Hall.

Comparison: Xuanzang was the most celebrated monk of the Tang, and Taizong ordered in advance that Fang Xuanling arrange his welcome. Even so, Fang Xuanling sent subordinates rather than going out himself, and the place of welcome was likewise the western outskirts (Caoshang), from which he entered the city. If the stele is right that Fang Xuanling personally "with the full honour guard in the western outskirts" received Alopen, it was a grade higher than the later welcome for Xuanzang.

Note: This comparison must be made with care. When Xuanzang returned Fang Xuanling was regent, Taizong himself was not in Chang'an, and Xuanzang arrived unexpectedly, so the welcome was improvised. When Alopen arrived Taizong was in Chang'an and things could be arranged at leisure. The circumstances differ, and one cannot simply say Alopen was treated better than Xuanzang.

Case 3: the Zoroastrian priest He Lu (631)

According to the Northern Song monk Zanning's Brief History of the Buddhist Clergy, last part:

In the fifth year of Zhenguan, the missionary mobad He Lu brought the Zoroastrian religion to the palace gate and reported it, and an edict ordered a Zoroastrian temple founded in the Chonghua Ward of Chang'an.

Zhipan's Fozu tongji, chapter 39 (Song), records much the same.

Comparison: He Lu was a Zoroastrian missionary (a muhu, mobad, is a Zoroastrian priest) who went in person to the palace gate to memorialise, and the court ordered a temple founded in Chang'an. The sources mention no ceremony of welcome. His surname He shows that he came from the Sogdian state of He (near Samarkand in today's Uzbekistan).

Note: In the same entry the Brief History says this Zoroastrian temple was "called the Daqin monastery, also the Persian monastery," plainly confusing the Zoroastrian and Luminous religions. Chen Yuan, in his 1923 study of Zoroastrianism in China, long ago pointed out that Song writers often confused the three foreign religions, so the details of this entry must be used with care. But at the least it shows that four years before Alopen, a missionary of a foreign religion had already won permission to found a temple in Chang'an by memorialising the throne.

Case 4: the Indian adept Nārāyaṇasvāmin (648)

According to the "Western Barbarians: India" section of the Old Book of Tang and the Zizhi Tongjian, chapters 199–200:

  • In the twenty-second year of Zhenguan (648) Wang Xuance, on embassy to India, captured Arunāśva and brought back an adept, Nārāyaṇasvāmin, who claimed to be two hundred years old and able to make an elixir of long life.
  • Taizong treated him with great reverence, lodged him inside the Jinbiao Gate to make the elixir, appointed Cui Dunli, Minister of War, to supervise him, and sent agents everywhere to collect ingredients.
  • The elixir was made; Taizong took it, and it did no good. After Taizong died, Gaozong sent the man home.

Comparison: this case has nothing to do with religious mission, but it shows Taizong's strong interest in "wonderful men and strange arts" from the west, and his willingness to treat them generously and set high officials over them.

The one item that stands out is "the chief minister with the full honour guard in the western outskirts"—and that is precisely the uncorroborated one.

5. Source criticism of the inscription

5.1 The inscription was written 146 years later

When Jingjing wrote in 781, Alopen had arrived a hundred and forty-six years earlier. His sources were probably the church's own tradition, copies of the edict kept in the monastery, and the original archives on which official works like the Tang Huiyao drew. The text of the 638 edict agrees closely with the Tang Huiyao, showing that Jingjing had a reliable text of it. But for scenes like "the full honour guard in the western outskirts" there was no archive to draw on.

5.2 Traces of rewriting

In his 1996 study "The Edict of 638 Allowing the Diffusion of Christianity in China," Antonino Forte argued that the Tang Huiyao preserves most of the original wording of the edict. Deeg went on to compare the two versions: the Tang Huiyao calls Alopen "a Persian monk," the stele "a man of great virtue from the kingdom of Daqin"; the Tang Huiyao has "brought scriptures and teaching from afar," the stele "brought scriptures and images"; and the monastery's name is changed back from "Persian monastery" to "Daqin monastery."

All these changes point one way: playing down the connection with Persia and playing up a source in "Daqin" (Rome, the holy lands of the west). Deeg sees in this the community's change of self-identification from "Persian" to "Daqin," against the background of the fall of the Sasanian dynasty and the suspicion of foreigners in the Tang after the An Lushan rebellion.

If even the wording of the edict was rewritten, the scenes are all the more likely to have been embellished.

5.3 Literary commonplaces

The stele says Alopen came "reading the omens of the azure clouds, he carried the true scriptures; watching the winds and the pitch-pipes, he hastened through hardship and danger," using the allusion from the Records of the Ten Continents attributed to Dongfang Shuo, of envoys from the Western Regions who came when the wind was right, bearing strange gifts. Deeg points out that this is allusion, and calls Alopen a "legendary" figure. Such commonplaces show that in describing Alopen's arrival, Jingjing was writing to the pattern of the Chinese histories' "distant peoples, drawn by our civilisation, come to court."

5.4 Likeness to Buddhist monks' biographies

Alopen's story (comes from afar with scriptures—welcomed with ceremony by the court—translates in the palace—the emperor issues an edict to spread the teaching) is very like, in structure, the legend of Kāśyapa Mātaṅga and Dharmaratna bringing scriptures east under Emperor Ming of the Han and translating them at the White Horse Monastery in Luoyang, and like the experiences of Prabhākaramitra and Xuanzang.

Note: Jingjing knew Buddhist literature well (he later translated a Buddhist sutra with Prajñā), and very likely borrowed the narrative pattern of the eminent monk coming east on purpose. This does not mean the events were invented; it means the inscription was written according to the conventions of religious history-writing of its day.

5.5 The practical aim of the stele

In 781, when the stele was raised, Dezong had only just taken the throne, and there was talk at court of reforming the clergy. In raising a stele that recounted the favour of every emperor since Taizong, the Luminous community had a clear practical aim: to remind the new emperor that the Luminous Religion had received the highest honours from the founding of the dynasty, a religion whose fortunes had gone hand in hand with the Tang's. The description of Alopen welcomed in the outskirts by the chief minister is the starting point of that narrative.

6. How to understand "the chief minister's welcome in the outskirts"

If we accept that the stele's description has some factual basis, Fang Xuanling's part in receiving Alopen can be understood in several ways.

6.1 Fang Xuanling's duties

In the Zhenguan years Fang Xuanling, as Left Vice-Director, headed the bureaucracy—"in charge of all offices, keeper of the rules"—and much business involving foreign religions passed through his hands: he assisted in checking Prabhākaramitra's translations, arranged Xuanzang's welcome home, and had Alopen's memorial translated and presented. He also chiefly compiled the Zhenguan Rites and knew guest ritual well. His receiving Alopen was less a special elevation of Alopen than the regular official for such matters doing his job.

6.2 Taizong's interest

Taizong was deeply interested in things from the west: in the Buddhism of Prabhākaramitra and Xuanzang, in Nārāyaṇasvāmin's elixir, in the geography and customs of the Western Regions. He later had Xuanzang write the Record of the Western Regions precisely to learn about them. Alopen brought a religion never seen before, and that Taizong wanted to learn about it himself fits his character.

6.3 Political considerations

In the ninth year of Zhenguan (635) the Tang had just defeated the Tuyuhun and was extending its reach into the Western Regions. In the fourth year (630) Taizong had already been acclaimed "Heavenly Qaghan" by the peoples of the northwest. To receive a religious leader from the distant west, showing that "distant peoples come to court," fitted the Tang idea of world order.

6.4 The needs of religious policy

In the first years of his reign Taizong had reformed Buddhism, strictly forbidding unauthorised ordination, while supporting the regular monastic community. Toward a newly arrived religion, the court's attitude was to examine first, permit afterwards, and bring it under management. "His scriptures were translated in the library; his Way was inquired into within the palace; knowing it deeply to be right and true, the emperor gave special command that it be taught" is exactly a process of examination: first translate the scriptures into Chinese, learn what the teaching is, confirm that it is harmless, then allow it to be taught. Seen this way, "inquiring into his Way" was less an act of reverence than an inspection.

7. Taizong's policy toward foreign religions: a summary

7.1 General principles

(1) A view of the world in which Chinese and barbarian are one

Late in life Taizong said of the secret of his success: "Since ancient times all have prized the Chinese and despised the barbarians; I alone love them as one, and so their tribes all rely on me as on father and mother" (Zizhi Tongjian, ch. 198, 21st year of Zhenguan). This attitude gave foreign religions political room to establish themselves in Chang'an.

(2) A view of religion that "sets up teachings to suit each place"

The edict of 638 opens: "The Way has no constant name; the sage has no constant form. Teachings are set up to suit each place, and quietly save the multitudes." That is: truth has no fixed name, the sage no fixed form; each place has its own way of teaching, and all quietly save the multitudes. This was the Tang court's official theoretical position on foreign religions: not to treat native religion as the only truth, but to grant that each teaching had its use. The line was quoted again and again afterwards and regarded as the charter of Tang religious tolerance.

(3) Management as "guests"

The early Tang placed both Buddhism and Daoism under the Office for Daoist Worship within the Court of State Ceremonial (after the first year of Yanzai under Wu Zetian, 694, Buddhist monks and nuns were transferred to the Bureau of National Sacrifices of the Ministry of Rites). The Court of State Ceremonial was the body in charge of foreign guests, and placing religion there reflected Buddhism's status as a "foreign teaching." As late as the fifth year of Huichang (845), when Wuzong destroyed Buddhism, the edict still said that monks and nuns should be placed under the Bureau of Receptions "to make plain that theirs is a foreign teaching," and at the same time ordered more than three thousand Daqin (Luminous) and muhu (Zoroastrian) clergy to return to lay life. The idea of a "foreign teaching" ran through the whole Tang.

(4) State control

Tolerance did not mean laissez-faire. The Tang kept all religions under strict state control:

  • founding a monastery required an imperial edict, and the monastery's name-board was granted by the court;
  • the number of clergy ordained was fixed by the court, as with Alopen's twenty-one;
  • unauthorised ordination was strictly forbidden.

7.3 The legal status of foreigners

The Tang Code with Commentary, "General Principles," lays down: "When persons from beyond the pale of civilisation offend against others of their own kind, each is dealt with according to his own customary law; when they offend against those of a different kind, they are dealt with by the statutes." That is, offences between foreigners of the same people were settled by their own customs; offences between different peoples were settled by Tang law. This principle guaranteed foreigners the right to keep their own customs in religion, marriage and the like, and was the legal basis on which foreign religions could continue within foreign communities.

7.4 The pattern for receiving foreign religious figures

Putting the cases together, the reception of foreign religious figures under Taizong falls roughly into this pattern:

  1. Prior notice: the newcomer first makes himself known to the court through a frontier prefecture, an envoy, or a memorial (Prabhākaramitra was reported by an envoy, Xuanzang sent a memorial ahead, and Alopen was met because "the emperor sent," showing it was known beforehand);
  2. The court sends officials to meet him: of a rank suited to his weight, up to a chief minister at the highest;
  3. Lodging in a monastery or guest house: Prabhākaramitra at the Daxingshan Monastery, Xuanzang at the Duting post-station and then the Hongfu Monastery;
  4. An audience and questioning by the emperor: to learn his doctrine and his origins;
  5. Translation of scriptures: organised or supplied by the court, often with high officials as supervisors;
  6. An edict after examination: confirming the teaching harmless, permitting it to be taught, or allowing a monastery and ordinations;
  7. Incorporation into state management: the monastery's name, the number of clergy and the responsible office all decided by the court.

Alopen went through all seven steps, and each at a fairly high rank. That is what was special about him: not that any one honour was without precedent or sequel, but that as the representative of a wholly new religion he received, within three years and in full, treatment and legal standing comparable to those of eminent Buddhist monks.
