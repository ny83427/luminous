Volume Seven　A-Lian

Chapter 43　Narsai

Now that morning Narsai came in at the monastery gate, passed Jingjing, and walked all the way to Yisi's small courtyard east of the monastery.

Yisi was in his room doing accounts.

Every month the monastery gave out gruel and medicine, and had to buy rice, firewood, medicinal herbs, and every item had to be recorded. These accounts had been Jingjing's charge, but Jingjing was busy translating, so Yisi had taken them over and kept them himself. He kept accounts slowly—read Tang characters slowly, laid out the counting rods slowly—and in a morning got down only a few lines. That morning he was entering two dan of millet bought the day before when he heard the courtyard gate creak.

He raised his head and saw Narsai standing at the door.

On the front of Narsai's grey robe a few dark spots had splashed. In his right hand he held that little knife, and what was on it had half dried. He came into the room, up to the low table, and laid the knife on Yisi's account book.

The knife lay across the line millet, two dan.

"I have killed Yonan," said Narsai.

Yisi sat there and did not move.

His right hand went to his waist. There was nothing there. The short mace hung on the wall, had hung there many years. His hand paused on his belt, and came back to the table.

"Where?"

"At the gate. Under the locust tree, by the well."

"Dead?"

"When I came away, not yet," said Narsai. "By now, probably."

Yisi stood up.

"He said something," said Narsai. "He said it in Tang. He said: don't hurt her. She is the Lord's sheep."

Yisi looked at him.

Narsai's face was as always—the high brow ridges, the straight nose, the eyes like two stones. When he had said that, he said one thing more. This, afterwards, everyone at the monastery came to know, word by word, as he recited the Law.

"I must obey the Patriarch's decision. But I cannot forgive Yonan."

Yisi walked past him to the door and stopped.

"Sit here," said Yisi. "Don't go out."

Narsai sat down on the mat by the low table, very upright, his two hands on his knees.

Yisi picked up the knife, put it in his sleeve, and went out. He pulled the courtyard gate shut behind him, and did not lock it.

A ring of people had already gathered under the locust tree.

Yisi pushed through them and saw Jingjing kneeling by the well, holding Yonan's hands in both of his. Yonan leaned against the well curb, his eyes already closed, his face perfectly clean, as if asleep. The bamboo basket lay by his hand, with eleven breads still in it.

Yisi squatted down before him.

He looked at Yonan a long time. He reached out and straightened the collar of Yonan's robe, smoothed it flat. Then he touched Yonan once on the forehead, once on the breast, once on each shoulder.

Jingjing did not raise his head.

Yisi did not call him either. He stood up and turned to face the ring of people.

The ward headman had already come. He was an old man of fifty or so who had been headman of the Yining Ward a dozen years and more; he knew Yisi, and knew Yonan, and every year at the Duanwu festival when the monastery gave out medicine he came for a packet. He stood at the edge of the ring, his face white, gripping a short stick, not knowing where to point it.

"Master Yi," said the headman, "this… this has to be reported to the county."

"Report it," said Yisi.

The headman was taken aback, probably not expecting him to answer so quickly.

"The one who killed him," said the headman, lowering his voice, "is one of your monastery's people?"

"He is."

"And where is he?"

"In my room."

The headman looked at him, opened his mouth, and no words came.

Yisi said: "There's an article in the code. When men from beyond the pale of civilisation, being of the same kind, offend against one another, each is judged according to the law of his own people."

Vice-Minister Liu had told him this article more than a dozen years before. That year, over the affair of the Pure Ones, he had been summoned to be questioned, and in a side hall of the Ministry of Rites Vice-Minister Liu had opened the General Principles of the code and pointed to this line and said: Master Yi, remember this article; one day you'll have need of it. He had not understood then what need he would ever have of it.

The headman said the line over twice in his mouth, understood it, and some of the white went out of his face.

"Then…"

"The dead man is one of ours," said Yisi, "and the man who killed him is one of ours. Report it to the county just so. If the county wants to come and question, I'll be waiting."

The headman went.

Later the county sent a clerk and a runner, who looked at the well, looked at the blood on the ground, asked a few questions, and wrote a few lines on a sheet of paper. As he left, the clerk cupped his hands to Yisi and said: "Since it is a case of those of the same kind offending against one another, to be judged by their own law, it is not convenient for the county to intervene." When he said not convenient, his tone was very courteous, like a man setting down a load.

Yisi cupped his hands too.

Yonan was buried that evening.

Narsai sat in Yisi's room all the while. No one went to call him, and he did not come out. Ishaq took him a bowl of rice and set it at the door, and when he went to collect it in the evening the rice was still there, not a mouthful touched, two flies settled on it.

When Yonan was buried, Yisi went back to his room.

Narsai was still sitting there, as upright as in the morning. It was already dark; no lamp was lit in the room.

Yisi lit the lamp.

The two of them sat with the low table between them. The account book still lay open on the table, and on the line millet, two dan there was a dark mark, left by the knife.

"He's buried," said Yisi.

Narsai nodded once.

Yisi said nothing more. He sat a while, closed the account book, and blew out the lamp.

The two of them sat a long time more in the dark. Then Yisi got up, went to the wall, and lay down on that old felt. Narsai still sat.

When it was light, Yisi woke and saw that Narsai was kneeling in the middle of the room, facing the wooden cross on the east wall. He had probably knelt all night.

The trial was on the third day.

The place was the back courtyard.

The jujube tree in the back courtyard was still there; its blossom had all fallen, and there was a layer of yellow-green litter on the ground, unswept. Sabrisho brought two monk-soldiers and carried in benches. The benches were the ones used at the wedding feast six days before; they had been carried back to the west wing only a few days ago, and now were carried out again. Sabrisho set them row by row, dead straight.

This time he did not step back to look, nor move the half inch.

Not many came. The three elders, Azarel, Marya and Samaan, sat in the front row. Of the seven who had come from Xizhou in those days, the five left—Ishaq, Babai, Gabriel, Sabrisho, Haddad—sat in the second row. Sargis stood at the courtyard gate. Jingjing sat to one side at a small table with paper spread before him and a brush in his hand. He was the recorder.

Yisi sat under the jujube tree, facing the benches.

Azarel was first of the three elders. But before the trial he had said one thing to Yisi: "The affairs of Chang'an are yours to direct." Yisi did not decline.

Narsai stood in the middle.

He still wore that grey robe; the few dark spots on its front had not been washed out.

Samaan spoke first.

He was Warden of the Rule, in charge of church law. Before he spoke he tucked his two hands into his sleeves as was his habit, and only when they were tucked did he open his mouth.

"By the rule," he said, "an ordained man who has blood on his hands is no longer an ordained man. He is deposed, never to be restored. He is cast out of the church, and may not receive the Eucharist until the hour of his death."

When he had said it he tucked his hands further into his sleeves.

Marya spoke next. His voice was hoarse; when he spoke it was like a door that had not been oiled.

"Church law is church law. The Tang have Tang law. He who kills shall die. If we shield him, we shield a killer. Today the county says it's not convenient to intervene; tomorrow, if a new magistrate comes and turns up the old case and says the hu monastery harboured a murderer, then it won't be only him who suffers."

He glanced at Narsai, and said again: "Hand him over to the authorities."

The courtyard was silent a while.

Sabrisho said: "Won't do."

Everyone looked at him.

Sabrisho's beard lay spread on his chest like a piece of felt. He said slowly: "Hand him over, and tomorrow they'll be saying all over Chang'an: a hu monk killed a hu monk over a woman. They'll say it for a hundred years."

He paused.

"Thirty years ago, that woman herself feared those words. She feared them thirty years."

Marya opened his mouth, and said no more.

Haddad stood up.

His hair was all white now, his skin still dark and shining, so that against the white hair it was like snow fallen on a block of ink. He said there was something he must tell them all.

"On the wedding day," said Haddad, "Yonan said something to me. He said: Narsai will come."

Narsai stood in the middle and did not move.

"I asked him what he'd do when he came," said Haddad. "He said: when he comes, I'll be there."

When Haddad had said it, he sat down.

Ishaq had kept his head down all along. He was fat, and when he sat on a bench both ends of it lifted. His shoulders were heaving, and he made no sound.

Babai could no longer see clearly; his eyes were half closed, and he said nothing.

Gabriel spoke. His left cheek wrinkled in the sun; his left eye was still a slit.

"Twenty years ago, in Xizhou," he said, "I asked Jingjing how to say absolution in Tang. He taught me."

He paused.

"I learned it, and never once used it."

He said no more. Jingjing, at the small table, held his brush still on the paper a long time before he wrote this sentence down.

Then Yisi questioned him.

He asked very briefly, as he always spoke. Jingjing afterwards recorded this passage without changing a word, adding nothing and taking nothing away.

"That knife—how many years have you used it?"

"Thirty-one."

"For what?"

"Trimming pens. Cutting paper."

"That morning, did you trim pens?"

"I did," said Narsai. "Three. I put them on Jingjing's table in the east wing."

Jingjing's hand shook. He looked down. On his table, in the brush stand, stood three reed pens, their tips trimmed very neatly. He had seen them that morning, and thought it an ordinary thing.

"Did Yonan try to get away?" asked Yisi.

"No."

Yisi looked at him.

"Did you want him to?"

Narsai did not answer at once.

The courtyard was very quiet. The leaves of the jujube tree rustled in the wind. At the courtyard gate Sargis shifted his weight.

After a long while, Narsai said: "Yes."

Yisi did not go on at once. He waited a while, and asked one thing more.

"The night before, he went to your door."

"He did."

"You knew it was him?"

"I knew."

"Why didn't you open the door?"

Narsai's lips moved. It was the first time in three days the people of the monastery had seen anything move in his face.

"I was afraid that if I opened the door," he said, "I couldn't do it."

Yisi stood up.

"You are deposed from your office," he said. "From today you do not go up to the lectern, do not read the scriptures, do not baptise, do not break the bread. The east wing, you will not enter again."

In the front row, Samaan nodded once.

"From the church, you are not cast out," said Yisi.

Samaan's head stopped.

"You'll still eat at the monastery, still sleep at the monastery," said Yisi. "The dead in Chang'an that no one gathers—you'll gather them. The frozen, the starved, those who die of sickness in the street—you'll gather them, you'll bury them. The orphans the monastery takes in, to be sent to Lingzhou, to Xizhou, to their kinsmen—you'll take them. When you've brought them there, you'll come back."

He paused.

"You will live."

Narsai raised his head and looked at him.

"Why not have me die?" asked Narsai.

Yisi did not answer at once. He thought of something Lord Guo had said to him twenty-one years before, in Lingzhou. He had just knocked down five men on the bank of the Yellow River, and none of the five had died. Lord Guo had looked at him and said: I don't want you to kill men. I want you to watch the road.

"I don't want you to die," said Yisi. "I want you to gather the dead."

He took the little knife from his sleeve. It had been wiped clean; he had wiped it himself. Its blade no longer than a finger, its handle ground bone gone yellow. He laid the knife on the bench before Narsai.

Someone in the courtyard drew a breath.

"Your knife," said Yisi. "Take it back. Use it to trim pens. For each dead man you gather, write down a name. If there's no name, write down what day, where, how old, what clothes. Write it in Tang."

Narsai looked at the knife.

"My Tang characters," he said, "are bad."

"Write them until they're good," said Yisi.

And he said: "The Eucharist you will not receive. On the day you die, I'll give it to you. If I die before you, Jingjing will give it to you."

In the front row, Samaan opened his mouth. Azarel reached out and laid a hand on his sleeve. Samaan shut his mouth.

Narsai stood there a long time.

Then he bent down, took up the knife in both hands, and put it in his sleeve. He raised his hands again and took from his neck the stole he had received at his ordination, folded it, folded it in four, folded it very neat, and held it out to Yisi with both hands.

Yisi took it.

Narsai turned and walked out of the back courtyard. As he went he passed under the jujube tree, past Jingjing's small table. He did not look at Jingjing. Jingjing did not look at him either.

That afternoon someone saw Narsai push a handcart out of the Yining Ward and go south. On the cart were a rolled straw mat and an iron spade.

In the evening he came back, and there was something more on the cart, wrapped in the mat. It was an old beggar who had died at the foot of a wall in the Yankang Ward, two days dead, and no one had taken him up.

He buried the old beggar outside the Kaiyuan Gate, at the edge of the monastery's burial ground, not far from Yonan's grave, with the young willow between.

When he had buried him he set up a wooden stake at the head of the grave. On the stake a few Tang characters were written in charcoal, written like two spears crossed, every stroke jabbed into the wood:

"Eleventh year of Dali, fifth month, twenty-second day. Foot of a wall, Yankang Ward. Old man, about sixty."

That night Yisi wrote to the Patriarchate.

He wrote in Syriac. His Syriac was what his father had taught him as a boy; in thirty years and more in Chang'an, apart from reading scripture, he had seldom put pen to it, and his letters were big and clumsy. He wrote a line, stopped and thought a while, wrote another. When he wrote something wrong, he scraped the parchment thin with his own knife and wrote it again.

The letter was not long.

He wrote of Yonan's marriage, of the three days, of the well under the locust tree. Wrote of Narsai, of the knife, of the words in Tang Yonan said as he died. He did not know how those Tang words should properly be written in Syriac; he thought a long time and spelled them out by sound, letter by letter. Wrote of the county, of those of the same kind who offend against one another are each judged by the law of their own people. Wrote of the trial, and how he had judged it.

At the end he wrote one line:

"What the Patriarch allowed was not wrong. The wrong was in Chang'an. Chang'an is in my charge. How it is to be dealt with, I will abide by the Patriarch."

He folded the letter, sealed it with wax, laid it on the table, and set a bowl on it.

Then he took out Narsai's stole, folded in four, laid it beside the letter, looked at it a while, and put it away again in the cupboard.

Jingjing was in the east wing.

He made a fair copy of all he had recorded that day, from beginning to end. When he had finished it was nearly dawn. He put down his brush and sat there looking out of the window. Outside it was grey; in the eastern sky there was a little white.

He got up and went to the corner.

Against the wall in the corner leaned a wooden board. The board was very old, its edges worn round, its face scored with scratches, left by writing and wiping and writing again. It was the board he had used twenty years before in Xizhou to teach the seven elders Tang. Water, fire, gate, road, hungry, cold, sick, peace. In those days he had written the characters very large, every stroke separate, and had them trace them. It was from this board that Yonan had traced that character heart in his sand tray, over and over.

When they came to Chang'an he had never thrown it away.

He carried the board to the table and wiped its face clean with a damp cloth. When it was wiped, he waited for it to dry.

In the brush stand stood those three reed pens, their tips trimmed very neatly.

He looked at them a while, and did not touch them. He took up his own brush and dipped it in ink.

He wrote a character.

He wrote it very large, every stroke separate, like the characters he had written twenty years before in Xizhou, as if writing it for someone who could not read to trace.

父. Father.

All his life he had called Yisi Pa, called him so thirty years, and would go on calling him so. This character he had never called anyone. He wrote it only this once.

He looked at the character, looked a long time. The ink slowly dried, dried to a very deep black.

Then he turned the board over, the character to the wall, and leaned it back in its old place in the corner.
