Volume Five　Chang'an

Chapter 22　Fifty

In the second month of the first year of Qianyuan, the willows outside the Kaiyuan Gate put out buds.

That year the era name was changed. The old one had been Zhide, the new one was Qianyuan, and when people in the city spoke of it they often still said the third year of Zhide, and only corrected themselves afterwards. The edict changing the era was posted in the gate passage, and stayed posted half a month until the rain had washed half of it out, and no one replaced it.

From the first month on, Old Duan went out to the Kaiyuan Gate every few days.

The Kaiyuan Gate was the northernmost of the gates on the west side of Chang'an, and every road going west left from it. Outside the gate stood a stone tablet carved with the words To Anxi in the west, nine thousand nine hundred li. Old Duan could not read many characters, but these he knew; Yisi had taught him. Yisi said the tablet should by rights have said ten thousand li, and it said nine thousand nine hundred for luck, so that the traveller would feel he had not yet come to the end.

Old Duan would stand by the tablet and look west. The road was a dirt road, stretching to the edge of the sky. On the road there were caravans, post horses, men with carrying poles, men driving sheep. He looked them over one by one, and when he had finished, went back into the city.

Xu Jing had sent a letter by a caravan coming from the west, saying they would set out in the first month. Babai had written the letter, in very proper characters, and at the end there was a crooked line that Jingjing had added himself: "Pa, I don't drink cold water."

Yisi read that line, read it a long time, folded the letter and put it inside his coat.

Old Duan asked him: "What does the letter say?"

Yisi said: "He's been drinking cold water."

They arrived on the nineteenth of the second month.

That day Old Duan was standing by the tablet as usual. Near noon a line of dust rose on the road to the west, and under the dust was a long string of camels, and before the camels were horses, and before the horses was one man on a thin horse, his right hand tucked in his sleeve.

Old Duan narrowed his eyes and looked a while, then turned and ran back into the city.

When he got to the Yining Ward, Yisi was minding the cauldron at the monastery gate. The gruel had just been cooked and was steaming, and several dozen people were lined up at the gate.

"They're here," said Old Duan.

The ladle in Yisi's hand did not stop. He ladled that spoonful of gruel into an old woman's bowl, filled it, handed the ladle to a young man from the ward who was helping, and said: "You serve. One ladle each. Not more, not less."

The young man took the ladle.

Yisi untied the cloth round his waist, folded it, and set it on the edge of the stove. He went to the well, drew half a bucket of water and washed his hands, washed them very carefully, under the nails too. Then he went back to the front courtyard, took down the old leather armour hanging on the wall, looked at it, and hung it back up. In his grey robe, he went out of the gate.

Old Duan followed him a few steps and could not help saying: "Get a move on, will you."

Yisi said: "I am."

He did not walk fast at all.

The caravan had halted just inside the Kaiyuan Gate.

Xu Jing was first off his horse. He was thinner and darker, the skin of his face flaking white from the wind, and when he saw Old Duan he broke into a grin that creased his whole face. He drew that right hand with two missing fingers out of his sleeve and clasped Old Duan's hand with it, clasped it very tight; neither spoke, and they held on a long while before Xu Jing said: "Old Duan, you waited in Chang'an, and you really did wait."

Old Duan said: "I keep my word."

Behind Xu Jing were the monk-soldiers. Twenty-four, four squads, six to a squad, in black, small round shields on their backs, leaning on shoulder-high staves shod with iron. Their leader Sargis sat his horse, tall and strong, a head above the men beside him. He looked at the city gate, looked at the street inside it, looked at the half-fallen ward walls on either side of the street, and his face showed nothing.

Behind the monk-soldiers were the seven teachers in grey robes. Ishaq's camel was the most conspicuous, because Ishaq was the fattest.

Further back was a boy.

The boy was on a small horse, a good deal taller than when he had left; his legs had grown longer, his neck longer, his face burned a dark red. He wore an old lined robe whose sleeves had grown short, showing a length of wrist. Round his neck hung a cord, and on the cord a small wooden cross.

He saw Yisi.

Yisi stood at the side of the street and did not come forward.

Jingjing got down from his horse. He got down very fast; his feet touched the ground, he ran two steps forward, and stopped. He stood there, still three or four paces from Yisi.

The two of them looked at each other.

There was wind in the gate passage, and it set the hem of Jingjing's robe fluttering.

Jingjing saw the scar on his father's face. From a little left of the middle of his brow it ran up onto his forehead; it was new, still red, with the marks of the stitches along its edges. Half his left eyebrow was missing.

Yisi saw Jingjing's wrists. They were very thin—a little thicker than when he left, but still thin. On the backs of his hands were the marks left by healed chilblains.

Jingjing took another step forward.

Yisi took a step forward too.

Between them there was still one step.

"On the road," said Yisi. "Did you drink cold water?"

Jingjing's mouth moved.

"Once," he said.

"Did you get the runs?"

"I did."

"Eat ginger."

Jingjing nodded. His eyes had gone red; he blinked hard, once, and did not let it fall.

Yisi raised his hand. It rose halfway, paused, and did not come down on Jingjing's head. He reached past and took the reins of Jingjing's little horse, and held them in his fist.

"Come on," he said. "There's gruel in the pot."

He led the horse into the city. Jingjing walked beside him, at the same pace as him, not fast, not slow.

Old Duan stood behind watching a while, then turned his face away and said to Xu Jing: "That's how he is."

Xu Jing said: "The boy's the same. In Xizhou he looked east every day. Ask him what he was looking at and he'd say the wind."

That evening a great many people came to the monastery.

The seven teachers, the twenty-four monk-soldiers, Xu Jing, Old Duan, and a few old believers from the ward. The main hall had not been repaired yet, and the hole in its roof was covered with straw mats, so everyone crowded into the side rooms, and those who could not fit stood in the courtyard.

Yisi greeted them one by one.

Narsai made him a bow, very correctly, and not one word more. Ishaq hugged him, hugged him so that the wound in his back went tight, and he made no sound. Babai took his hand and said all those letters he had written for the boy; the boy's characters were better than his, only he would not write more. Gabriel smiled at him, the left side of his face wrinkled, the right side whole, so that when he smiled half of it was a smile and half was something else. Sabrisho asked him who was keeping the monastery's rule these days. Haddad asked him the price of grain in the city.

Yonan was last.

Yonan stood at the door and did not come in. His face was still as young as ever, beardless, but his hands were an old man's hands, folded before him.

Yisi went over.

The two had known each other before. In those days one had been the bishop, the other a priest. More than ten years ago.

Yonan looked at him, at the scar on his face, looked a while and said: "He's grown up."

Yisi said: "Mm."

Yonan said again: "He teaches Tang speech well. Of the seven of us I learned slowest, and he was never impatient."

Yisi said: "He takes after his ma."

Yonan said nothing more. He bowed his head slightly, turned and went out. He went to the back courtyard; Yisi heard water being drawn at the well.

Jingjing sat in the corner hugging his knees, watching all the while.

He asked nothing.

Supper was mutton broth, stewed most of the day. Yisi could not stew; an old believer from the ward had done it. The elder Ahad had stayed in Balkh and not come back; Yisi had heard, and said nothing, only when he took up the broth he held the bowl a little longer than usual.

Before they ate, Yisi set two bowls beside himself.

One was Jingjing's. One was of coarse earthenware, with a chip in its rim and a pinch of earth in it.

Jingjing looked at that bowl.

"Du De's," said Yisi.

Jingjing had heard the name. Before he left, Pa had told him about Changshan, told him about an old soldier who changed his dressings. He did not know what happened afterwards.

He did not ask. He moved his own bowl over beside that one, moved it until the two bowls touched, and only then picked up his chopsticks.

At table they spoke Tang. The seven teachers' Tang, after most of a year's study in Xizhou, was good in some and bad in others. Ishaq wanted more broth and said "Mo pa, another bowl," and the whole room laughed. Ishaq laughed too, and when he had finished laughing he said: "I know I said it wrong. I just like the word."

After the start of spring, Chang'an was short of grain.

The city had been recovered, but the fields had not been sown. Last autumn most of the crops of Guanzhong had rotted in the fields with no one to bring them in. When the rebels left, they had plundered once more. By the second and third months the price of rice in the city had risen to several hundred cash a dou, and in the wards some families began pulling down their door panels to trade for rice.

The government opened its granaries.

The place where the granary was opened was outside the East Market. The day people went to draw rice, the line stretched from the gate of the East Market all the way to the Chunming Gate, several li. The official in charge stood at the granary gate, gave the word, and the gates were opened.

What came after, no one could say clearly.

Some said that the people at the back heard there was not enough rice and pushed forward; some said someone at the front drew his rice and would not move, and those behind shoved him; others said no one pushed at all, there were simply too many, and they fell of themselves. However it was, all at once there was chaos at the granary gate. The people surged forward like water bursting a dike. The official in charge stood on the steps and tried to shout and could not, and was squeezed off the steps.

When the crowd had gone, a dozen and more people lay on the ground. Old people, children, and that official.

The official was a revenue clerk of the capital prefecture; what his surname was, afterwards no one remembered. When they lifted him up his official cap was gone, one shoe was gone, and he still had a roll of registers clenched in his hand.

The man in charge of relief was an official named Pei.

Official Pei was about forty, not tall, with very high brow ridges and deep-set eyes, so that when he looked at you it was as if he were looking out from behind a mountain ridge. He was the owner of that roll of registers. He had had the registers drawn up, drawn up over half a month, ward by ward, and when they were done gave them to the revenue clerk to issue the rice.

After the clerk died, Official Pei did not leave his house for three days.

On the fourth day he went to the Yining Ward.

He had heard people say it. People said there was a hu monastery in the Yining Ward that gave out gruel at its gate every day, had done so all winter, and not one person had been crushed to death. He did not quite believe it, and went to see for himself.

When he arrived it was morning. More than a hundred people were lined up at the monastery gate, a long line, but a very orderly one. There were two cauldrons at the gate; by one stood a hu, by the other a scar-faced old soldier. The people in line came up to the cauldron, held out a bowl, got one ladle, and went off. Not more, not less.

Official Pei stood across the street and watched all morning.

Towards noon the gruel ran out. The hu scraped the last of it from the cauldron and gave it to a child, then straightened up and looked once across the street.

Official Pei went over.

"Are you the Great Virtue Yi?"

Yisi said: "Yisi."

Official Pei looked at him a while and said: "A hundred-odd people. How do you line them up?"

Yisi said: "A hundred-odd people are easy to line up."

"And six thousand?"

Yisi did not answer.

Official Pei said: "The Ministry of Revenue has allotted rice. Spring famine—I've registered the families in the city short of grain, more than six thousand souls. The rice is in the granary, and it can't be given out. Last time it was given out, a dozen and more died."

When he came to the word died, the eyes under those brow ridges moved.

Yisi said: "I don't know how."

Official Pei said: "You've a hundred-odd here, and not one died."

Yisi said: "With a hundred-odd, no one dies. With six thousand, they will."

Official Pei said nothing more. He stood there looking at those two empty cauldrons, looked a long time. Then he cupped his hands to Yisi and went.

Yisi watched him go. The man walked a little lopsided, as if the sole of one shoe had worn thin.

That night Yisi did not sleep.

He lay in the side room listening to the snoring of the monk-soldiers in the courtyard. Twenty-four men in two tents, snoring now here, now there, like two herds of oxen fighting. Jingjing slept beside him, slept very deeply, on his side, one hand clutching the wooden cross round his neck.

Yisi thought about that crowd at the granary gate.

When there are many people, they become water. Water runs downhill; people push forward. The ones in front cannot keep their feet, and the ones behind cannot either. Out of a thousand, let one panic, and the thousand panic.

He had seen it in the field. Fighting goes stroke by stroke; a rout goes swathe by swathe.

He could not think of an answer.

Near dawn, he got up.

The monastery's morning praises were said under the jujube tree in the back courtyard. The main hall had not been repaired, its roof let in the wind, and Gabriel said under the jujube tree was just as good; the Lord did not mind.

That morning it was Gabriel's turn to expound the scripture.

What he expounded was the scripture of the Dharma King Mojuci. He sat on a stone under the jujube tree with the scroll spread on his knees; the scroll was in Syriac, and he read it and expounded it in Tang as he went. His Tang was the best of the seven, only he liked to use hard words, and having used them, explained them again himself.

He came to that day when the Lord was in a desert place, and five thousand were following him. It was late, and the disciples said: send them away, let them go into the villages and buy themselves food. The Lord said: give ye them to eat.

The disciples said: we have only five loaves and two fishes.

When Gabriel had read this far he paused, raised his head and looked at the people sitting before him. Monk-soldiers, teachers, a few old believers from the ward, Jingjing, and Old Duan, standing at the very back leaning against the wall. Old Duan did not believe, but he came every morning, to hear the fun, he said.

Gabriel bent his head again and read on.

"And he commanded them to make all sit down by companies upon the green grass. And they sat down in ranks, by hundreds, and by fifties."

Yisi raised his head.

Gabriel was still reading on. The Lord took the five loaves and the two fishes, looked up to heaven and blessed, and brake the loaves and gave them to the disciples to set before them. And they did all eat, and were filled.

Yisi did not hear what came after.

He was thinking of the sentence before. Sit down.

People standing will push. People sitting cannot. A man sitting on the ground who wants to get up has first to put his hand down, first to bend his legs. In that putting down and bending, half the panic is gone.

By fifties.

Fifty people one man can watch. Out of fifty, who is too hungry to stand, who has a child in her arms, who is crying, who is looking ahead—one man can see them all.

Six thousand people one man cannot watch. But six thousand is a hundred and twenty fifties.

Gabriel finished reading, closed the scroll, raised his head, and saw Yisi looking at him.

"Yisi," said Gabriel. "Are you doing sums?"

Yisi said: "A hundred and twenty men."

That morning at the hour of si, Yisi went to find Official Pei.

Official Pei was in a side hall of the capital prefecture, with registers piled on his table. When he had heard Yisi out, for a long time he made no sound.

"A hundred and twenty men?"

"A hundred and twenty," said Yisi. "Each in charge of fifty."

"Where would you get a hundred and twenty men?"

Yisi counted them out for him one by one. Seven teachers, twenty-four monk-soldiers—that made thirty-one. Old believers from the ward who could stand steady and speak clearly, about twenty. Still seventy short.

"In Lord Guo's camp," said Yisi, "there are old soldiers."

Official Pei said: "Soldiers? Soldiers in charge of famished people—aren't you afraid they'll lay hands on them?"

Yisi said: "Old soldiers don't lay hands on people. The young ones do."

He went to Old Duan. Old Duan went to Xu Jing. Xu Jing went to Hou Siyuan and Cheng Chongyi. Hou Siyuan and Cheng Chongyi went round the camp and came back with more than fifty men, all past thirty—some short an ear, some lame in a leg, some short of nothing, only not fond of talking. Another dozen and more were found in the ward, and that made up a hundred and twenty.

Yisi called the hundred and twenty men to the back courtyard of the monastery, stood under the jujube tree and told them the rules.

There were only a few.

First sit down, then the rice is given. Anyone not sitting gets none.

Each man is in charge of fifty. The fifty sit in one block, sit in front of you. You watch them, they watch you.

The rice is brought forward from the back; no one is to come to the front to take it.

No shouting. If anything's wrong, raise your hand.

Sargis stood among the men, and when he had heard it all, asked: "And if someone won't sit?"

Yisi said: "Wait for him to sit."

"And if he never sits?"

"Wait as long as it takes."

Sargis thought and nodded, and said a few words in Syriac to his monk-soldiers. Yisi heard; what he said was: hold, and there'll be no need to fight.

The day the rice was given out was the ninth of the third month.

The place chosen was in the south of the city, an open stretch west of the Qujiang Pool. It had once been a garden; the garden's owner had fled, the walls had fallen, the flowers and trees inside had been cut down for firewood, and only a waste ground was left. Grass had grown on it, and now that it was spring the grass was green.

Before it was light the people came.

At first it was still chaos. People came from every side and pushed forward, pushed towards the rice carts. The carts stood at the north end of the open ground, twenty of them, the sacks of rice piled high on them.

The hundred and twenty men stood spread out, a dozen and more paces between each.

Old Duan stood in the very front row. He unbuckled his sword and handed it to Xu Jing, and stood empty-handed before the crowd.

Someone pushed up right in front of him.

Old Duan did not push him back. He sat down himself first, plumped down on the grass, crossed his legs, and looked up at the man.

"Sit," he said.

The man stood stunned.

"Sit down," said Old Duan. "Sit down, and the rice gets given out."

The man looked at him, looked at the rice carts behind him, looked left and right. A dozen paces to the left, a hu monk in black had sat down too, his staff laid across his knees. A dozen paces to the right, an old soldier missing an ear had sat down too. Further right, a fat hu in a grey robe had sat down too, and was smiling at him and saying something. It took the man a long time to make out that it was mo pa.

The man sat down.

The people behind him saw him sit, and sat too.

One sat, two sat, a whole swathe sat. The sound of their sitting down was very soft, like wind passing over a wheatfield.

That day Jingjing stayed by Yisi, running back and forth with messages. Afterwards he wrote in his journal that he did not remember much of that day. He remembered only that, looking south from the rice carts, the ground was covered with sitting people, block by block, a man in charge sitting at the front of each block, like rows and rows of crops growing in a field. He remembered too that it was very quiet that day. Six thousand people, so quiet you could hear the sacks being unloaded from the carts.

He remembered an old woman too. She was sitting in the block Xu Jing was in charge of, and when the rice was brought to her she did not take it, but first wept. Xu Jing squatted down and asked her what was wrong. She said: last winter, her man had died at the granary gate.

Xu Jing laid that dou of rice in her lap, pressed it with the hand that was missing two fingers, and said: "Sit, don't hurry. There's enough for everyone."

They gave out rice from the hour of mao to the hour of shen.

Six thousand one hundred and more souls, one dou each.

Not one person fell.

Official Pei watched from beginning to end.

He stood by the rice carts from the hour of mao to the hour of shen and did not move. He held the registers in his hands, and the names in the registers were ticked off one by one, and all the while he watched the brush moving across the paper.

When it was done the people dispersed. On the grass they left patches of flattened grass, block by block, so that you could still see the shape where each fifty had sat.

Official Pei came over to Yisi.

He looked at Yisi a long time. The eyes under those brow ridges were not as they had been that morning.

"This method," he said. "Where did it come from?"

Yisi said: "A book."

"What book?"

Yisi thought and said: "A book about a man who gave a meal to five thousand people."

Official Pei said: "He could afford to feed five thousand?"

"He only had five loaves."

Official Pei said nothing.

The wind blew over from the Qujiang Pool, with a little moisture in it, a little smell of green grass. Some way off, Old Duan and Xu Jing were sitting side by side on the grass, one cursing the other, the other laughing. Hou Siyuan had lain down, his two hands under his head, looking at the sky.

Official Pei stood a while, rolled up his registers and tucked them under his arm.

Then he sat down on the grass.
