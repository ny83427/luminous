Volume Two　Li Guangbi

Chapter 8　Du De

In the fifth month, the armies of Guo and Li fought Shi Siming again at Jiashan.

It was a great battle. Shi Siming lost forty thousand men. He himself fell from his horse, lost his helmet and ran out of his shoes, and made his way back to camp after dark barefoot, leaning on a broken spear, and that night fled into the city of Boling. When the people of a dozen commanderies of Hebei heard of it they killed the officials the rebels had set over them and opened their gates to the Tang. The armies of Guo and Li surrounded Boling, and it looked as though it must fall.

Everyone in camp said that if the fighting went on like this, by autumn they would be at Fanyang, and smash An Lushan's den.

In the middle of the sixth month a horseman came from the west. He was starved past recognition, and his horse was starved too; when he came through the camp gate and dismounted his legs gave way and he went down on his knees. He had ridden all the way from Hedong changing horses, and the dispatch he carried was wrapped in three layers of oilcloth.

Tong Pass had fallen.

Geshu Han's two hundred thousand had been destroyed in one battle on the western plain at Lingbao, and Geshu Han himself had been tied up by his own officers and handed over to An Lushan. With Tong Pass gone, Chang'an had no door.

In the command tent Guo Ziyi and Governor Li sat facing each other the whole night. The guards outside heard them speak a few times, never loudly, and then there was no sound at all.

At daybreak the order came down: lift the siege of Boling; the whole army to withdraw west into Jingxing.

Two days later a second horseman came, with news worse than the first: in the middle of the night the Sage had gone out through the Yanqiu Gate and fled west. Where he had gone, nobody knew.

A withdrawal is not a flight.

But what a withdrawal fears most is turning into one. Tens of thousands of men and thousands of baggage carts, falling back from the Hebei plain into the Taihang mountains under the rebels' very eyes—and when you fall back, someone always follows.

Shi Siming did follow.

He had been besieged in Boling more than a month, and was full of bottled-up fury. As soon as the Tang army withdrew he came out after it with the horse he had left in the city, chasing and biting all the way, catching hold of a stretch of the column and swallowing it.

Governor Li put his crossbowmen at the front. On the first day he sent a thousand crossbowmen ahead into the hills to take the ridges on both sides of the eastern mouth of Jingxing; the carts went their way, and the crossbowmen were not to move. Some in camp grumbled that the men at the back were getting cut to pieces while the crossbowmen ran off first. The talk reached Governor Li's ears, and he paid it no attention.

The rearguard was chosen by lot.

This was an old rule of Governor Li's army. The rearguard was neither named nor called for. Name a company, and it would resent it, and the others would wonder what grudge the governor bore against it; call for volunteers, and the men who stepped forward would mostly be reckless brutes who did not care if they lived, and reckless brutes can charge but cannot hold. So every day at the fifth watch the head of each company came before the command tent and drew one arrow from a quiver. The arrows stood head down so they could not be seen; of thirty-odd, only one had red cloth wound round its head. The company that drew the red cloth went last that day. A company that had once drawn the red cloth did not draw again.

The company that drew the red cloth on the first day came back that evening with half its men. The company on the second day came back with three.

At the fifth watch on the third day, Du De stood in the line of company heads.

He was not a squad leader. His company's commander had been killed by a stray arrow the night before, and his second was wounded too and lay on a cart unable to get up. Of those left, Du De was the oldest and had served the longest, so they all looked to him. So he went.

That day the wind came from the north, hard and dry, blowing sand into everyone's eyes. Two torches burned before the command tent, their flames tugged every which way by the wind. The provost in charge of the quiver held it in his arms and went down the line one man at a time. There were fewer arrows in the quiver than the day before: two fewer.

The men in front drew, and one after another let out a breath, and none of them spoke; they only handed their arrows back to the provost.

When it came to Du De, he put his hand in, took one at random, and drew it out.

Under the torches, a strip of red cloth was wound on its head, fluttering in the wind.

The provost glanced at it, scratched a mark on the wooden tablet in his hand, said "Du De's company," and went on with the quiver to the next man. The next man did not draw. The arrows left in the quiver were no longer needed.

Du De put the arrow in his own quiver.

He went back to his company. All thirty-odd were awake, sitting on the ground watching him. He said nothing, drew the arrow from his quiver, showed it to them once, and put it back.

Someone cursed. Someone said: "Old Du, that kick of yours goes in backwards. Pick an arrow, and your hand's just as true."

A few of them laughed, very briefly. Du De laughed a little too, and said: "Pack up."

Before daylight the things sent down from headquarters arrived. Every man got two more quivers of arrows; a dozen long spears were sent too, and several suits of good armour taken off the wounded; whoever lacked an arm guard, whoever was short a helmet, was made up item by item. The baggage officer who brought them counted them out and left, and did not ask them to sign for anything.

Before it was fully light someone in camp called the withdrawal. The baggage carts moved west one after another, their wheels cutting deep ruts in the dirt road. The horses took fright and screamed together. The men in front went too fast, the men behind could not keep up, and the column stretched longer and longer, like a rope pulled thin.

Du De's company went last. There were thirty-odd of them, old and young, their armour newly made up, their quivers full.

At noon the carts ahead stopped.

Du De rode forward a stretch and saw why. A gully lay across the way, with only one wooden bridge over it, narrow, wide enough for one cart. A dozen baggage carts were all jammed at the end of the bridge, some trying to get ahead, shaft locked against shaft; one cart's wheel had already caught in a gap between the planks, and the driver was whipping his beasts and the beasts would not move, so the men behind came up and pushed, and the more they pushed the tighter it jammed. Two soldiers had left the carts and were trying to wade across the gully.

Du De drove his horse to the end of the bridge and roared at the top of his voice:

"One cart at a time!"

That voice of his he had shouted out on the pitch outside Jiumen; it was coarse and loud, and the end of the bridge fell silent at once.

"The ones at the back, ten paces back," he said. "That stuck one—unhitch the beasts and carry it. The next man who pushes, I cut down first."

The two soldiers wading in the gully looked at him, and climbed back out.

The carts went onto the bridge one at a time. The stuck one, seven or eight men carried over by the wheels. On the far side of the bridge there was a pass, and on the ridges either side of it figures could be dimly seen: Governor Li's crossbowmen. Beyond the pass was Jingxing. Once inside Jingxing, Shi Siming's horse would have no room to work.

Du De looked behind him.

On the horizon behind them a line of yellow dust had risen. The dust came closer and rose higher, and in it horses' heads and spear points could dimly be seen.

He looked at the end of the bridge again. A dozen carts still to go.

He reckoned it. A dozen carts across the bridge: the time it takes to drink a cup of tea.

"Draw in the rope," he said.

The men around him looked at him.

"Draw the rope tight," said Du De. "Don't let the carts get out of order."

Someone asked: "Old brother Du—are we really going to hold?"

Du De did not turn round. He brought his horse's head round to face that line of yellow dust behind. In his hand was an old lance, its shaft blackened with sweat, its point ground bright as snow.

"Hold for a cup of tea," he said.

The thirty-odd men drew up before a bank of earth on the east side of the gully.

The bank was not high, a little more than half a man's height—an old field ridge the farmers had built long ago, gullied and pitted by rain. Du De had two baggage carts that had broken down on the road pushed over and laid across in front of the bank, the wheels taken off and the bodies turned over: a low wall. Behind the cart wall they drove a few sharpened stakes, slanting outward.

There was a young soldier in the company, sixteen or seventeen, only drafted in a few days ago, not yet grown to his full height, his armour hanging loose on him. He came running with an armful of stakes, his hands shaking so that they kept falling.

Du De glanced at him, took the stakes from him, and drove them into the ground.

"You go over the bridge," said Du De.

The boy stood still. "Uncle Du, I can shoot—"

"I know you can shoot," said Du De. "Go over the bridge. Follow the last cart. Until that cart's into the pass, don't let it stop."

The boy did not move.

"Go."

The boy bit his lip, turned and ran toward the bridge, ran a few steps, and looked back once. Du De was not looking at him any more.

By the time all this was done, the pursuit had arrived.

The horse came first, two or three hundred, charging hard. They saw the baggage carts by the gully and the few dozen men behind the cart wall, and came on howling, meaning to swallow this cut-off tail in one bite.

Du De stood behind the cart wall and waited.

"Wait till they're close before you shoot," he said. "Shoot the horses. When the horses go down, the ones behind get in a tangle."

The horsemen came to fifty paces. Forty. Thirty.

"Shoot!"

A dozen bows sounded at once. Several horses in the lead were hit, their forelegs buckled, and they went down on their knees; their riders were flung off, and the horses behind could not stop and crashed into those in front, and all at once it was a heap.

But there were more behind.

The horsemen went round the fallen horses and came up to the cart wall. A horse crashed into it, and the wall shook but did not go over. One horse was run through the chest by a stake and reared up screaming and threw its rider; the man fell inside the cart wall, and before he could get up an old soldier had cut into his neck.

Du De's lance went out and took a horseman in the thigh, came back, went out again, and took a horse in the eye.

In front of the cart wall men and horses were jammed in a mass.

Du De did not remember how many thrusts he made.

He remembered only that at first there were men all round him, men on the left and men on the right, and when he thrust, the man beside him cut. Later there were fewer on the left, fewer on the right. Later still he turned his head to look and there were only seven or eight men left beside him.

A horseman burst in through a gap in the cart wall and swung his sword. Du De blocked with the shaft of his lance, and the shaft snapped with a crack. The blade came on down and struck his left shoulder. He felt the shoulder go heavy, and then hot.

He threw away the broken lance, drew his sword, and cut at the horseman's horse's legs. The horse went down, and so did the man. He threw himself on him, one cut, then another.

When he stood up he felt another pain, at his waist. He looked down and saw a length of spearhead sticking out of his side. He reached to pull it out and could not. He turned and cut, and caught the arm of the man behind him. The man let go, and the shaft stayed in him.

He leaned on the cart wall, breathing hard.

He looked west once. On the wooden bridge over the gully there were no carts any more. On the far side the last baggage cart was moving into the pass, the flag at its tail fluttering in the wind. Beside the cart ran a small figure, running and looking back.

Over.

He thought: a cup of tea—that's about it.

He had not fallen yet.

He leaned on the cart wall, one hand pressed on the spear shaft in his side and the other holding his sword, and looked at the tangle of men and horses in front of him. The pursuers were still coming this way, but slower now. Too many dead men and dead horses lay before the cart wall; horses could not get over, and men had to climb over the dead.

Another man climbed over. Du De cut at him and missed his aim, and the blade struck the man's helmet with a clang and turned its edge. The man's spear came at him and went into his thigh.

He cut again. This time he hit.

He felt his legs go, and slid slowly down the cart wall, and lay flat on his back on the ground.

The sky was grey. The wind came from the north and blew on his face, very hard, very dry. There was sand in the wind, and the smell of blood, and horse sweat, and burnt wood. His ears were humming, as if a great many people were shouting a long way off.

He thought of that threshing floor outside Jiumen.

That day he had lain like this too. The ball had flown back over his head, and he had fallen on the gleaming ground and seen nothing, only the sky. The sky was blue. At the edge of the pitch there was first a roar of shouting, and halfway through it suddenly broke off, so quiet you could hear the straw rustling. He lay there not knowing whether it had gone in, and just lay, and waited.

Then the drum had sounded.

He thought of the foreign monk, too, helping him to sit on the stone in the courtyard, the moon very bright. He had said, after this, in battle, if you hear somebody shout Du De, don't take it for anybody else. The monk had said, I'll remember.

He lay there, waiting for that drum.

Another spear came at him.

Du De raised his sword to block it. The sword was half an inch slow.

Yisi heard about it the next day.

That day he was in Jingxing, going west with Guo Ziyi's central army. The road in the hills was narrow and the column moved slowly, going a while, stopping a while. While they rested, Governor Li's men came up from behind, company after company, squeezing past Guo Ziyi's soldiers.

Yisi sat on a stone by the road and watched them pass.

He was looking for one face in the crowd. The face was past forty, deeply lined, a little stooped, walking without lifting its feet much, as if not to waste the effort. Since that day at Jiumen he had seen it several times in Governor Li's camp, and every time, from a long way off, the man had jerked his chin at him.

One company passed; not there. Another passed; not there either.

The last to come were crossbowmen, twenty or thirty men, crossbows on their backs, their faces grey with dust. At once Yisi saw Tie Qianshan. He was a head taller than the rest and walked at the back. Beside him walked a half-grown boy, his armour hanging loose on him, his eyes swollen, his head down all the way.

Yisi stood up, and looked over the company again from front to back.

Tie Qianshan had seen him too, and came over and stopped in front of him.

Yisi asked: "Where's Du De?"

Tie Qianshan did not answer at once. It was the boy who spoke first, his voice so hoarse it could hardly be heard:

"Uncle Du sent me over the bridge."

Having said that, he said nothing more, but kept his head down, his two hands clutching the edges of his coat.

Tie Qianshan pressed his hand once on the boy's shoulder and turned to Yisi. "I was up in the pass. I saw it all."

He spoke the way he always did, one word jumping out at a time.

"Thirty-odd men, on the far side of the gully, held for a cup of tea. All the carts got over the bridge. Then there was nobody standing over there, and the pursuers came pouring onto the bridge. The governor's order was not to shoot till they reached our end of the bridge. When they reached it, a thousand crossbows loosed at once. Two volleys, and they fell back. They were afraid there was an ambush in the pass, and didn't dare come on."

Yisi listened.

"After dark the governor sent us down to burn the bridge," said Tie Qianshan. "Before we burned it, we went over once and looked. He was lying at the foot of the cart wall, face up to the sky. Sword still in his hand. The hilt was stuck to his hand with blood. We couldn't prise it loose."

He stopped a moment.

"Couldn't prise it loose, so we buried him with the sword."

He took something from inside his coat and held it out to Yisi. It was a coarse earthenware wine bowl with a chip out of its rim.

"Found it in his saddlebag," said Tie Qianshan. "That night at Jiumen, he took this bowl and went over to you to drink. I saw."

Yisi took the bowl. In it was a pinch of earth, yellow with a little dark red in it.

He said nothing. He held the bowl in his hands, held it a long time. The boy kept his head down all the while, and Tie Qianshan stood beside him and did not go either. Then word came down from the front to move on, and Tie Qianshan touched the boy, and the two of them turned and went back among the crossbowmen.

In the seventh month the Crown Prince took the throne at Lingwu, and became the Emperor Suzong.

In the eighth month Guo Ziyi brought fifty thousand Shuofang troops to Lingwu and was received by the new Son of Heaven. The new Son of Heaven made him Minister of War and Administrator of Lingwu, still military governor of Shuofang, and told him to put his troops in order and prepare to recover the two capitals.

Yisi went back with him to Lingzhou.

It was evening when he came back to that side courtyard on the west of the city. A layer of dust had settled on the gate, and the lock had rusted. He undid it and pushed the gate open. The courtyard was overgrown with weeds, and there were bird droppings on the stove.

He went into the house.

Everything in it was still where it had been. The low table, the mats, the brazier. The pegs on the wall were empty. The cupboard was shut.

He took the mace and sword from his belt and hung them back on the pegs, and hung the shield up too. Then he took the coarse earthenware wine bowl from inside his robe. The pinch of earth was still in it. He set the bowl on the table.

He sat by the table a long time.

It grew dark. He did not light the lamp.

Later he got up, opened the cupboard, and took out a bowl. It was Jingjing's. He set it on the table, beside Du De's.

Jingjing had been gone nearly half a year, and there had been no word of him at all.
