Volume Two　Li Guangbi

Chapter 8　Du De

In the fifth month, the armies of Guo Ziyi and Li Guangbi fought Shi Siming again, at Jiashan.

It was a very great battle. Shi Siming lost forty thousand men. He himself fell from his horse; his helmet came off, his topknot came loose, one of his shoes was lost in the running, and barefoot, leaning on a broken spear, he groped his way back to camp only after dark, and that same night fled into the city of Boling. When the common people of a dozen and more commanderies of Hebei heard of it, one after another they killed the officials the rebels had sent them, opened their gates and returned to the Tang. The armies of Guo and Li followed at once and laid siege to Boling, and it seemed they would take it any day. Everyone in camp said that if the fighting went on like this, by autumn they would reach Fanyang and smash An Lushan's lair.

In the middle of the sixth month, a horseman came from the west. The man had wasted to nothing, and his horse was nothing but a frame of bones, and when he got down inside the camp gate his legs gave way and he fell to his knees. He had ridden all the way from Hedong, changing horses, and the dispatch on him was wrapped in three layers of oilcloth. The dispatch said that Tong Pass was lost. Geshu Han's army of two hundred thousand had been destroyed in one battle on the western plain at Lingbao, and Geshu Han himself had been tied up by his own men and handed over to An Lushan. With Tong Pass lost, Chang'an had no gate.

That night Guo Ziyi and Li Guangbi sat facing each other in the command tent the whole night through. The guards outside heard them speak only a few times, never loudly, and later there was no sound at all. At dawn the order came down: raise the siege of Boling, the whole army to withdraw west into Jingxing and hold Taiyuan. Two days later a second horseman arrived with news worse than the first: the Sage had gone out by the Yanqiu Gate in the middle of the night and was travelling west, and where to, no one knew.

Tens of thousands of men and horses and thousands of baggage carts had to fall back from the plain of Hebei into the Taihang Mountains, and the rebels behind them were not going to let them go unpursued. The men withdrawing all had their minds on what was behind, and as they walked their feet went faster; the old soldiers in the camp all knew that at such a time an army was most easily thrown into confusion.

Shi Siming had been besieged in Boling more than a month. When the city gate opened, his vanguard horse came after them. Most of these horsemen were Tongluo, Xi and Khitan, men raised on horseback who could draw a strong bow and ride fast. They did not charge home; they only clung to the tail of the Tang army from a distance, and where they had a grip on a stretch of it, loosed a volley of arrows, seized a few carts, set a fire, and when the Tang turned round, scattered again.

Li Guangbi was used to this kind of fighting. On the first day he sent a thousand crossbowmen ahead into the mountains to take the ridges on either side of the eastern mouth of Jingxing, and told them to hold the mouth of the pass and nothing else—whatever happened outside it was not their concern; carts would go their way and men theirs, and not one crossbowman was to come down from the ridge. Some in the camp grumbled: the brothers at the back were taking the blades, and the crossbowmen had run off into the hills first to take their ease. Word of it reached Li Guangbi, and he paid it no heed.

The main army went in front, the baggage carts followed, and last of all came the rearguard battalion. The rearguard duty Li Guangbi let no one compete for, and let no one push off on others: the battalions took it in turn, one battalion a day, and whichever battalion's turn it was, that was the one. Nor did the rearguard battalion fall back all in a mass. It was split into two halves: one half found a bank of earth or a stand of trees beside the road, dug in, and held off the pursuers with bows and crossbows; the other half fell back to the next place where a man could stand, and dug in there; only then did the first half pull out, pass through the second, and fall back further still. So it went, half relieving half, like a man walking: the left foot set firm before the right foot is lifted.

The battalion that held the rear on the first day came back in the evening more than a hundred men short. The battalion on the second day lost more, and one of its companies was almost wiped out.

On the third day the rear was held by the battalion of the detached commander Sun Shouliang. Du De was in this battalion.

He was not a company leader. His company's leader had gone to relieve himself at the edge of the camp the night before and been shot through the neck by a stray arrow from nobody knew where, and died; the second-in-command was wounded too, and lay on a cart unable to get up. Of the thirty-odd men left, Du De was the oldest and had served the longest years, and from the football outside the walls of Jiumen everyone knew him besides, so they all looked to him. At the fifth watch, when Sun Shouliang called the names and gave his orders before his tent, Du De stood in for his company among the men who led them, heard the orders, and came back and passed them on to the rest one sentence at a time.

The wind that day came from the north, hard and dry, and blew sand into everyone's eyes. Before it was full light the camp was called to withdraw. The baggage carts moved off west one after another, their wheels cutting deep ruts in the dirt road; the horses took fright and whinnied all together; those in front walked fast and those behind could not keep up, and the whole column was stretched longer and longer, like a rope pulled thin. Sun Shouliang's battalion came last, on horseback, falling back half relieving half. Du De's company was cavalry, thirty-odd horses; on the way they changed places three times, dug in, loosed, fell back, and by noon five or six men had been left behind on the road and had not caught up again.

Around noon, the carts ahead stopped. Du De rode forward a stretch and saw why. Across the road ahead lay a gully. It was not wide, and the water in it was not deep, but its banks were steep and the carts could not get down them, and over the gully there was only one wooden bridge, so narrow it would take only one cart at a time. A dozen and more baggage carts were jammed at the bridgehead. Some were trying to get across first, shaft locked against shaft; one cart had its wheel stuck in a crack between the planks of the bridge, and its driver was whipping his beasts for all he was worth, and the beasts would not move, and the men behind went up to push, and the more they pushed the worse the jam. Two soldiers had abandoned their cart and were trying to wade across the gully.

Du De looked at that knot of carts and men jammed together, and for a moment was stunned. His elder brother, years ago, on the retreat toward Tong Pass, had been trampled to death by his own side's men and horses. He galloped up to the bridgehead and bellowed at the top of his voice: "One at a time!" That voice of his had been shouted into shape on the ground outside the walls of Jiumen, coarse and loud, and the bridgehead went quiet all at once. He said too: the carts behind were to back off ten paces, the one that was stuck was to have its beasts unhitched and be carried across, and whoever pushed again, he would cut down first. The two soldiers wading stood in the gully, looked at him, and climbed back out. Only then did the carts go onto the bridge one at a time, and the stuck one was shifted across by seven or eight men carrying its wheels.

Beyond the bridge was a gentle slope, and at the end of the slope was the eastern mouth of Jingxing; on the ridges on either side, men could be dimly seen standing—Li Guangbi's crossbowmen. From the bridgehead to the mouth of the pass was three or four hundred paces at least, and the crossbows on the ridges could not reach this side of the bridge.

Du De looked back once to the east. On the eastern horizon a line of yellow dust had risen. It was still far, a dozen li off or so, but it was rising higher and higher: horsemen. He looked again at the bridgehead: a dozen-odd carts still to go. A dozen-odd carts over one narrow bridge would take the time it takes to drink a cup of tea, at the least.

Sun Shouliang had seen the line of dust too. He rode once round the bridgehead, looked at the gully, and looked to the north. Two li off to the north the gully bent; there its banks sank low and the water was shallow, a ford that horses could cross. If the rebel horse came round that way, they could cut in between the bridge and the mouth of the pass and take the last few carts. Sun Shouliang said he would take most of the battalion's horse north to hold the ford, and at the bridgehead here one company would have to be left to hold, to hold until the carts were across.

Which company to leave, he did not name. It was an old rule in Li Guangbi's camp: who in the rearguard battalion was left to hold the last place was always decided by drawing lots, and in a bodyguard's quiver there was always kept one arrow with a strip of red cloth wound round it. Sun Shouliang had the bodyguard bring the quiver. In it stood a few arrows, heads down so they could not be seen, and on the head of only one was wound a strip of red cloth. The men who led the companies reached in one by one to draw. Those before him drew, and each handed his arrow back to the bodyguard, and no one spoke.

When it came to Du De, he reached in, took hold of one at random, and drew it out. Round its head was wound a strip of red cloth, fluttering in the wind.

Sun Shouliang looked once and asked him how long he could hold. Du De looked toward the line of dust in the east and said: "A cup of tea." Sun Shouliang said that when the carts were across, they were to mount and cross the bridge and fire it, and not linger to fight. When he had said it he wheeled his horse and led his men off north.

Du De put the arrow into his own quiver. He went back to his company. Twenty-odd men sat their horses looking at him; he said nothing, drew the arrow out, showed it to them once, and put it back. Someone swore. Someone said: "Old Du, you can kick it in backwards, and drawing an arrow your hand's just as true." A few of them laughed, a very short laugh. Du De smiled too, and said: "When you've done laughing, get down and get to work."

The twenty-odd men got down at the east end of the bridge, tied their horses behind a bank of earth and left one man to watch them, so that they could mount and cross when the carts were over. In front of the bridgehead ran a bank of earth, more than half a man high, a field ridge built long ago by men who farmed there, gullied and pitted by the rain. Du De had them push over two baggage carts that had broken down on the road, tip them on their sides wheels and all, and lay them across in front of the bank, and that made a low wall; and the carts' shafts, and a few spare spear-shafts lying ready, he had planted slantwise in the ground before the cart-wall, points outward, for a barrier against horse.

In the company there was a new recruit of sixteen or seventeen, only added a few days before, not yet full grown, his armour swinging loose on him. He came running with an armful of poles, his hands shaking so that the poles fell to the ground one by one. Du De looked at him once, took the poles from his hands, planted them in the ground, and said to him: "Get on your horse, go over the bridge, and stay with the last cart. Until that cart's into the mouth of the pass, don't let it stop."

The boy stood stunned, and said he could shoot; at home he'd shot hares, seven or eight out of ten. Du De said he knew—that was exactly why he was sending him to guard the carts: if those last few carts were cut off on the slope with not one man by them who could shoot, it was all lost. The boy still stood without moving. Du De said no more to him, raised a hand and gave his horse a slap on the rump, and the horse carried him onto the bridge. The boy turned in the saddle and looked back once. Du De was no longer looking at him.

The line of dust came faster than he had thought, and they had barely got things in order on this side when the pursuers arrived. The first to come were two or three hundred horse. They saw the cart-wall at the bridgehead, saw the twenty-odd men behind it, and did not charge; they spread out a bowshot off and rode round the cart-wall, loosing as they rode, turning in the saddle. The arrows came in volley after volley, stuck in the cart-boards, stuck in the bank, whistled over men's heads. Du De had everyone lie down behind the cart-wall and not show their heads, and not shoot at random either, but wait for the horsemen to come close.

Of the men lying behind the cart-wall, two showed a little too much and were hit; one in the shoulder, and one was gone on the spot.

The rebel horse went round twice, and seeing no movement behind the cart-wall, thought the men inside were frightened stiff, and a few dozen of them drew together and charged, whooping. Du De waited until they were forty or fifty paces off, and only then shouted: "The horses!" A dozen bows sounded together; the first few horses in the charge were hit, their forelegs folded and they went down on their knees, their riders thrown a long way, and the horses behind could not pull up and crashed into the ones in front, and in a moment it was all one heap. The rest wheeled their horses and fell back again beyond a bowshot.

Du De knew this would not frighten them for long. He looked back once; on the bridge the carts were still crossing one at a time.

After a while another body of rebel horse came up from behind, twice as many. This time they did not ride round. The greater part of them dismounted and came on with blades and spears, while the others kept up the arrows from horseback on either side, and those on foot swarmed at the cart-wall. The Xi and the Khitan could shoot on horseback and cut on foot, and they came on fast and fierce.

Du De held an old lance whose shaft was black with soaked-in sweat, but whose point was ground bright as snow. The first rebel to reach the cart-wall he took in the thigh with the lance, and the man pitched back; the second had just got up onto the cart-boards when an old soldier beside him cut him in the neck. The poles planted slantwise in front of the cart-wall stopped some of them and tripped some of them, and those behind climbed on those in front, and in a moment the ground before the wall was heaped high.

Afterwards Du De could not remember how many times he thrust. He remembered only that at first there were men on his left and men on his right; he would thrust, and the man beside him would cut; later there were fewer on his left, and fewer on his right; later still he turned his head to look, and there were only seven or eight left beside him. A rebel squeezed in through a gap in the cart-wall and raised his blade to cut; Du De met it with the lance shaft, and with a crack the shaft was cut through, and the blade came on down and bit into his left shoulder. He felt only his shoulder go heavy, then hot. He threw away the broken lance, drew his straight sword, cut once at the man's leg; the man went down, and he threw himself on him and cut again.

As he stood up, something hurt at his waist again. He looked down and saw a length of spearhead sticking out of his side. He turned and cut at the arm of the man behind him; the man let go, but the shaft stayed in him. He leaned against the cart-wall, breathing hard, and looked once to the west. There were no carts left on the bridge. On the slope beyond the bridge, the last baggage cart was going into the mouth of the pass, the flag at its tail fluttering in the wind, and beside the cart went a small horse, and on the horse a small figure, riding and looking back.

He thought: a cup of tea—that's about it. But there were rebels all round the cart-wall, before and behind, and the few horses behind the bank had long since been cut down by someone or other. They could not get out now.

He had not fallen yet. He leaned against the cart-wall, one hand pressing the length of spear-shaft at his waist, one hand gripping his sword, looking at the confusion of men before him. The rebels were still climbing this way, but more slowly; too many lay in front of the cart-wall, and those behind had to clamber over the dead. Another clambered over. Du De cut at him and cut askew, struck the man's helmet with a clang, and the edge of his blade turned; the man's spear came in and went into his thigh. He cut again, and this time he struck home.

His legs went soft. With his back against the cart-wall he slid slowly down, and lay on his back on the ground.

The sky was grey. The north wind blew on his face, hard and dry; in the wind there was sand, and the smell of blood, and the smell of horse-sweat, and the scorched smell of wood burning somewhere. His ears hummed, as if many people were shouting a long, long way off.

He thought of the threshing floor outside the walls of Jiumen. That day too he had lain on his back like this. The ball had flown back over his head, he had fallen on the ground rolled smooth and shining, and seen nothing, nothing but the sky, and the sky was blue. At the edge of the ground there had been a roar of shouting first, and halfway through it had broken off, so quiet he could hear the wheat straw rustle. He had lain there not knowing whether it had gone in, and just lain there, waiting. And then the drum had sounded.

He thought too of that hu monk. That night he had sat down first on the stone in the courtyard and patted the place beside him for the hu monk to sit too, and the moon was very bright. He had said to the hu monk: from now on, if you're in a battle line and hear someone shout Du De, don't take it for anyone else. And the hu monk had said he would remember.

He lay there, waiting for that drum.

Another spear came thrusting at him. Du De raised his sword to meet it, and his sword was half an inch slow.

The rebels poured over the cart-wall and crowded onto the bridge. At the ford to the north, Sun Shouliang's horse had fought and fallen back, fought and fallen back, and already pulled into the mouth of the pass ahead of them. The rebels crowded onto the bridgehead, crowded over the bridge, crowded up the gentle slope, crowded right up to two hundred paces or so from the mouth of the pass. Only then did the thousand crossbows on the ridges loose all together.

Yisi heard of it the next day.

That day he was in the mountains of Jingxing, going west with Guo Ziyi's central army. The road in the mountains was narrow, and the column moved very slowly, going a while and stopping a while. When they halted to rest, the men of Li Guangbi's camp caught up from behind, company after company, squeezing past Guo Ziyi's soldiers. Yisi sat on a stone by the road watching them pass, looking in the crowd for a face. The face was in its forties, deeply lined, a little hunched, and walked without lifting its feet much, as if loath to waste the effort. Since that day at Jiumen he had seen the face several times from afar in Li Guangbi's camp, and each time the man had jerked his chin at him from a long way off.

A company went by, and it was not there. Another company went by, and it was not there either.

Last came a company of crossbowmen, twenty or thirty of them, crossbows on their backs, their faces grey with dust. Yisi saw Tie Qianshan at once: he was a head taller than the rest and walked at the very back, and beside him went a half-grown boy, his armour swinging loose on him, his eyes swollen, keeping his head down all the way. Yisi stood up and looked the company over again from the start.

Tie Qianshan saw him too, came over, and stopped in front of him. Yisi asked him where Du De was. Before Tie Qianshan could open his mouth, the boy spoke, in a voice so hoarse it could hardly be heard: "Uncle Du sent me over the bridge." When he had said that, he could say nothing more, and kept his head down, both hands clutching the hem of his coat.

Tie Qianshan put out a hand and pressed the boy's shoulder, then turned his face to Yisi and said that he had been at the mouth of the pass that day and seen it all. He talked the way he always did, spitting the words out one at a time, a sentence and a pause. He said Du De's company, twenty-odd men, had held at the east end of the bridge for a cup of tea. The carts had all got across the bridge. It was too far, the crossbows could not reach, and the men on the ridges could only watch. Later there was no one left standing over there, and the rebels poured onto the bridge, poured up the slope, and when they came where the crossbows could reach, a thousand crossbows loosed together. After two volleys they fell back, afraid of an ambush in the mouth of the pass, and did not pursue again.

"After dark, the governor sent us down to burn the bridge," said Tie Qianshan. "Before we burned it, we went over to look. He was lying at the foot of the cart-wall, face to the sky, his sword still in his hand. The hilt was stuck fast with blood, and we couldn't prise it loose. We couldn't prise it loose, so we buried him and the sword together. When we'd buried him, I took a handful of earth from his grave."

He took something from his coat and handed it to Yisi. It was a coarse earthenware wine-bowl, with a chip in its rim. He said they had found it in Du De's saddlebag; that night at Jiumen, Du De had carried this very bowl over to find Yisi and drink with him—he'd seen it. The handful of earth in the bowl was from the grave.

Yisi took the bowl. In it was a pinch of earth, yellow with a little dark red in it. He held the bowl in his hands and said nothing. The boy kept his head down all the while, and Tie Qianshan stood beside him and did not go either. Later the word to move on came down from the front; Tie Qianshan nudged the boy, and the two of them turned and went back among the crossbowmen. Yisi wrapped the bowl in a cloth, tucked it into his coat, and went on west with the central army.

In the seventh month, the Crown Prince took the throne at Lingwu: he who would be known as Suzong. In the eighth month Guo Ziyi brought fifty thousand men of Shuofang to Lingwu and saw the new Son of Heaven. The new Son of Heaven made him Minister of War and Administrator of Lingwu, still in command of Shuofang, and had him put his troops in order and prepare to recover the two capitals.

Guo Ziyi stayed some days at the imperial lodging and sent up several memorials, mostly on troops, horses, money and grain. At the end of one of them he added a line, saying that under his command there was a monk of the Daqin monastery named Yisi who since the twelfth month of last year had gone with the army finding the way, had carried a letter to Changshan, and had been with the army in the battles at Jiumen and Jiashan; that for a monk to serve with the army without leave by edict was irregular, and he begged that the monk be permitted to go with the army and serve as needed. The memorial was sent up, and two days later it came down again, with one character written after it: Approved.

Early the next morning a herald of the staff came to the tent where Yisi lodged, called him out, read the edict aloud to him before a few bodyguards, and gave him a certificate, on which was written that the monk Yisi of the Daqin monastery was by edict to go with the army and serve as needed, stamped with the seal of the Military Governor of Shuofang. The herald said that from now on, if anyone at a pass or a checkpoint asked, he was to show them this. Yisi knelt to receive it and knocked his head to the ground. The herald, having said what he had to say, turned and went to carry orders to another camp. Yisi folded the certificate and put it with the travel pass he carried.

Whose idea that line had been, and what else the memorial said, Yisi did not know, and no one told him.

In the days when it came down, Guo Ziyi met, outside the imperial lodging, one of the chief physicians of the Palace Medical Service. The man had served in the Eastern Palace in the Tianbao years, and had now followed the Emperor to Lingwu. The two stood exchanging a few idle words, and the physician mentioned the Daqin monk in the memorial, saying he seemed to have heard the name before. Early in Tianbao, he said, a young princess in the Eastern Palace had had a disease of the eyes, and the court physicians had treated her more than two months without improvement; the Crown Prince had sent to the Yining Ward to fetch an old teacher of the Law from the Daqin monastery who could treat eyes. The old teacher had had a young hu with him, who carried the medicine chest and handed him the knives and needles; the old teacher could not speak Tang clearly, and it was the young man who passed on what he said, sentence by sentence. He remembered that the man was called Yisi. Guo Ziyi heard it and said only: "So he can do that too," and then spoke of other things.

Yisi went back with the army to Lingzhou.

When he got back to that side courtyard in the west of the city, it was evening. A layer of dust had settled on the courtyard gate, and the lock had rusted. He opened the lock and pushed the gate in. The courtyard was overgrown with grass, and there were bird droppings on the stove. Inside, everything was still where it had been: the low table, the sitting mat, the brazier, the wooden pegs on the wall empty, the cupboard shut. He took off his mace and blade and hung them back on the pegs, hung the shield up too, then took the cloth bundle from his coat, opened it, and set that coarse earthenware wine-bowl on the table. The pinch of earth in the bowl was still there.

It grew dark, and he did not light the lamp, but sat on at the table. Later he stood up, opened the cupboard, and took out a bowl. It was Jingjing's. He set this bowl on the table too, next to Du De's.

Jingjing had been gone almost half a year, and there had not been a word.
