Volume Seven　A-Lian

Chapter 39　Reunion

In the seventeen years since Yonan came back to Chang'an, he had never once set foot in the West Market.

At first the people of the monastery did not notice. Later they noticed, and no one asked. The Yining Ward was only two wards from the West Market, and the monastery bought its rice and oil, its paper and ink, its medicines all at the West Market. When Jingjing gave out gruel, a few days every month he pushed the gruel cart to the lanes at the south end of the West Market. Every one of the elders had been; Ishaq went most often, being fond of the roast mutton at a hu shop there, and every time he went he came back a size rounder.

Yonan did not go.

What he needed, he had others bring. When someone asked him to go along, he said his legs were not good. His legs were in fact very good; at seventy he still walked with his back bolt upright, and from behind he looked no different from the young deacons of the monastery.

Ishaq joked with him once: "Yonan, does somebody in the West Market owe you money?"

Yonan said: "I owe them."

Ishaq laughed, taking it for a joke too.

In the third month of the tenth year of Dali, a porter came to the monastery looking for someone.

There was hardly anyone at the monastery that day. The elder Azarel had taken several of the elders to a believer's house in the south of the city, where an old man was dying. Jingjing had gone to the East Market to have eye medicine made up for Babai. In the front hall there was only Yonan, kneeling before the cross saying the evening office.

The porter was twenty or so, his head and face running with sweat. He stood at the hall door, not daring to come in, craned his head and asked: "Is there a hu master here who can treat the sick?"

Yonan stood up.

The porter said his child had had a fever two days and could not be got to take milk, and was crying so weakly no sound came. He had heard that at the hu monastery in the Yining Ward there was an old master who treated the sick and took no money.

"Where do you live?" asked Yonan.

"The West Market," said the porter. "The south end, the lane behind a hu bread shop."

Yonan stood there and for a good while did not move.

The porter thought he would not come, and in his anxiety began to kneel.

Yonan reached out and held him up. He turned, went back to his room, and took his bag of medicines. This bag was new, its leather still stiff, but what was in it was the same as in that one thirty years before: apricot kernel, liquorice, a few roots, a few little porcelain bottles.

He followed the porter out of the monastery gate. The lamp at the gate was not yet lit; it was still light.

The West Market was not quite as he remembered it.

By the north gate a few new buildings had gone up, the silk shops had moved, a row of medicine shops had gone and become shops selling hu ewers. There were fewer people in the streets than in those years; there had been war, and Chang'an had lost a great many people, and had not yet recovered. But the sounds were the same sounds—hawking, haggling, mules braying, the clang of the smithies—all mixed together into a humming, like a pot on the boil.

The porter walked fast, and Yonan, following behind, walked fast too.

They went through from north to south, to the south end of the West Market. The porter turned into a narrow lane.

It was not that lane. Yonan knew it; it was not. That lane was to the east, two streets over. Something in his heart loosened, and he did not know why it should loosen.

The porter's home was a half-fallen mud hut; inside was a straw mat, and on the mat lay a child of a year or so, his face burning red, his lips so dry they were peeling. The child's mother squatted beside him wiping his forehead with a wet cloth.

Yonan squatted down, felt the child's forehead, took his little wrist and felt the pulse, counting a while.

"Water," he said. "Hot."

The child's mother got up to heat water.

He gave the child medicine, and taught the mother how to wipe the child down with warm water—where to wipe, and how long. His Tang was much better than in those years, but he still spoke slowly, squeezing out the words one at a time. The mother listened very carefully; he said a sentence, and she nodded.

He sat in that mud hut more than an hour. The child broke into a sweat, the fever went down a little, he whimpered twice, and opened his mouth for the breast.

The porter knelt and knocked his head on the ground to him. He raised the porter up and said: "Tomorrow, I come again."

When he came out of the lane, the sun was already past its height.

He did not go north at once. He stood at the mouth of the lane and looked east. Two streets to the east, a thread of white steam rose from behind the rooftops—some shop cooking something.

He stood a while, and went east.

He told himself he was going to see whether that hu eating-house was still there. One look, and he would go back.

The eating-house was not there. Where the great cauldron had stood at the mouth of the lane there was now a fodder seller, with a few stacks of hay at the door and a donkey tethered to a post, its head down, chewing. The lane was still there, as narrow as ever, the foot of its walls still wet, but the half-open doors on either side had all changed; some were sealed up, some had become shopfronts.

He stood at the mouth of the lane and looked in a long time. That corner of the wall at the very end of the lane he could not see; a pile of bamboo poles was in the way.

He turned and walked back.

A dozen steps on, he smelled bread baking.

The smell came from a little shop across the way. The shop was very small, its front only one bay wide, with a hu bread oven set up at the door, the charcoal in the firebox glowing red, a row of flatbreads stuck to the oven wall, puffed up and golden-brown. Inside were two low tables, at which a few porter-looking men sat eating bread with a bowl of soup.

On the doorframe hung a menu board.

Yonan had been about to walk past. He glanced at the menu board, and his feet stopped.

The menu board was a whitewashed plank with the things for sale written on it: bread, soup, mutton soup, noodle pieces. The characters were Tang, not handsomely written, every stroke put down very hard. But below the menu board there was a smaller plank hung on a nail, on which line after line had been written in charcoal, like an account.

Those things were not Tang characters.

They were Syriac letters.

Line after line, right to left. But what they spelled was the sound of Tang speech. Zhang the Third, owes four breads. Old Wang, owes two soups. So-and-so, paid. They were spelled crookedly, some letters written backwards, but none was wrong.

At the end of every line, the letter Nun turned its tail up.

Yonan stood before that plank, stood a long time. So long that the boy baking bread by the oven raised his head to look at him.

"Bread for you, sir?"

It was a woman's voice, from inside the shop.

Yonan did not turn round. He looked at the plank and listened to the voice coming out from inside, a step, two steps, to the door.

"Sir?"

He turned round.

At the door stood a woman of fifty or so, not tall, grown a little stout, her hair more than half white, coiled at the back of her head and held with a wooden pin. She wore an apron, and both her hands were covered with flour, up to the wrists.

Her eyes were very black.

She looked at Yonan, one look, and all at once the look on her face changed.

She said not a word. She simply stood there looking at his face, looked a long time. Flour fell from her fingers bit by bit onto the threshold.

"Your face," she said at last, her voice very low, "is still that face."

Yonan opened his mouth. The Tang he could speak was several hundred words more than thirty years before. Now he could not find one.

He raised his hands and showed them to her.

They were the hands of a man of seventy. Thin, dry, the tendons standing out one by one, the backs covered with spots, the knuckles thick, the lines in the skin like a dried riverbed.

She looked at those hands a while, and raised her own. Both hands were covered with flour, and under the flour were crack after crack, scars from burns, the hard calluses left by chilblains that healed and came again and came and healed.

The two of them stood with a threshold between them, holding up their hands.

The boy by the oven looked from one to the other, and put down the iron hook in his hand.

Afterwards she brought him into the shop and sat him at the low table at the back. The few porters in the shop finished eating, left their money, and went. She told the boy to watch the oven, and sat down across from Yonan.

She brought him a bowl of water. Hot.

"You remember," said Yonan.

"That time you held my bowl down," she said, "I remembered."

"You're not sick now."

"Drink it hot, and you don't get sick."

Yonan held that bowl of hot water a long time, and did not drink.

She told him about these years.

She told it very briefly. That autumn, a few days after the child was given away, she left, went to Luoyang, helped in a bread shop in Luoyang, and learned to bake hu bread. Then the war came, Luoyang was lost, she went south with the refugees as far as Xiangzhou on the Han, and stayed in Xiangzhou some years. When the war was over she heard Chang'an had been recovered, and came back. It took her the better part of a year. When she got back she took out all the money she had saved these years, rented this shopfront, and baked bread to sell herself.

"How many years?" asked Yonan.

"Eight," she said.

Eight. Yonan reckoned it in his heart. Eight years, and he had been two wards away.

"How did you live?" he asked.

She thought.

"By the Lord," she said. "And by my hands."

She laid her two hands on the table, palms up. The flour had dried, and stuck in flakes to her palms.

Yonan looked at those two hands. He thought of thirty years before, how every time she came to the monastery she washed her hands clean at the gate before she held them out for him to put on the salve. In those days her hands were covered with chilblains, split open, weeping bloody water.

He put out one finger, and in the layer of dry flour on her palm slowly wrote a character.

One curving hook, three dots.

She looked down, watching the character come out stroke by stroke in her palm. The three beans had all fallen into the bowl.

She did not move. She kept her hand open like that, and let the character stay in her palm.

After a long while she drew her hands back, wiped them on her apron, stood up, went to the oven and took a flatbread, came back, and set it before him.

The bread was fresh from the oven, too hot to hold. She picked it up, broke it in two, set one half before him and kept the other in her own hand.

Yonan picked up the half and took a bite.

She watched him eat. Her own half she held in her hand, and did not eat.

"At the monastery gate," she said, "that lamp is still lit."

Yonan raised his head.

"I went to see once," she said. "The winter I first came back. I went at dusk, stood over by the ward gate, and saw the lamp lit at the monastery gate. I stood a while, and came back."

"Why didn't you go in?"

She did not answer. She put down the half bread in her hand.

"I've seen him," she said.

Yonan's hand stopped.

"He comes to the West Market to give out gruel," she said. "Pushing a cart, bringing a few people, setting up at the mouths of those lanes to the south, a bowl each. I've watched from the back of the crowd. Watched several times."

She paused.

"He's grown tall. Taller than Master Yisi."

Yonan said nothing.

"He has my eyes," she said.

"Your eyes," said Yonan.

She nodded. After a while she asked again: "Does he still drink cold water?"

Yonan was taken aback.

"He doesn't," he said. "Yisi sees to it. He sees to it himself too. In winter when he's writing, even when the inkstone's frozen, he runs to the kitchen to pour himself something hot."

When she heard it she bowed her head and smiled. Smiled very quickly, and took it back at once.

Exactly as thirty years before.

It was nearly dark.

The boy was clearing up the oven at the door, stacking the leftover breads one by one into a bamboo basket. Before long the West Market drum would sound, and when it had sounded the market gates would shut.

Yonan stood up.

She stood up too, and saw him to the door.

At the door he stopped before that little account plank and looked at it once more. Right to left, line after line. Zhang the Third, owes four breads. Old Wang, owes two soups.

"These people," he said. "Do they all pay?"

"Some pay," she said. "Some don't."

"And the ones who don't?"

"The ones who don't," she said, "when they come again, I give them bread."

Yonan looked at her. She stood inside the door, her apron covered with flour, her hair more than half white, many lines in her face now, her eyes still black.

"Tomorrow," he said, "I must come again. That child—I must see him again."

"Which child?"

"In the lane to the south, a porter's child. Feverish."

She looked at him. She seemed about to say something, and did not. She only nodded.

"Then come and eat bread," she said.

By the time Yonan came out of the north gate of the West Market, the drum had beaten its first round.

He walked very fast. From the West Market to the Yining Ward was two wards; he went through one ward gate as the drum beat its second round, through another as it beat its third. He got into the Yining Ward before the ward gate shut, turned along that stretch of narrow street, and saw the monastery gate.

The lamp at the monastery gate was already lit.

Jingjing stood under the lamp with a packet of medicine in his hand—the eye medicine made up for Babai at the East Market. He seemed just to have arrived and was about to go in when he saw Yonan coming from the ward gate, and stopped.

"Elder," he said, "where have you been?"

"The West Market," said Yonan. "A child is sick."

Jingjing looked at him. On Yonan's cuff there was a little flour, white.

Jingjing did not ask. He stood aside and let Yonan go in first.

Yonan walked past him and in through the gate. A few steps on, he stopped and turned his head.

"Tomorrow," he said, "I'll go again."

Jingjing said: "Mm."

Yonan turned and went towards the back courtyard. Jingjing stood at the gate watching his back go into the dark. That back was held bolt upright.

That night, Yonan washed his hands very slowly. He soaked his hands in the basin, soaked them a long time, before he took them out.

The little flour on his hands washed away.
