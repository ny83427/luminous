Chapter 37　Water and Lamp

At the gate of the Daqin monastery in the Yining Ward of Chang'an, there was a lamp.

It was not a big lamp—a bronze oil saucer set on the stone block to the left of the gate, with a cover of woven bamboo over it, pasted with paper to keep off the wind. It was lit as soon as dusk fell; when the ward drums had finished beating it was still burning; in the middle of the night it was filled once, and only at daybreak put out.

Everyone in the ward knew this lamp. The old charcoal seller, packing up late and feeling his way home in the dark, saw that spot of yellow and knew he was nearly there. The ward police on their night rounds would stop under the lamp too, stamp the dust off their feet, and go on. When children asked their elders why there was a lamp at the hu monastery's gate, the elders said: it's the hu monks' rule.

The people of the monastery themselves could not say whose rule it was.

In the winter of the first year of Yongtai, it was mostly Yonan who lit the lamp. That year he was sixty, and his face looked not yet forty. Every evening he came out from the back courtyard with an oil jug in one hand and a fuse cord in the other, lifted the paper cover, filled the oil, trimmed the wick, lit it, and put the cover back. When it was on, he did not go at once, but stood a while at the gate, looking east, looking south, as if waiting for someone to turn in from the ward gate.

The young deacons said: Elder, let me do it.

Yonan said: I'll do it.

What year the lamp was first lit, only two people at the monastery now knew. One was Yonan. The other was Yisi, who in those days was with the Shuofang army and came back to Chang'an only a few times a year.

It was in the fourth year of Tianbao.

That year Yonan was forty.

He had come to Chang'an the autumn before. The Patriarchate had sent him as bishop, to have charge of the monastery in Chang'an and the people of Chang'an. When he arrived, the old believers of the monastery came out to meet him, saw his smooth face without a single hair of beard, the skin drawn very tight, the eyes very bright, and muttered among themselves: why has the Patriarchate sent us a child? Later they saw his hands, and stopped muttering. His hands were thin, the tendons standing out, ten years older than his face.

He was a diligent bishop. Every day he preached, baptised, kept the accounts, met officials.

He preached in Syriac. There was an old believer at the monastery, a Sogdian who had lived thirty years in Chang'an, who put it into Tang for him. Yonan spoke a sentence and stopped, and waited for the old believer to translate it. Meeting officials was the same: he said a sentence, the translator said a sentence, the official said a sentence, the translator said another. Back and forth, and a morning was gone.

He knew a few dozen words of Tang. Eat, drink, sit, good, not good, thank, Lord. He could understand more than that, but could not say it. Of Tang characters he could write not one.

He had a young priest beside him called Yisi, from Balkh, twenty years younger than he, three years in Chang'an, who spoke Tang slowly but bit every word clear. Yisi had been married three years, to a Sogdian woman, and the couple lived in the side courtyard east of the monastery, and had no children. Sometimes when Yonan had finished preaching, he would have Yisi say the important parts over again for him. When Yisi had said them, the people below would nod. Yonan watched them nod, and in his heart was a little envious, and a little ashamed.

One day in the second month, a porter came to the monastery looking for him.

That day Yonan was in the front hall seeing a petty clerk of the capital prefecture about the boundary of a piece of the monastery's land, and talked the whole morning. The porter waited outside the gate. When he could wait no longer, he told the gatekeeper that his child was sick, had had a fever three days, and he wanted to borrow a few cash from the bishop for medicine. He said he lived in the West Market, in the lane behind the hu eating-house; ask, and anyone would know. Having said it, he went, probably afraid the child had no one to watch it.

When Yonan had seen the clerk off and heard what the gatekeeper said, he went back to his room, took a string of cash, and took his bag of medicines too. At the Patriarchate he had studied some years with an old physician, and could treat the sick and make up a few medicines. He thought: three days of fever—money alone isn't enough.

He went alone.

The West Market was not far from the Yining Ward, two wards away. He went in by the north gate of the West Market, through the silk sellers, the medicine sellers, the sellers of gold and silver ware, went south, and came to the mouth of a narrow lane. At the mouth of the lane was an eating-house kept by a hu, with a great cauldron set up at its door, mutton bones boiling in it, the steam rising in white clouds. Behind the eating-house was that lane.

The lane was very narrow, the walls on either side close together; the sun did not reach in, and the ground was always wet. Rotten vegetable leaves and charcoal ash were heaped at the foot of the walls. A few doors stood half open, and in them sat women with powdered faces, who looked at him once as he came in, and then did not look. One laughed and said something in a hu tongue, and the woman beside her laughed too.

Yonan understood what she said. He did not stop.

He asked three people in the lane, and all said they knew of no porter. He walked from this end of the lane to that, and back again. He did not find him.

He thought: perhaps I've remembered the place wrong. Perhaps the porter came back, saw the child was a little better, and went out to work again. He stood in the middle of the lane, not knowing whether to go or wait. He thought, and decided to wait a while.

After waiting about the time it takes to drink a cup of tea, he heard someone coughing at the foot of a wall.

The coughing was muffled, as if into a hand; a fit, a pause, another fit. Yonan followed the sound to the very end of the lane, where a wall turned a corner.

A woman was squatting at the foot of the wall.

She was very thin, wrapped in a lined jacket of no colour you could tell, her hair loose, her face buried in her knees, her arms hugging herself. Every time she coughed, her shoulders shook. On the ground was what she had coughed up, threaded with blood.

Yonan squatted down.

"You," he said. He wanted to say you're sick, but for the moment he could not remember the word for sick. So he reached out to feel her forehead.

She shrank back at once, and the back of her head struck the wall. She raised her head and looked at him.

It was a very young face, twenty or so, the cheekbones made high by sickness, the lips white. The eyes were very black. Black as a well.

"Don't touch," she said. "Dirty."

That word Yonan knew. He did not draw back his hand. He laid it on her forehead; it was very hot. He took her wrist and felt the pulse, counting a while. It was fast and floating.

"Cold?" he asked.

She looked at him, nodded, and shook her head.

Yonan stood up and looked about. The few doors in the lane had all shut. He thought, then helped her up and walked her to the eating-house at the mouth of the lane. The hu who kept the eating-house knew her; he frowned and said she had been coughing half a month, the room she lived in let in the wind, and she couldn't afford charcoal.

Yonan counted out some coins from his string, laid them on the keeper's chopping board, and said a few words to him in a hu tongue. The keeper took the money, led her into a little room behind the kitchen, brought a brazier, and brought a bowl of water.

She took the bowl and was about to drink.

Yonan put out his hand and stopped the bowl. It was water fresh drawn from the well, the chill still on its rim.

"No," he said. Again he could not think of the words, so he pointed at the pot on the stove, and then at the bowl.

The keeper understood, poured away the cold water, and ladled out a bowl of hot.

She held that bowl of hot water a long time before she drank it, in small sips. When she had drunk it she raised her head, looked at Yonan, and said something. Yonan did not understand. Only later did he learn that what she said was: "Since I was little I can't drink cold water; if I drink it my stomach goes bad. How did you know?"

He had not known. He was only a man who treated the sick, and men who treat the sick don't let the sick drink cold water.

That day he made up a packet of medicine for her—apricot kernel, liquorice, and two kinds of root he had brought from the Patriarchate—and told the keeper to brew it for her once a day. When he left, the lane had grown dark. He remembered the porter, and asked round the lane again, and still no one knew.

That porter never came to the monastery again. Yonan never knew whether his child got well.

Afterwards he went to see her several times.

The first time was three days later. She was sitting by the brazier, coughing less, a little colour in her face. When she saw him come in she stood up, not knowing how to make a bow, and stood with her head down.

He asked her name.

She said: A-Lian.

He repeated it after her: "A—Lian." He said it very stiffly, as if with a pebble in his mouth. She smiled a little, very quickly, and took it back at once.

The second time, she was no longer coughing. He changed her medicine. She asked him how much the medicine cost. He said: no money. She did not believe him, and kept asking. He could not find the words, and pointed at the sky.

She looked where he pointed, looked a while, and said: "Heaven gave it?"

He thought, and nodded. It was more or less that.

The third time, when he went, she was not in that little room. The keeper said she was well, and had gone back to the lane. When the keeper said it, he did not look at him.

Yonan stood at the door of the eating-house a while, then turned and went back to the monastery.

That night he knelt before the cross a long time. He did not know what he was praying for. He only knelt.

Half a month later, she came to the monastery.

It was an afternoon, and there was preaching in the monastery. Yonan stood at the front preaching, the old Sogdian believer standing beside him translating. Twenty or thirty people sat below, more hu than Han. As he preached, Yonan saw someone standing at the door, wrapped in that jacket of no colour, leaning on the doorframe, not coming in, not going away.

He paused. The translator thought he had finished a sentence, and translated. He went on.

When he had finished and the people had gone, she was still standing at the door. Yonan went over. She looked at him and said: "I came to listen."

He said: "Sit."

That word he could say.

From then on she came every few days. She always sat in the last row, near the door, and when it was over she went, and spoke to no one. Some of the old believers frowned when they saw her; some pretended not to see. One old woman said a few words to Yonan in a hu tongue, meaning: Bishop, people from that lane shouldn't be brought into the monastery.

Yonan heard her out and said: "She, listen."

The old woman opened her mouth, and no words came.

Preaching at the monastery was in the afternoon, and when it was over it was nearly dark. From the main gate of the Yining Ward to the monastery gate you had to go through a stretch of narrow street with no shops along it, and no lamps. One corner of the step at the monastery gate was broken, crushed by a cart wheel years before, and had never been mended.

One day she left a little later, when it was already dusk, and going out set her foot on that broken corner and fell to her knees on the step with a thud, and cut both knees.

Yonan heard it and came out of the hall, and she was already on her feet, brushing the dust off her clothes, saying: it's nothing, it's nothing; and limped away.

The next evening there was a lamp on the stone block at the monastery gate. A coarse earthenware oil saucer, with a paper cover over it.

The people of the monastery asked who had put it there. Yonan said: the step is broken; it gives some light.

The step was mended afterwards. The lamp was not taken away.

She listened to the preaching at the monastery half a year, and in the autumn was baptised.

That day was a morning in the eighth month, when the weather had already turned cool. The monastery's font was a great bronze basin, usually filled with well water. The evening before, Yonan went and said one sentence to Yisi's wife, and early the next morning that Sogdian woman boiled a pot of water on the stove, and when it boiled added cold to it, added until it no longer scalded the hand, carried it into the hall, and poured it into the bronze basin.

Yonan put his hand in to try it. The water was warm.

A-Lian knelt before the basin. Yonan cupped water in his hands, poured it over her head, and recited the words. The water ran down her hair, over her forehead, over her eyes, and dripped onto the floor before her knees.

She kept her eyes shut all the while. When Yonan had finished reciting, she opened them, and her eyes were full of water, and you could not tell which kind of water.

She stood up and said in a small voice: "It's warm."

Yonan said nothing. He turned round and shook the water left on his hands dry.

She wanted to learn to read.

She said so herself. After her baptism she came more often still. One day when the preaching was over she stayed behind and told Yonan she wanted to learn letters, wanted to be able to write her own name. When she said it, her two hands were twisted together, as if she were saying something very shameful.

Yonan could not write Tang characters. He thought, fetched a spoiled wooden writing tablet from his room, and with a stick of charcoal wrote four Syriac letters on it, right to left.

"Alaph," he said, pointing at the first. "Lamadh, Yudh, Nun."

She looked at those four curling things a long time.

"That's me?"

"You," said Yonan.

She put out her finger and traced along the four letters. She traced very slowly, and when she had traced them, traced them again.

She learned very fast. In a month she knew all twenty-two letters. In two months she could spell the name of everyone at the monastery. She spelled Yisi, spelled Yonan, spelled the old Sogdian believer, spelled them crookedly, but with no mistakes. Yonan found an old roll of the Psalms and taught her to read it letter by letter. She could not understand the meaning, but she could sound it out.

When she read, Yonan sat beside her and listened. He listened to her reading Syriac in a Chang'an accent, the tail of every word turning up. As he listened, he smiled.

She stopped and asked him what he was smiling at.

He said: "Good."

She taught him too.

She knew hardly any characters, so she said herself. But she had lived some years behind the eating-house, and at the eating-house door there hung a menu board with the things it sold written on it, and she had looked at it every day until she knew it by heart. Bread, soup, mutton, wine, noodles, money. The Tang characters she knew were all things to eat, plus one for money.

One day she said: "You preach in Chang'an and can't write a single Tang character. That won't do."

So she taught him. She wrote with the charcoal on the back of that wooden tablet; she wrote one, had him read it, and had him copy it. Bread. Soup. Mutton. The characters he wrote had crooked horizontals and crooked verticals, like a fence blown sideways by the wind. She laughed at them, and when she had laughed, had him write them again.

She taught him a great many. He learned one, and forgot one. Today he learned soup, and tomorrow he remembered only the three drops of the water radical.

In the end she had no way left. She thought, and wrote the simplest of all.

"心," she said. Xin, heart.

She said this was the xin of dianxin, the little cakes on the menu board, and of all the characters she knew it was the easiest to write. One curving hook, three dots.

"Look," she said, pointing at it, "it's like a bowl with three beans in it."

Yonan looked a long time.

He took the charcoal and wrote one. The hook curved too wide, and the three dots were crowded to one side. She shook her head. He wrote another.

He wrote all afternoon. By dark, in the hearts he wrote, the three beans had at last all fallen into the bowl.

She said: "Remember this one, and never mind the rest. You people preach about the heart all the time."

He remembered it. Never mind the rest.

What came after, Yonan could seldom remember in order.

He remembered only scraps.

He remembered that when she ate flatbread she always broke it in two first, kept one half and pushed the other across to him. He said he had eaten; she paid no heed and pushed it across. So he ate it.

He remembered that in winter she had chilblains on her hands, split open and weeping bloody water, and she still worked at the eating-house stove washing bowls with her hands in cold water. He made her a jar of salve, mutton fat mixed with a few powdered medicines. Every time she came to the monastery she first washed her hands clean at the gate, and only then held them out for him to put on the salve.

He remembered her reading the Psalms and stopping at a place, asking him what it meant. He put it into Tang and could not, and gestured a long time. At last she said: "I understand. It's someone keeping watch in the night, waiting for the morning."

He remembered the first time she called him by his name. She said Yonan, but Tang mouths were not used to the sound, and it came out Yuenan. He did not correct her.

And he remembered that every night he knelt before the cross and begged the Lord to take this thing out of his heart.

He begged all one autumn, and all one winter.

The Lord did not take it.

The next spring, she came to find him.

That day she did not sit in the last row. She waited until everyone had gone, came up to him, stood, and told him.

She said it very evenly, as if she had thought about it for many days, and having thought it all through, all that was left was to say it.

She said: "You're the bishop. If people find out about this, you're finished, and the church is finished too. People outside already say yours is a hu teaching, a heresy; if a thing like this comes out, they'll say: look at those hu monks, having children with a whore."

Yonan stood there and could not say a word. It was not because his Tang was not enough.

She said: "I won't have you ruined. I'll go. Take the child and go, go far away where no one can find us."

She said it and went. The lamp at the gate had already been lit; she walked past under it, her shadow trailing very long, all the way to the ward gate, and turned a corner and was gone.

Yonan stood in the hall until midnight.

She did not go far.

A woman alone, no money, no home, her belly bigger every day—how far could she go? She lived another half year in that lane in the West Market; the keeper of the eating-house took pity on her and let her help in the kitchen and gave her two meals a day. The women in the lane said she was a fool, said a hu monk had tricked her. She said nothing, washed bowls, split firewood, and at night sat alone in that draughty little room by a little fire and traced those four letters on the wooden tablet.

In the autumn of the fifth year of Tianbao, the child was born. A boy, very thin, his cry very small, his eyes black as hers.

On the fifth day after the child was born, she carried him to the Yining Ward and did not go in at the monastery gate. She went round to the side courtyard east of the monastery and knocked at the door.

The one who opened it was Yisi.

That year Yisi was twenty-one, not tall, broad-shouldered, short-haired, his brown eyes very deep. He looked at this woman outside the door, at the child in her arms wrapped in rags, looked a while.

He knew her. She had sat in the last row of the monastery a year; there was no one at the monastery who did not know her.

A-Lian told him. She told it very briefly, only a few sentences. Whose the child was, she did not say. Yisi did not ask.

Yisi asked only one thing: "Can this child live?"

A-Lian said: "He can."

Yisi said: "Then I'll own him."

He turned and called into the house. His wife came out. That Sogdian woman saw the child, stood stunned a moment, and then, saying nothing at all, reached out and took him. She carried the child in, laid him by the brazier, and turned to the stove to heat water.

A-Lian stood at the door watching the woman inside squat before the stove feeding it wood. She watched a while, did not go in, and did not say goodbye. She turned and went.

Yisi stood at the door and waited until she was out of the lane before he shut the door.

That night Yisi's wife washed the child in warm water. She mixed the water herself, mixed it three times, tested it with her elbow three times.

When the child was washed he stopped crying, and opened those black eyes, and looked up at the roof.

The Patriarchate's letter came in the winter.

The letter said: Bishop Yonan is recalled to the Patriarchate, to set out at once. Why he was recalled, the letter did not say. Later some said someone at the monastery in Chang'an had written to the Patriarchate. Others said no one wrote, and the Patriarchate had caught the rumour by itself. Yonan himself never asked.

The day he left, it was snowing lightly.

He did not go to the West Market. She did not come.

He came to the monastery gate. The lamp was not yet lit; it was still light. He stood a while before the stone block, lifted the paper cover, filled the oil saucer to the brim, and put the cover back.

Yisi was waiting outside the gate with the horse. In Yisi's arms was that child, wrapped in a sheepskin, only his little face showing.

Yonan looked at the child once. Only once, and turned his head away, and mounted.

"The lamp," he said from the saddle. "Keep it lit."

Yisi said: "I'll keep it lit."

Yisi did not ask why. He never asked why.

Yonan turned his horse and went west. The snow fell on his shoulders, fell on that smooth, beardless face of his. He never looked back.

The next year a letter came from the Patriarchate to Chang'an, sent by Yonan through someone else's hands, asking Yisi to go to the West Market and find someone. Yisi went. The people in the lane said she had gone—a few days after the child was given away she went, and took nothing with her. No one knew where.

From that year on, Yonan washed his hands three times a day. Why three times, he never told anyone.

On a winter evening in the first year of Yongtai, Yonan came out from the back courtyard with the oil jug.

It was getting dark earlier than usual. At the ward gate the drum had already beaten its first round. He came to the gate, lifted the paper cover, filled the oil, trimmed the wick. The fuse went out once in the wind; he turned his back, shielded it with his body, blew on it again, and it caught.

There were footsteps behind him.

It was Jingjing, back from giving out gruel at the West Market, rice-water on his sleeves, an empty wooden bucket in his hand. He was nineteen, half a head taller than Yisi, his eyes very black.

He saw Yonan squatting by the stone block shielding the flame, put down his bucket, came over, squatted down too, and cupped his two hands round the fuse.

"Elder," he said. "Let me."

Yonan said nothing. He handed him the fuse.

Jingjing lit the lamp and put the paper cover back, and set it very straight.
