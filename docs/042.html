Volume Seven　A-Lian

Chapter 42　Yonan

After his wedding, Yonan lived three days.

The first day, Yonan rose before A-Lian.

That small courtyard at the north end of the ward had belonged to an old oil-seller: two rooms, a small well, a grapevine trained along the foot of the wall, the grapes still green. A-Lian had brought a little clay oven from the shop, its firebox only big enough for six cakes, and set it up under the eaves, beside the well. When Yonan got up it was still dark; the ward drum had not yet sounded. He felt his way to the well, drew a dipper of water, washed his hands, and when he had washed them stood there not knowing what to do next. All his life he had lived in the monastery: when the board sounded he got up, and when he got up he went to the hall to say the morning office; here there was no board and no hall, and the dew on the grape leaves dripped drop by drop onto the well rim. He stood a while, then knelt facing east, right there on the muddy ground by the well, and said the morning office, said it very softly, for fear of waking the one in the house.

When he had done he turned round and saw A-Lian standing in the doorway in an old jacket thrown over her shoulders, watching him. She said nothing at all, but drew him over to the little oven, put a pair of fire-tongs in his hand, and said: if the fire's low add a piece of charcoal, if it's high close the oven door a little. She went into the house to mix the dough, rolled out the cakes, sprinkled sesame, brought them out one by one and stuck them inside the firebox. Yonan squatted before the oven watching the cakes swell little by little, a ring of brown forming at their edges, and called her; she came out and hooked them out with the iron hook and stuck in the next batch. At the fourth batch the ward drum sounded; A-Lian picked out twenty cakes, put them in a bamboo basket, handed it to him, and said: go.

Yonan carried the basket across half the ward to the monastery gate. Seven or eight people were already standing under the locust tree, all familiar faces; when they saw him turn the corner from the lane to the north they stood still a moment, and a lame old man laughed first and said: the Elder's cakes today were baked by the bride. Yonan laughed too, gave out the cakes, and when they were given out drew water at the well and washed his hands, and broke off two more cakes and laid them at the root of the locust tree for the sparrows.

That day Yonan wrote in the sand tray by the west wing door, still heart, with the bamboo stylus that had appeared beside the sand tray the day after the wedding, sharp at one end and round at the other. Jingjing knew the knife marks on that stylus, stroke by stroke, level as water; he meant to tell Yonan who had shaved it, and did not; Yonan probably knew. In the evening Yonan went to the gate and lit the lamp and went off with the empty oil jug toward the north end of the ward; at the corner of the ward street A-Lian happened to be coming back from the West Market; the two of them met and went north together.

That night A-Lian had Yonan sit at the chopping board and said: at the monastery you said that from now on you'd learn a second character. She took a charred stick and wrote on the board the character lian, lotus. She had learned this character herself after coming back to Chang'an: to open the shop she had to write the menu board, and she went to an old letter-writer at the corner of the West Market and asked him one character at a time, and when she'd asked cake and soup, asked her own name in passing too, took it home and traced it, traced it a whole winter. Yonan had learned this character under Jingjing's lamp at Xizhou, written it twenty-odd times, and written it later in the sand tray too, written and smoothed it away, and never shown it to anyone. He took the stick and copied it; the lian, connect, at the bottom he wrote too wide, the che, cart, in its middle as big as a real cart, squeezing both sides crooked. A-Lian looked at it and laughed, the lines at the corners of her eyes all coming out at once; Yonan watched her laugh, and the stick in his hand stopped. He wrote it a dozen-odd times, and in the last the cart was a little smaller, but still crooked. A-Lian said: that'll do; your lian isn't like mine, but I know it's yours. He took a sheet of paper, traced the last one onto it, folded it, and tucked it into his left sleeve.

The second day, Yonan went with A-Lian to the West Market.

A-Lian said the people of the West Market all know you, only they don't know what you're like in the shop; go and sit a day, let them see, and after that they won't ask. So Yonan went, and squatted before the oven tending the fire. When the young helper came and saw an old hu in a black robe squatting at the oven he jumped; A-Lian said this is the master of your house; the young man bowed to Yonan and Yonan bowed to him. After the noon drum an idler who often ran up an account came in, saw Yonan's beardless face, and laughed, and said: A-Lian, I hear you've married a monk; so monks take wives too. A-Lian said he's not a monk; how many cakes? The idler looked at her face and said no more, took his cakes, threw down his money and left; on the board he still owed three cakes, and A-Lian did not mention them.

In the latter part of the afternoon, when the shop was quiet, Yonan went to that small accounts board at the door and looked at it line by line; he came to a letter written backwards and pointed it out to her; A-Lian said let it be backwards; as long as people know it's them, that's enough. Yonan asked whether he was in it. A-Lian said: you don't owe anything; why would I put you down? Yonan asked for the charcoal and in the empty space at the very bottom of the board wrote a line: his own name, the letters thin, slanting right, the strokes ending in a small hook; after it he spelled three Tang sounds: qian bing yi, owes one cake. A-Lian asked which one he owed. Yonan said: this morning's—he'd eaten it on the sly in front of the oven; it was burnt, and he'd been too embarrassed to say. A-Lian laughed aloud and said: then that one, you can pay off slowly.

In the evening, before the gong, Yonan went back to the monastery to light the lamp, and when it was lit went in at the gate and walked all the way to that row of small rooms on the north side of the back courtyard, and stopped at the door of Narsai's. A lamp was lit inside; through the crack of the door came a thin yellow line that fell across his instep; there was no sound inside.

Jingjing at that moment was in his own room at the north end of the west wing; he pushed open the window to throw out a basin of brush-washing water and saw Yonan standing under the gallery. Yonan raised his hand, and the hand stopped an inch from the door, stopped a while, came down; rose again, came down again; then he turned and left, his footsteps sounding a few times on the flagstones of the front courtyard and then no more. Jingjing threw the basin of water out softly at the foot of the window and shut the window. That night he got up once; the yellow line was still under Narsai's door, and inside there was the sound of bamboo being scraped, sha, sha, scraping right up to the fourth watch.

When Yonan got back to the north end of the ward, A-Lian asked why he'd been so long. Yonan said he'd gone to stand a while at Narsai's door, and hadn't knocked. A-Lian said: that elder who stood under the gallery trimming pens on the wedding day, didn't drink one mouthful of soup, looked over this way several times, and every time he looked bent his head and trimmed another pen—she'd run a shop in the West Market nine years; of those who owed, she could tell at one look which would come and pay; that elder was one who wanted to come and pay. Yonan heard it, ate his cake, and washed his hands at the well, more quickly than usual.

On the morning of the third day A-Lian as usual picked out twenty cakes, put them in the bamboo basket, and handed it to Yonan. Yonan, holding the basket, looked back at her once at the courtyard gate. A-Lian was squatting at the little oven sticking in cakes, and did not raise her head, and said: "Come back early, Yuenan. There's a lot of dough today; I can't knead it all by myself." A Tang mouth was not used to his name; she had called him so thirty years ago, and he had never corrected her. Yonan said he would, and went off carrying the basket.

Jingjing was up early that day too. The night before he had translated the passage of the shepherd: the shepherd who lays down his life for the sheep; the word she, to give up, was too like Buddhist talk; the word juan, to donate, too like officials' talk; he had thought half the night and not settled it, the ink in the inkstone had dried, and before light he took a small water jar to the well at the monastery gate to draw water. The water of the well in the back courtyard was bitter, and ink ground with it went harsh; the water for his ink he always drew from this well at the gate.

He came to the inside of the monastery gate and saw across the threshold that nine people had come to stand under the locust tree: the lame old man, a blind old woman, a few porters, and a barefoot child who was first there every day. Yonan finished giving out the cakes, set the basket on the well rim, drew up a bucket of water, squatted, and put both hands into the water. Locust flowers fell one by one, floated in the bucket, fell on the blue stone by the well rim, and soaked by the splashed water stuck there in a yellow layer. Jingjing stopped inside the threshold; when Yonan was washing his hands, he never disturbed him.

Narsai came up behind him. He walked very fast; at the threshold he saw Jingjing, paused a moment, and stepped over. In his right hand was that little pen-knife, in his left a bamboo stylus, sharpened at one end, still square at the other, bamboo shavings stuck to his cuff. Lingbao had just been shouting in the courtyard that Elder Yonan was giving out cakes at the gate; he had probably heard, stood up and come out at once, without even putting down the knife.

Yonan saw Narsai, took his hands out of the water, shook them, and stood up; his knees cracked; he was seventy-one. The two of them stood with the bucket between them. Narsai spoke in Syriac, and Jingjing inside the threshold heard it clearly. Narsai said: the Patriarch has granted it. Yonan said: he has. Narsai said: he submitted to the Patriarch. Yonan said he knew. Narsai paused, held the bamboo stylus in his left hand out a little further, and said: last night you came to my door and I didn't open it; I hadn't yet thought what to say to you; this stylus is for her; she keeps her accounts with charcoal, and it breaks every time she writes; a bamboo stylus sharpened and dipped in ink, written on wood, won't rub off; one end isn't finished; see first whether it suits.

Yonan looked at that stylus, and the lame old man said afterwards that the look on his face was as if someone had given him a cake. He stepped forward one pace and reached out to take it, and under his foot the blue stone, soaked with water and coated with locust flowers—he set his foot on it, and his whole body pitched forward.

Narsai reached out to catch him. Jingjing saw it clearly: Narsai's left hand went out first and gripped Yonan's arm, and could not hold him; Yonan was falling, and Narsai's right hand went out after to take him round the waist, and in that hand was still the knife. At the wedding feast, when Lingbao tripped at the foot of the west wing steps, Narsai had reached out like this too, and that time he turned his wrist out, the blade facing outward, and Shiyiniang beside him called: Elder, the knife. This time no one called; there was no time for his hand to turn; and Yonan's falling body struck full on that hand.

The two of them went down kneeling together by the well. The bucket overturned; locust flowers swirled in the water.

The barefoot child ran screaming into the monastery. Jingjing stepped over the threshold and ran out; Narsai was still kneeling, both arms round Yonan; under Yonan's left ribs a dark patch was spreading across the grey robe, spreading very slowly, like ink dropped on paper. The knife had gone in all the way; only the ground-bone handle showed. Narsai looked down at the hilt; that hand was trembling; he wanted to let go and dared not let go; he raised his head, saw Jingjing, his mouth open, and not one word came out. The bamboo stylus had fallen into the water and floated under the well rim.

Yonan turned his face first toward Narsai and said something in Syriac, very soft; Jingjing heard it clearly only when he knelt down:

"It was you who caught me."

Narsai's lips moved, and nothing came. Yonan said it again, it was you who caught me, and when he had said it laid his hand on Narsai's hand that held the knife, to tell him not to move. Jingjing knew the knife must not be drawn now; Ishaq had said, draw it, and the blood won't stop. He shouted through the gate for Ishaq, shouted for Yisi, shouted till his voice cracked.

Only then did Yonan turn his face to look at Jingjing, looked very closely, as if to fix this face in memory, or as if he had long since fixed it, and now was only checking it. He spoke in Tang, very slowly, very awkwardly, just as twenty years ago in that small room at Ctesiphon when he first said to Jingjing, "I am called Yonan":

"Lot of dough. She can't knead it all by herself."

Jingjing said he'd go; he'd knead it. Yonan seemed at ease, and leaned his head back against Narsai's arm. Locust flowers fell on his beardless chin, and he did not brush them away.

The fifteenth of the first month of the fifth year of Tianbao, the Lantern Festival that year: for three nights the ward gates were not shut. At dusk he lit that oil-dish lamp at the monastery gate, and when it was lit he saw the people of the ward street pouring out in crowd after crowd, carrying lanterns, carrying children, the main street outside the ward gate as bright as day. He stood a while under the lamp, did not go back to the back courtyard, and went off toward the West Market.

The West Market did not shut that night either; the streets were full of lanterns, paper ones, silk ones, made into fish, made into lotus flowers. He pushed his way to the mouth of that back lane; the back lane was dark, only the window paper of that little room showing a point of light, far smaller than the lanterns in the street. He looked at that point of light, and went in. She opened the door, an old jacket over her shoulders, her hair loose, that wooden writing tablet still in her hand; seeing it was he, the tablet slid from her hand to the floor, and she did not pick it up. She asked why he had come; he said he'd come to see the lanterns. She laughed, said the lanterns are outside, what lanterns do you come to see here, let him in, and shut the door.

The room was very cold; the fire in the stove was nearly out; she squatted to add sticks, and he squatted too, the two of them facing that little fire. The drums and music outside came in in waves; a child was shouting to see the dragon lanterns. When the fire was up she warmed her hands over it, reached across, and took his hands. His hands were cold the whole year round, washed several times a day, washed till there was no warmth in them at all. She held them, rubbed them, and said: your hands are like ice; were you fished out of a well? He said he'd just washed them. She said: how many times a day do you wash them, to make them so cold? The Lord doesn't want you to wash like this either. She held them like that, held them till his hands were as warm as hers.

Of that night what he remembered were all small things. There was a smell of woodsmoke in her hair, from the eating-house stove. The chilblains on her hands were split open, and felt rough on the backs of his hands, prickling a little. She turned his hand over and in his palm wrote those four letters, and at the Nun her finger flicked up, flicked till his palm tickled, and he laughed, and she laughed too, and asked what he was laughing at, and he said: good. On the window paper the lanterns outside were reflected, now red, now yellow; someone carrying a lantern passed the mouth of the lane. Then he fell asleep, slept more soundly than he had in many years, and when he woke the drums and music outside had stopped, it was not yet light, and she was asleep leaning on his shoulder, one hand still holding his hand, and that hand was warm. He dared not move at all, but listened to her breathing in her sleep, listened as the last stick in the stove burned out and cracked once.

For thirty years after, he never let himself think that night through from beginning to end; when he thought of its beginning he knelt and begged the Lord to take it away, and when it would not be taken away, went to wash his hands. Now it turned itself up, thing by thing: the smell of woodsmoke, the split chilblains, the Nun flicking up in his palm, and last of all that hand, warm, holding his.

By the time Ishaq arrived, Yonan was no longer speaking.

Isaac came running holding Ishaq up by the arm; Ishaq leaned on the well rim and gasped a few breaths, squatted to feel Yonan's pulse, raised his head and looked at Jingjing, and shook his head. Isaac turned and ran back for the medicine box, and when he had brought it, it was no longer needed. Yisi came too; Shiyiniang stood inside the threshold holding Lingbao, pressing Lingbao's face into her breast. Yisi squatted down and pried Narsai's fingers open one by one; Narsai's fingers were rigid; as each was pried open it closed back a little. When they were open, Yisi drew out the knife, wiped it on his sleeve, and put it away in his own sleeve.

Narsai was still holding Yonan; Yisi called him twice before he let go. Jingjing reached out and took Yonan and leaned him against the well rim. Narsai stood up, his knees and the front of his robe all blood and water; he looked down at his two hands, turned and went in through the monastery gate, stumbled once crossing the threshold, steadied himself on the doorframe, and did not look back.

Yonan leaned against the well rim, his eyes still open. Jingjing said "Elder," said it twice, and no one answered. He brushed two locust flowers from Yonan's face and reached out to close his eyes, tried twice, and closed them. Yonan's two hands lay on his knees, palms up, still wet; the blister raised on his right forefinger had broken; the sinews on the backs of his hands stood out one by one; the lines of the skin were like a dried riverbed; they were an old man's hands, just washed. Jingjing took those hands and held them; the hands slowly went cold, and still he held them. The lame old man pushed through to Yisi, pulled at his sleeve, and said over and over: that elder went to catch him, he saw it, the stone was all water, all locust flowers; and the blind old woman said too: she heard it, it was the sound of someone slipping. Yisi listened, nodding, nodding.

The one who went to the West Market with the news was Jingjing. His knees and sleeves were covered with Yonan's blood, and he did not change; he went all the way past the corner of the Liquan Ward, where the bench from the carriage-blocking a few days before was still leaning against the foot of the wall.

A-Lian was kneading dough, both hands pressed on that great lump of dough, pushing, pulling. She raised her head, saw Jingjing standing at the door, saw his sleeves, and her hands stopped on the dough. Jingjing opened his mouth; the scholars of Chang'an said his Tang was better than a Tang's, and now he could not say one sentence; he stood in the doorway, his two hands hanging. A-Lian wiped her hands clean on her apron, wiped the dough from between her fingers too, untied the apron and folded it, covered that lump of dough with a wet cloth, and put up the shutters one by one, not one of them crooked. When they were up, she said: let's go. At the north gate of the West Market, without turning her head, she asked how he died. Jingjing said he slipped, and Elder Narsai reached out to catch him with the pen-knife in his hand. A few steps more, and she asked what he said at the end. Jingjing said he said: lot of dough; you can't knead it all by yourself. A-Lian asked no more, and all the way to the Yining Ward did not open her mouth again.

By the custom of the banks of the Two Rivers, the dead were buried the same day.

Before the burial A-Lian went to the room Yonan had lived in at the monastery to fetch his clothes. In the room were a narrow bed, a wooden chest, a wooden cross on the wall. In the chest were folded two old robes, one black and one grey, and under the robes a cloth bundle, and wrapped in it a stub of charcoal, so short two fingers could not hold it, its broken end worn round; in the autumn of the fourth year of Tianbao, it was with a stub of charcoal that he had taught her, on a spoiled wooden tablet, to draw those four letters. On the windowsill were stacked several dozen small boards, all the ones she had had the porter bring with the cakes: hot; it's turned cool; Old Wang has paid; and one with nothing written, only a heart drawn. She put the bottom one, "hot," inside her jacket, and stacked the rest just as they were, as neatly as Yonan had stacked them. She said: take the grey robe; he was used to black at the monastery; over there, let him wear grey for once.

When the body was washed, A-Lian said the hands were hers to do. She knelt on the ground, put Yonan's two hands into the basin, and washed them finger by finger, very slowly, as he had washed them himself that morning. When they were washed she dried them and straightened his sleeves, and from the left sleeve drew out a folded paper; she opened it: a lian, the cart in its middle still too big. She put the paper away in her own sleeve. Lingbao fished the bamboo stylus out of the muddy water under the well rim and wiped it clean, and Shiyiniang took it to her; she looked at the unfinished square end, did not ask who had shaved it, and put that away in her sleeve too.

The grave was outside the Kaiyuan Gate, beside a small willow. Yonan was wrapped in white cloth, his head to the east; Ishaq said that all his life at night he had looked east, and lying so, he would go on looking east. Marya led the psalms, in Syriac; A-Lian tugged at Jingjing's sleeve and said: you translate for me. Jingjing stood beside her, and Marya said a line and he translated a line, very low. When they came to: those who sleep, when the day comes, the Lord will raise them up—he stopped at the word raise; the word in the scripture meant the dead living again; in the west wing he had thought about it several times, and could not find a Tang character that would hold it. A-Lian waited, and he said: raise—it's waking, the way you wake in the morning. A-Lian nodded and said: then she'd wait. Babai carved Yonan's name on the wooden cross and asked A-Lian whether she wanted Tang characters carved; A-Lian said: carve a heart.

When the earth was filled in, the sun was just going down. A-Lian did not kneel, nor weep, but stood until the reading was done, and only then turned and walked back toward the city; Jingjing wanted to see her home, Shiyiniang wanted to go with her; she shook her head at both, and went in through the Kaiyuan Gate alone.

Narsai did not go to the grave. He was in Yisi's room the whole time.

That night Jingjing went back to the west wing; the passage of scripture was still spread on the table; beneath the word she he added the word shen, body—to give up the body—looked at it, and struck it out again, leaving only she. He could not sit still, and went out of the monastery gate. The well had been washed down; the blue stone was damp, black under the lamp; his water jar still lay overturned under the well rim; he picked it up and went toward the north end of the ward. The gate of the small courtyard was shut; through the crack came a little red glow from the small oven. He did not knock, but sat down on the stone step outside the gate. There was no weeping in the courtyard; after a while the charcoal in the oven crackled once; after another while someone added a piece of charcoal to the oven. He sat till the small hours; the dew soaked his robe, and he did not move.

When the ward drum had beaten its first round, the courtyard gate opened. A-Lian came out carrying a small lantern, and holding the lantern forward saw Jingjing on the step, his robe wet halfway up, his sleeves still with yesterday's blood. She did not ask when he had come; she turned back into the house, brought out an empty bamboo basket, put it in his hand, and said: take this, come with me.

At the shop she took down the shutters and lit the oven; the lump of dough half kneaded yesterday had stood all night under its wet cloth and risen just right. She had him wipe the board, had him roll up his sleeves, had him wash his hands clean, divided the lump of dough and pushed half across to him, and said: knead; push forward, pull back; use the heel of your hand, not your fingers. Jingjing kneaded very clumsily; the dough stuck to his hands and would not shake off; she said nothing to him, but kneaded her own half. When the first batch of cakes came out she picked out twenty, put them in the bamboo basket, and had him take them back to the monastery gate. He carried the basket back under the locust tree, gave out the cakes, drew a bucket of water at the well, washed his hands, and went back to the West Market and went on kneading.

After the noon drum, the idler of the day before yesterday came again. On the small accounts board at the door he still owed three cakes. He looked at the bamboo basket at the door, looked at A-Lian, then looked at the young hu monk with his sleeves rolled up kneading dough at the board, and opened his mouth as if to say something. A-Lian did not raise her head, and said: "How many cakes?"
