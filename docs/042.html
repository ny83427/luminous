Volume Seven　A-Lian

Chapter 42　Yonan

After he married, Yonan lived three days.

Those three days, the people of the monastery afterwards told over and over, told for many years. So many told them that each told them a little differently. Some said it rained those three days, some said it did not; some said Yonan never came through the monastery gate in those three days, some said he came once. A storyteller can only tell what everyone agrees on.

What everyone agrees on is this.

The first day, Yonan rose before A-Lian.

A-Lian's shop was at the south end of the West Market, its front only one bay wide, the hu bread oven set up at the door, two low tables inside, and further in a little partitioned room, and behind the room a courtyard the size of your palm, with a water jar in it. There was a boy who worked in the shop, who came at daylight.

When Yonan got up it was still dark. He felt his way into the little courtyard, ladled a dipper of water from the jar, and washed his hands. When he had washed them he stood there not knowing what to do next. He had lived all his life in a monastery, and all his life when the bell rang he rose, and when he rose he went to the hall. Here there was no bell, and no hall.

Then A-Lian got up too. She saw him standing in the courtyard, said nothing, pulled him over to the oven and pushed a pair of fire tongs into his hand.

"Mind the fire," she said.

So Yonan minded the fire.

A-Lian mixed the dough, kneaded it, rolled out the breads, scattered sesame on them, stuck them into the oven. She did these things very fast, without a word, her hands rising and falling on the board as if playing a zither. Yonan squatted by the oven with the tongs, watching the charcoal in the firebox redden bit by bit.

When the first batch came out, the sky was just turning grey.

A-Lian picked out twenty breads, put them in a bamboo basket, and handed it to him.

"Go on," she said.

Yonan carried the basket out of the north gate of the West Market, went north, through two wards, to the Yining Ward. Under the locust tree there were already people standing, seven or eight of them, all familiar faces. They saw him turn the corner from the south and were taken aback, and then a lame old man laughed and cupped his hands to him.

Yonan gave out the bread piece by piece.

It was the first time in eighteen years he had given out bread A-Lian had made. It was thinner than the monastery's, more fragrant, the sesame scattered evenly. When it was all given out he drew a bucket of water at the well and washed his hands as always.

That evening, when the shop's shutters were up, A-Lian lit a lamp and had Yonan sit at the kneading board.

"You said at the monastery," said A-Lian, "that from now on you'd learn the second character."

Yonan said: "Jingjing said that."

"He said it," said A-Lian. "Do you own to it?"

Yonan thought and said: "I own to it."

A-Lian took a burnt stick of firewood and wrote a character on the board. She wrote it not fast, stroke by stroke.

蓮. Lian. Lotus.

She had learned this character herself after she came back to Chang'an. A shop needs a menu board, and the characters on the board she had gone to ask, one by one, of the old gentleman at the street corner who wrote letters for people; when she had asked for bread and soup, she asked for her own name as well.

Yonan looked at the character a long time.

In Xizhou, at night, kneeling on the other side of the wall, he had said this word over and over. Said it twenty years. But he could not write it.

He took the stick and copied it. The grass radical came out well enough, but the 連 beneath he wrote too wide; the 車, the cart, in the middle was as big as a real cart and squeezed both sides crooked.

A-Lian watched, and smiled a little.

When she smiled, all the lines at the corners of her eyes came out at once. Yonan looked at her, and the stick in his hand stopped.

"Again," said A-Lian.

He wrote another. Still crooked.

That night he wrote a dozen and more. At the last one the cart was a little smaller, but still crooked. A-Lian said that would do. He put the stick down, looked at the dozen crooked lotuses on the board a while, took a sheet of paper, traced the last one onto it, folded it, and put it in his sleeve.

Before she blew out the lamp, A-Lian asked one thing.

"That bowl of ginger water that day—did he drink it?"

"He drank it," said Yonan.

"And when he'd drunk it?"

"He washed the bowl and took it back to the kitchen."

A-Lian asked no more. She blew out the lamp.

The second day, an idler came into the shop to eat, saw an old hu in a grey robe squatting by the oven, beardless, and laughed.

"A-Lian," said the idler, "I hear you've married a monk?"

"Mm," said A-Lian.

"Monks marry?"

"He's not a monk," said A-Lian. "How many breads?"

"Two."

A-Lian picked out two breads, wrapped them in a lotus leaf, and handed them over. The idler took them and wanted to say something more, looked at her face, looked at the old hu by the oven, didn't, paid, and went.

Yonan squatted by the oven, and never raised his head.

That day he burned himself on the oven, on the forefinger of his right hand, and it blistered. A-Lian put a little lard on it and bound it with a strip of cloth. He looked at that bound finger a long while and said: "Today I can't wash my hands."

A-Lian said: "Wash once less, and the Lord won't blame you."

Yonan thought and said: "How do you know?"

A-Lian paid him no attention and went back to rolling bread.

In the evening, Yonan said he would go back to the monastery a while.

A-Lian asked what for, and he said to fetch a few old clothes. A-Lian asked no more.

When he got back to the monastery it was already dark. The small lamp at the monastery gate was lit, shining on the well under the locust tree. He passed the well, went in at the gate, through the front courtyard, past the hall, to the back courtyard. The benches in the back courtyard had all been taken away; there was only the jujube tree, its blossom fallen all over the ground, unswept.

He did not go to fetch clothes.

He went along the gallery of the east wing to the innermost room, and stopped.

It was Narsai's room.

A lamp was lit inside, and the light came through the crack of the door, a thin yellow line falling on Yonan's feet. There was no sound inside—no chanting, no turning of pages.

Yonan stood at the door and raised his hand.

His hand stopped in mid-air, stopped a long time.

Then he let it fall, turned, walked back along the gallery, out of the back courtyard, out of the monastery gate. Passing under the locust tree he stopped a moment, looked at the well, looked at the small lamp at the gate, and then went south.

Inside the room, Narsai sat under the lamp with that little knife and a reed pen in his hands. He listened to the footsteps outside the door come, stop, and go away.

He did not move at all.

On the morning of the third day, A-Lian picked out twenty breads as usual and put them in the bamboo basket.

Yonan took the basket, stood at the door of the shop, and looked back at her.

A-Lian was sticking breads into the oven, and did not raise her head.

"Come back early, Yuenan," she said. "There's a lot of dough today; one person can't knead it all."

Thirty years before she had called him that. Tang mouths were not used to the sound, and he had never once corrected her.

"Mm," said Yonan.

He took the basket and went.

That morning it did not rain. On this, afterwards, everyone agrees.

Nine people came under the locust tree.

A lame old man, a blind old woman, three porters, two women fleeing famine from the countryside, a young man carrying a child, and a little boy of seven or eight, barefoot, from a poor family in the ward, who came every day and every day was first.

Yonan gave out the bread piece by piece.

When he had finished, there were eleven breads left in the basket. He set the basket on the well curb, drew a bucket of water from the well, put the bucket down, squatted, untied the cloth from the forefinger of his right hand, and put both hands into the water.

The barefoot boy squatted beside him gnawing his bread, watching him wash his hands.

Locust blossoms fell one by one and floated in the bucket.

Yonan finished washing his left hand and washed his right. When he came to that blistered forefinger, he washed it very slowly, very carefully.

Just then, someone came out of the monastery gate.

It was Narsai.

Narsai came to the well and stopped.

He wore his grey robe, very old, washed pale, patched at the collar. His face was as always—the high brow ridges, the straight nose, the eyes like two stones.

Yonan raised his head and saw him.

Yonan's two hands were still in the water. He took them out, shook them, and stood up. As he stood his knees cracked; he was seventy-one.

The two of them stood face to face, the bucket between them.

The barefoot boy looked from one to the other, his mouth full of bread, and stopped chewing.

Narsai spoke. He spoke in Syriac, which the boy did not understand, nor the lame old man. Afterwards it was Narsai himself who told what was said, word by word, as he recited the Law, like knocking in nails.

"The Patriarch has allowed it," said Narsai.

"He has," said Yonan.

"I obey the Patriarch."

"I know."

Narsai paused.

"I do not forgive you."

Yonan looked at him. His hands were still wet, the water running down his fingers and dripping onto the bluestone of the well curb, drop by drop.

"I know," said Yonan.

Narsai took that little knife from his sleeve.

The knife was very small, its blade no longer than a finger, the edge thin, the handle ground bone, thirty years of trimming pens. The morning sun came through the gaps in the locust leaves and fell on the edge, and it flashed once.

Yonan did not move.

He did not step back, nor raise his hands. He stood by the well, his two wet hands hanging at his sides, looking at Narsai, looking at the knife. The look on his face, the lame old man said afterwards, was like a man waiting to be given a piece of bread.

Narsai stepped forward and drove the knife in.

The barefoot boy screamed, dropped his bread, and ran into the monastery.

The blind old woman heard the scream, clutched the sleeve of the person beside her, and kept asking: "What is it? What is it?" No one answered her. The three porters backed away, backed to the foot of the ward wall. The young man with the child turned and went, went a few steps, then stopped and turned round to look.

Yonan leaned against the well curb and slowly sat down.

On his grey robe, on the left side below the ribs, a dark patch was spreading, spreading very slowly, like ink dropped on paper. He looked down at it, then raised his head and looked at Narsai.

Narsai still stood there with the knife in his hand. There was blood on the knife; he did not wipe it, nor throw it away.

Yonan opened his mouth.

He spoke in Tang. Not Syriac—Tang. He spoke very slowly, very stiffly, squeezing out the words one at a time, as twenty years before in that little room in Ctesiphon, the first time he said to Jingjing, my name is Yonan.

"Don't hurt her."

Narsai looked at him.

"She is the Lord's sheep."

When Yonan had said it, he seemed tired. He leaned his head back against the well curb. The locust blossom fell, fell on his face, on his beardless chin. He did not brush it off.

Narsai stood there looking at him.

After a while Narsai bent down, picked up the bamboo basket from the well curb—there were still eleven breads in it—set it by Yonan's hand, set it straight, and stood up.

Then he turned and walked in at the monastery gate.

Jingjing was in the east wing.

He had risen early that day and was sitting by the window translating a passage. He had come to the verse the good shepherd giveth his life for the sheep, and was thinking how to write giveth his life—whether with 捨, to let go, or with 捐, to give up. Outside the window there was a sudden scream, then the slap of bare feet on the ground, and a little boy burst into the courtyard shouting something, shouting it in no sort of sentence.

Jingjing threw down his brush and ran out.

He ran through the front courtyard to the monastery gate, and ran straight into someone coming in from outside.

It was Narsai.

The two of them passed each other at the threshold. Jingjing saw the knife in his hand, saw what was on the knife. Narsai saw him too. Narsai did not stop, nor turn aside; he went past him towards Yisi's courtyard.

Jingjing stood on the threshold, stunned a moment, then rushed out.

Yonan leaned against the well curb, his eyes still open.

Jingjing knelt beside him and called him. Called him "Elder." Called once, and again.

Yonan did not answer.

Jingjing put out his hand and touched his face. It was still warm, with two locust blossoms lying on it. He brushed the blossoms away, and reached to close his eyes. He closed them once, and they did not stay closed; he closed them again, and they stayed.

Then he saw Yonan's hands.

Yonan's two hands lay on his knees, palms up, still wet. On the forefinger of the right hand was a blister, broken, red. The tendons on the backs of the hands stood out one by one, and the skin was all fine lines, like a dried riverbed.

They were an old man's hands, just washed.

Jingjing took those hands in his.

He held them a long time. Held them while they slowly grew cold, and still held them. The people under the locust tree slowly gathered round in a ring, and no one spoke. The barefoot boy had run back again and stood outside the ring, crying and hiccupping.

Later Yisi came too.

It was Jingjing who went to the West Market with the news.

When he came into that cross street, A-Lian was kneading dough. A great mass of dough lay on the board, and she had both hands pressed into it—push, pull, push, pull. There was a lot of dough today; one person couldn't knead it all.

She raised her head and saw Jingjing standing at the door.

She saw his face, and saw his sleeve. There was a dark patch on his sleeve.

Her hands stopped on the dough.

Jingjing opened his mouth, and could say nothing.

A-Lian looked at him, looked a while. Then she took her hands from the dough and wiped them on her apron, wiped them very carefully, the dough between her fingers too. She took off the apron, folded it, and laid it on the board. She went to the door and put the shutters up one by one, put them up very steadily, not one of them crooked.

When they were up, she stood outside the door and looked at Jingjing.

"Let's go," she said.

Yonan was buried that evening.

By the old custom of the banks of the Two Rivers, when a man dies he must be buried the same day, not left overnight. Gabriel and Ishaq washed his body. When they came to his hands, A-Lian said she would do the hands.

She drew a basin of water, knelt on the ground, put Yonan's two hands in it, and washed them finger by finger. When she came to the broken blister on the right forefinger, she washed it very slowly, very carefully, just as he had washed it himself that morning.

When she had washed them she straightened his sleeves for him. As she straightened the left sleeve she felt something inside it, stiff—a folded sheet of paper.

She took the paper out, opened it, and looked at it once.

It was one character, traced crookedly, the cart in the middle still too big.

She looked at it a long time. Then she folded the paper and put it in her own sleeve.

The grave was outside the Kaiyuan Gate, where the people of the monastery were all buried when they died—a dozen and more mounds of earth with wooden crosses set before them. Yonan's grave was at the very end, beside a young willow. Babai carved his name on the cross in Syriac letters, and asked A-Lian whether she wanted Tang characters carved too.

A-Lian said: "Carve the character heart."

Babai carved it.

As the last earth went in, the sun went down. The people of the monastery recited prayers before the grave, recited a long time. A-Lian stood at the back, did not kneel, did not weep. She stood, stood until the prayers were finished and everyone had risen, and only then turned and walked back towards the city. Jingjing wanted to see her home, and she shook her head.

She went in through the Kaiyuan Gate alone, and went south.

Early the next morning, that little shop at the south end of the West Market, only one bay wide, took down its shutters as usual.

The oven was lit, the charcoal reddening bit by bit. On the board lay a great mass of dough, the half kneaded yesterday, covered with a damp cloth, left overnight, risen just right.

A-Lian kneaded it alone.

When the first batch came out, the sky was just turning grey. She picked out twenty breads, put them in a bamboo basket, set it on the bench at the door, and covered it with a cloth.

After a while, the idler of the day before yesterday came again. On the little account plank at the door, he still owed for three breads. He stood at the door, looked at the bamboo basket, looked at A-Lian. News travels fast in the West Market; he had probably heard. He opened his mouth to say something.

A-Lian did not raise her head.

"How many breads?" she said.
