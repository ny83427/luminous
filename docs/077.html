Volume Twelve　Jade Gate

Chapter 77　Old Zhu

On the evening of the day they went out of the Kaiyuan Gate, the caravan halted at the Linggao post station. Linggao was twenty li from Chang'an, the first station going west; in former days, when the people of Chang'an saw someone off beyond the passes, they saw him this far, broke a willow twig under the willows outside the station, drank a cup of wine, and went back. That day the willows outside the station had shed all their leaves; the branches were bare, and when the wind blew they lashed the station wall with a crack, crack. The caravan unloaded on the open ground outside the station; the camels knelt to chew their fodder; Headman Cao's men lit a fire and boiled a pot of water.

Those being sent back were not only the dozen-odd of the Daqin Monastery. The roll the Office of Receptions made held forty-odd souls in all—of the Daqin Monastery, of the Zoroastrian shrine in Liquan Ward, of the Zoroastrian shrine in Buzheng Ward, and a few households of the Magi—all put into this one caravan going west. The escort was two clerks sent by the Office of Receptions, one old, one young, riding donkeys, carrying the roll, who said they'd see them as far as Jingzhou, and west of Jingzhou it was no longer their charge. That evening the old clerk stood at the station gate, unrolled the roll, had everyone stand in a row, and called the names one by one; one called, one answered, and when each answered he made a tick in the roll.

He came to the Daqin Monastery, to Yi Lingbao, and He Siniang answered for him; Lingbao lay in the cart wrapped in two felts, coughing. He came to Jingfu, and Jingfu answered. He came to Joseph, Elijah, Salang; came to the two old brothers; one answered; one lay in the cart and could not make a sound, and Elijah answered for him. He came to the Zoroastrian shrine; the first called was an old man surnamed Zhu, past sixty, not tall, a little hunchbacked, his face a blackish red, as if roasted by fire all his life; he answered once, his voice very clear. The old clerk made his tick and said: Zhu, keeper of the fire of the Zoroastrian shrine of Liquan Ward, a man of Kang; to be sent back to his own place. The old man stood there listening, and only when the clerk called the next did he turn and go back to his family.

His family were five: himself, his old wife, a son, a daughter-in-law, and a grandson of ten or so. They had little baggage—two chests, a few rolls of felt, a pot; the old man held in his arms a clay jar, its mouth tied over with oilcloth, tied very tight; he held it; when he sat down he still held it; when he lay down he set it on his chest.

That night Jingfu fell to talking with him by the fire. It was the old man who spoke first. He came to the fire of the Daqin Monastery's people, squatted down, warmed his hands, looked at Jingfu, looked at Salang, looked at Lingbao lying in the cart, and asked whether they were of the Daqin Monastery in Yining Ward; Jingfu said they were; the old man said he knew of them; he said your monastery had a stele, and it's been buried; the people of Liquan Ward are all talking of it. Jingfu said yes, it's buried. The old man nodded and said: that's good, then; stone buried in earth—stone feels no pain.

He said further that his surname was Zhu; the people of the shrine all called him Old Zhu; he had kept the fire forty years; his Pa kept it forty years; and before that, his Pa's grandfather—the old fire-keeper who in the first year of Shangyuan was beaten to death with a carrying pole beside the altar. He said: this business—your monastery's people probably know of it too.

Jingfu said they knew. He said that year he was not yet born; the business was recorded in the monastery's accounts, entered by Elder Samaan; the next day the Great Worthy Yi went to the Zoroastrian shrine with money, with medicine, with bread, and bowed deeply before the old fire-keeper's widow; among those who went that time was a fourteen-year-old boy—his Master.

Old Zhu said he knew; his Pa had told him. He said that year his Pa was twelve or thirteen, standing in the courtyard, and a fat hu monk handed him a dry cake; he took it, gripped it in his hand, didn't eat it; his granny was in the inner room, and he didn't dare eat. He said his Pa told him later: that cake he gripped all night, gripped it to crumbs; the next morning, while his granny slept, he sat alone by the fire and ate that handful of crumbs, and when he'd eaten them he wept; he said he wept for his grandfather, and for that cake too; that cake was sweet—it had honey in it; he'd never eaten a cake with honey in it.

Jingfu listened. He said that fat hu monk was called Ishaq, an elder of the monastery, long dead—died in the Zhenyuan years. Old Zhu said he knew; his Pa said the man was called Ishaq, and when he talked he said mo pa, "don't be afraid," as mo pa, "grind and crawl"; his Pa imitated him all his life, imitated him saying grind and crawl, for him to hear.

Salang beside them asked what he was holding in his arms. Old Zhu looked down at the clay jar, felt the oilcloth over its mouth, and said: ash. He said in the eighth month when the decree came down, the shrine was sealed and the fire was forbidden to be lit; that basin of fire his family's ancestors brought from Kang had burned in Liquan Ward over a hundred years and never gone out a single day—not the year An Lushan broke into Chang'an, not the year the Tibetans came into the city, not the year of Zhu Ci; in the first year of Shangyuan, when your monastery's people threw water on it, his Pa's grandfather shielded it with his body, shielded it till he died, and it didn't go out. He said on the twenty-third of the eighth month the Jingzhao prefecture's men came, stood before the altar, and told him to put it out himself; he wouldn't; the Jingzhao men said: if you don't put it out, we'll pour water on it; he said he'd do it himself. He said the fire he had kept forty years, he put out himself; he didn't pour water on it; he covered it with an iron plate, covered it all night, and lifted it the next morning, and the fire was gone; what was left was a basin of ash, still warm. He put that basin of ash into this jar. He said he didn't know what the ash was for. He said he just wouldn't throw it away; throw it away, and that basin of fire would truly be gone; keep the ash, and the fire is out, but the fire has been.

The caravan went west by the road to Jingzhou, past Xianyang, past Liquan, past Binzhou, ever northwest, colder the farther they went. In the eleventh month the first snow fell; not heavy; it fell on the camels' backs, on the cart awnings, on the felts Lingbao was wrapped in; every watch He Siniang brushed the snow off the felts. Lingbao's cough grew worse day by day; at night he coughed so that the people in the post station could not sleep; Headman Cao came to look once, looked, and had another felt awning added to Lingbao's cart.

Past Liquan there was a stretch of slope with ice on the road; Old Zhu's old wife could not keep her feet and fell twice; Salang saw, took her on his back, carried her over that stretch of slope, set her down, and went back and carried Old Zhu's pot over too. That night by the fire Old Zhu asked Salang: your Pa guarded a monastery too, didn't he? Salang said yes; his grandfather did, and his grandfather's father too; he said those who guard monasteries can't bear to see people fall.

Of those two old brothers, one died at Binzhou. The caravan halted half a month at Binzhou for him, waiting for him to breathe his last, waiting for his burial, and waiting too for Lingbao's cough to ease a little; they had left Chang'an on the twentieth of the tenth month, and by the time they reached Jingzhou it was already the twelfth. He was seventy-nine; since leaving Chang'an he had lain in the cart, unable to eat all the way; at Binzhou they spent the night in the horse shed of the post station; Elijah watched over him; in the middle of the night he called Elijah once and said in Syriac that he was cold; Elijah covered him with his own felt, and by daybreak he was already cold. He had come from the Patriarchate in the Yuanhe years and lived over thirty years in Chang'an; his Syriac name was Thomas; what his Tang name was, the monastery's people could not remember; Jingfu turned through the account books Lingbao kept before he found it: Mingyuan. They buried him on a patch of waste ground outside Binzhou; the ground was frozen; Salang alone dug a whole morning, dug till a man could be laid in it; Jingfu wrote a stake: Mingyuan, monk of the Daqin Monastery of Chang'an; died at Binzhou, eleventh month of the fifth year of Huichang.

That day was the day of the offering once in seven; when they had buried him they held worship in the horse shed of the post station outside Binzhou. Lingbao sat in the cart and had himself helped down and into the horse shed; in the shed was a wooden feeding trough; Jingfu wiped the trough clean, took the glass bowl from his bundle, and set it on the trough; He Siniang took out bread baked on the road and put it in the bowl. Lingbao stood holding the trough; his eyes could not see, and his hands shook badly; he recited the consecration very slowly, word by word; Jingfu stood beside him, his left hand holding the bottom of the glass bowl.

The Zoroastrian families all stood outside the horse shed watching. Old Zhu was there too, holding his clay jar, standing at the horse shed's door, not coming in. Lingbao broke the bread and handed it out piece by piece; when he had handed it to the monastery's people there was still one piece in the bowl; he asked Jingfu who was standing outside; Jingfu said: Old Zhu of the Zoroastrian shrine. Lingbao said: give it to him. Jingfu took that piece of bread to the door of the horse shed and handed it to Old Zhu. Old Zhu looked at the bread, looked at Jingfu, and said he didn't believe in your Lord. Jingfu said he knew; this piece is Chang'an bread, baked on the road, blessed; if Old Zhu doesn't believe, it doesn't matter; just take it. Old Zhu put out one hand, took it, and gripped it in his hand. He did not eat it, nor throw it away; he gripped it, and with his other hand holding the clay jar went back to his family.

They reached Jingzhou in the twelfth month. The town of Jingzhou was on the Jing river, not big, its walls of earth, soldiers standing on the walls; the citadel of the Jingyuan military governor was inside; at the town gate was pasted a notice, which the old clerk read to everyone. The notice said: the Jingyuan circuit is broad of land and few of people; the military farms are short of hands; any vagrants, monks and nuns returned to lay life, or foreign guests being sent back who wish to stay, are permitted to register at Jingzhou, to be given so much land, excused rent and levies for three years, and entered as military-farm households. When the old clerk had read it, he told everyone: we've reached Jingzhou; past Jingzhou is the Tibetans' country; where to go, how to go, and where counts as your own place, the roll doesn't say, and he doesn't know either. He said: if you're willing, stay at Jingzhou; once registered you're registered householders of Jingzhou; farm, pay grain, and after this you're no longer people being sent back.

That night those being sent back debated in the post station, debated all night. Some said stay: going west, past Jingzhou, the Tibetans take duty, and having taken it, whether they let you through no one knows; and even if they do, further west—to Liangzhou, to Shazhou, to Xizhou, to the Pamirs—where is your own place? Their fathers' fathers came from Kang, came from Persia; whether the people over there would still own them, no one knew. Some said go: they were people being sent back; the roll said sent back to their own places; register at Jingzhou, farm the land, and if one day the Jingzhou officials remember and say you're people being sent back—how is it you stayed? they'll be driven out again, and driven where?

The few of the Daqin Monastery debated once too. Jingfu spoke first; he said he wanted to stay. He said he was born and raised in Chang'an; Uncle Lingbao had once left the register and been a registered householder; the Jingzhou notice says returned monks and nuns may register too; the two of them, registered at Jingzhou, would be Tang again; Jingzhou is near Chang'an; if one day the new Son of Heaven changes his mind and allows the Daqin monasteries to reopen, they could go back and be there in a few days; Brothers Joseph, Elijah and Salang are foreigners and can't stay; the three of them go west, reach the Patriarchate, and tell the Patriarch that Chang'an still has people waiting. Lingbao lay in the cart, heard him out, and said: you stay; he won't. He said the Office of Receptions' roll says he's of Balkh, so he'll go toward Balkh; a man nearly eighty registered at Jingzhou can't farm, can't pay grain; it would be registering an empty name; he said: if you stay, and Joseph and the other two go west, who leads morning devotion on the road, who counts the people, at the offering once in seven who holds the bottom of the bowl? He said: you're the leader; the monastery is gone; the leader is still here; where the leader is, there morning devotion is. Jingfu stood beside the cart, stood till the fire was nearly out, and said: then we won't stay.

By daybreak, twenty-odd souls were staying. The few households of the Buzheng Ward shrine, two households of the Magi, those of the Liquan Ward shrine, Old Zhu's family—of the Zoroastrian families, not one went further west; and the one remaining old brother of the Daqin Monastery, called Simeon, Tang name Daoming, eighty-one, who after two months jolting on a donkey's back had legs too swollen to get off the donkey; he told Jingfu he couldn't walk any further; he'd stay at Jingzhou; Old Zhu said his family had a spare bed, and they'd look after him. Old Zhu came to find Salang at daybreak. His family had debated all night; his son wanted to stay, his old wife wanted to stay, his grandson, ten or so, didn't understand and cried along. Old Zhu said he'd stay too; he was sixty-seven; he couldn't walk west; his family's ancestors came from Kang and walked a lifetime to get to Chang'an; walking back from Chang'an he'd never get to Kang; wherever he got to, he'd die there; to die at Jingzhou was the same as dying halfway.

He took out the clay jar from his arms, held it in both hands, and held it out to Salang. Salang's hands hung down; he looked at the jar, its mouth's oilcloth tied tight, and on the jar's body a ring of bright marks Old Zhu's hands had worn holding it all the way. Salang said: Old Zhu, this is your family's fire. Old Zhu said: it's ash. Old Zhu said he was staying at Jingzhou; Jingzhou had no Zoroastrian shrine, and would never have one; he'd hold this jar of ash till he died, and when he died his son wouldn't know what it was, and his grandson still less; a jar of ash, and someone would use it to bed down a pigsty. He said: you're going west, Salang; your grandfather's father came from the Patriarchate; the Patriarchate is by the two rivers; beyond the two rivers, east of them, is Persia, and Persia still has fire, and people keeping it. He said he asked nothing else of you: when you reach a place with fire, give this jar of ash to the one who keeps the fire, and tell him: this is the fire of Liquan Ward in Chang'an; it burned over a hundred years; it went out on the twenty-third of the eighth month of the fifth year of Huichang; its keeper was surnamed Zhu, a man of Kang. If you can't reach a place with fire, and you die, let it be buried with you; buried in the earth, the ash goes back to the earth, and that's good too.

Salang said he didn't believe in your fire. Old Zhu said he knew; he didn't believe in your Lord either; but that year your monastery's people threw water all over his Pa's grandfather, and the next day the Great Worthy Yi came and bowed before his Pa's grandmother and brought bread, and there was honey in the bread, and his Pa ate it. He said: giving you this jar of ash today, there's honey in it too. Salang put out both hands and took the clay jar. The jar was heavy, heavier than he had thought; he held it, held it against his chest.

The caravan left Jingzhou on the twentieth of the twelfth month. When they went out of the town it was still dark; at the town gate stood a few people—those Zoroastrian families who were staying, Old Zhu at their head, his old wife holding him up, his grandson beside him rubbing his eyes. They did not wave; they only stood and watched the camels come out of the gateway one by one, go down to the bank of the Jing, and onto the road going northwest.

Salang walked beside the first camel, holding the halter. The clay jar he had wrapped in a felt and bound to his chest, bound tight; at every step the jar swung against his chest. When they had gone a long way he looked back; those few at the town gate were still there; too far to make out faces; he could see only a few black dots. He hitched the jar on his chest up a little, turned, and walked on.
