Volume Ten　Huichang

Chapter 57　As Far as the Edict Reaches

After the Manichaean temple was burned, Chang'an was quiet for more than a year.

Quiet did not mean nothing happened. It meant things came one by one, not in a hurry, the way a sky clouds over, overcast one day and the next and the next, and the rain never falls.

In the fourth year of Huichang, the Son of Heaven built a high terrace in the palace, called the Terrace for Watching the Immortals, on which, it was said, he would wait for the immortals to come. In the palace he kept a Daoist priest named Zhao, to whom the Son of Heaven listened in everything. That year, all the small temples, hermitages and mountain cells in the empire that had no name-board granted by the court were pulled down. Of the timber taken from them, some was carted off to repair government offices, some split for firewood.

At the start of spring in the fifth year of Huichang, the Bureau of Sacrifices sent down a notice ordering every prefecture and county in the empire to count its temples and its monks and nuns.

In the fourth month, monks and nuns under forty were ordered to return to lay life. In the fifth month, those under fifty as well.

The West Market filled with shaven heads. On the shaven heads a green stubble had just grown, and on top of it were caps that did not fit; they walked the streets with their heads down, walking very fast. Some went back to their home villages; some had no village to go back to, and squatted at the entrance of the West Market, waiting to be hired.

When Salang carried goods, there were several such men beside him now. They were not strong; after one bale they were out of breath, and when they could not manage, Salang carried it for them a stretch. They did not thank him either. When they had their breath back, they took it from him and went on.

Everyone in the monastery knew: after the Buddhists, it would be them.

No one said it aloud.

Lü Shao heard in the seventh month.

That day he was copying in the Imperial Library, an old history; by afternoon his eyes ached, and he put down his brush and went out to stand in the gallery a while. Two colleagues were standing there talking in low voices. Seeing him come, they stopped a moment, then went on.

One said that over at the Secretariat the edict had already been drafted. Of the empire's temples, a superior prefecture would keep one, a lesser prefecture none. The two capitals would keep four each, each with thirty monks. All the rest were to be pulled down without exception, the monks and nuns returned to lay life, the lands taken by the government, the bronze images and bronze bells taken to cast coin, the iron taken to cast farm tools.

The other said that he had heard there was one more clause.

"What?"

"Daqin, the Muhu, and the Zoroastrians—they're in it too."

The two of them looked at Lü Shao together.

Lü Shao said nothing. He stood a while, went back to his desk, sat down, and took up his brush. The ink on the brush had dried; he dipped it and went on copying. Half a line on, he miscopied a character. He looked at that wrong character a long time.

He had copied books in the Imperial Library for twenty years and had never miscopied a single character.

When his duty ended he did not go home, but walked all the way to the Yining Ward.

When he reached the monastery gate it was still light, and the lamp at the gate had not yet been lit. He looked up through the gate at the stele in the middle of the front courtyard. In the setting sun, a film of red lay over the blue-black stone.

There was a line on the stele he could have written with his eyes shut. The Law flowed through the ten circuits, and the state was rich in peace; monasteries filled a hundred cities, and every house abounded in Luminous blessing. His grandfather had written it.

Every time he read the last two words, he thought: that is probably where the Master's name came from.

He had never asked.

In the eighth month, the edict came down.

A few days later, the county people came.

The man who came was a sheriff of Chang'an County, not old, a little over thirty, in a green robe, a film of grey over his face, two bluish patches beneath his eyes. With him was a scribe of fifty or so, a little stooped, a stack of registers under his arm, a brush and an inkbox clenched in his hand. Behind them came the warden of the Yining Ward, and four constables.

What the warden's surname was, everyone in the monastery knew, and it need not be told here. He had been warden of the Yining Ward for more than ten years, and every spring he came to the monastery to beg a bowl of flatbread soup, and when he had drunk it he wiped his mouth and said: "Your house's soup is still the proper stuff."

This time he did not beg soup. He stood behind the sheriff and looked at the ground.

The sheriff stood in the middle of the front courtyard, drew a notice from his sleeve, unfolded it, and read.

He read very fast, as if he had read it many times these past days and knew it by heart. The people of the monastery stood in a row and listened. Some words they heard clearly, some not; the ones they heard clearly, pieced together, made these few things:

The Daqin monasteries, the Muhu, and the Zoroastrian shrines are to be reformed together with the Buddhist temples, as one body. Their clergy are ordered to return to lay life. Those whose registered home is in Tang lands are each to return there and be enrolled as households under the Two-Tax law. Foreigners are to be sent back to their places of origin and taken into custody there. Buildings, lands, money and goods are confiscated to the government. Other articles are to be disposed of as appropriate.

When he had finished, the sheriff rolled up the notice and tucked it back into his sleeve.

"Start counting," he said to the scribe.

The scribe set up a small table in the gallery, sat down, opened a blank register, and licked the tip of his brush.

"People first," he said. "How many clergy?"

"Nine," said Jingfu.

"One at a time."

The first was An Yan.

"Name?"

"An Yan."

"Age?"

"Seventy-four."

The scribe looked up at him once, and lowered his head and wrote. "Registered home?"

An Yan paused. "Chang'an."

"Forebears?"

"Bukhara," said An Yan. "They came in my grandfather's generation. I was born in Chang'an."

The scribe nodded and wrote a few characters in the register. "Registered Chang'an, returned to lay life, to the Yining Ward, enrolled as a household." He read it out once. "Any family?"

"None."

The scribe's brush stopped a moment, and wrote on.

The second and third were two old monks from Persia, one seventy, one sixty-eight, whose Tang speech stumbled; they gave their names three times before the scribe got them right. When the scribe asked their registered home, they said a place-name the scribe had never heard of; he had them say it again, and they said it again, and still the scribe had never heard of it.

The sheriff beside him grew impatient and said: "Write Persia."

The scribe wrote Persia. "Foreigners, to be sent back to their places of origin."

The fourth to the eighth were the monastery's middle-aged and young monks—some Sogdian, some of mixed Tang and hu birth, one a Tang man whose family was in Lantian. The scribe asked them one by one, and wrote one by one. The Lantian man he wrote as returned to Lantian; of the Sogdians, some had been born in Chang'an and he wrote returned to Chang'an, some born in Liangzhou and he wrote returned to Liangzhou, and one, who had come from Samarkand with a caravan, he wrote as sent back to his place of origin.

The ninth was Jingfu.

"Name?"

"Jingfu."

"Lay name?"

"A-Fu," said Jingfu. "My father called me that. The men in the camp called me Flatbread."

The scribe's brush paused; he did not write Flatbread. He wrote A-Fu.

"Age?"

"Fifty."

"Registered home?"

Jingfu did not answer at once.

The scribe waited. The sheriff waited too. Everyone in the courtyard was looking at him.

"My father was a soldier of the Army of Divine Strategy," said Jingfu. "A Sogdian, surnamed An, baptized in this monastery. In the Zhenyuan years he went to fight in Huaixi, and died there. My mother was Tang, the daughter of a small household outside Chang'an; what her surname was, I don't know. I came into the monastery at five."

When he had heard it, the scribe thought, and laid down his brush.

"Mother Tang, born in Chang'an," he said. "If you wanted to return to Chang'an County and be enrolled as a household, that would pass."

The courtyard went still a moment. An Yan looked at Jingfu. Lü Shao looked at Jingfu too. The two old Persian monks did not quite understand; they looked at the scribe, and then at Jingfu.

The sheriff came over, stood in front of Jingfu, and looked him up and down. After a while, the sheriff said: "Looks hu."

The warden said in a low voice behind him: "Looks Tang too."

The sheriff turned his head and looked at the warden once. The warden said nothing more.

The sheriff turned back, looked at Jingfu, and said: "You say."

Jingfu glanced at the two old Persian monks. One seventy, one sixty-eight, their Tang speech halting; where their places of origin were, they could not say themselves.

"My father was hu," said Jingfu. "My master was hu. My master's father was hu."

The sheriff nodded and said to the scribe: "Write hu. Foreigner."

The scribe took up his brush and wrote it. When he had written it, he asked again: "Place of origin?"

Jingfu looked at him.

"Place of origin," said the scribe. "Where are we to send you back to?"

Jingfu thought a long time.

"The west," he said.

The scribe's brush hung over the register, hung a while. He looked up at the sheriff; the sheriff had already walked off, and was looking at a pillar of the gallery. The scribe lowered his head and wrote a few characters. What they were, Jingfu did not see.

When the nine had been counted, the scribe looked up and saw two grown men and a child still standing beside the gallery pillar.

"And those?"

Salang took a step forward. He was too tall; standing in the gallery, the top of his head nearly touched the rafters. The scribe tipped his face up to look at him.

"You're of the monastery?"

"I carry water," said Salang.

"Live in the monastery?"

"No. South of the West Market."

"Registered?"

"Yes," said Salang. "Chang'an County register. My mother was Tang."

The scribe leafed through another register, leafed a long time, found it, and nodded. "Lay," he said. "None of your concern."

Salang stood and did not move.

"None of your concern," said the scribe again.

Salang stepped back beside the gallery pillar.

The scribe looked next at Lü Shao. Lü Shao gave his name and his office; at the words "Imperial Library" the scribe did not even pick up his brush, and waved a hand.

Last was Xiaoman.

Xiaoman was hiding behind Lü Shao, tugging at the hem of his robe. The scribe looked at him once and asked: "Whose child?"

"The monastery found him," said Jingfu.

"Has he been ordained?"

"No."

"Then he's a lay child, not on the clergy register," said the scribe. "Ordered back to his own family. Where is his family?"

No one in the courtyard spoke.

Lü Shao spoke. "To my family," he said.

The scribe looked at him once, then at Xiaoman, wrote a line of small characters in the margin of the register, and when he had written it, turned the page.

When the people had been counted, the things were counted.

The scribe took two constables and went room by room, counting room by room. So many bays of hall, so many of chapel, so many monks' cells; kitchen, woodshed, stable, so many each. In the courtyard, one well, so many trees. Outside the city, so many dozen mu of monastery fields, beyond the Jinguang Gate, let out to a few households to farm; the deeds were in the cupboard, and were taken out, and the scribe looked at them and took them.

The scripture cupboard was opened. Inside were scriptures, one after another, all copied by Lü Shao, wrapped in blue cloth, each with a slip of paper pasted on it bearing the scripture's name. The scribe counted them and recorded a number, and asked again: "Any bronze images?"

"No images," said Jingfu.

The scribe gave him a puzzled look. "The monastery has no images on its altar?"

"A cross on its altar."

"Is the cross bronze?"

"Wood."

The scribe thought, and wrote: wooden cross, one.

He asked again: "Bronze bell?"

"We don't use a bell," said An Yan. "We use a wooden board."

The scribe wrote again: wooden board, one.

He asked again: "How many slaves and bondservants?"

Jingfu said: "None."

The scribe's brush stopped. He raised his head and looked from one person of the monastery to the next, all the way along.

"Not a single one?"

"Not a single one."

The scribe had been a scribe in Chang'an County for thirty years, and had counted no fewer than a hundred temples. He had never seen a temple with dozens of mu of fields and dozens of rooms and not one bondservant. He looked at Jingfu a while, lowered his head, and wrote one character in the register.

None.

Last of all, he went to the middle of the front courtyard, stood before the stele, and looked up. He looked a while, and asked: "What's this?"

"A stele," said Jingfu.

"What stele?"

"It was raised in the second year of Jianzhong," said Jingfu. "It tells how our teaching came."

The scribe walked once round the stele, looked at the curling script on the two sides, then at the Tang characters on the face. He could read; he read a few lines, and when he came to the words the Emperor Taizong, Cultured, and then to the words Duke Guo Ziyi, his eyebrows moved.

He went back to the small table and wrote: stele, one.

When the counting was done, it was already late.

The sheriff stood in the middle of the front courtyard and said a few things to Jingfu. He said them very simply; he had said them so often these days they came easily. The hall doors, the scripture cupboard, the monastery gate would be sealed today. The monastery's people would stay for now in the monks' cells in the back courtyard, and were not to leave the ward. Those registered in Tang lands had so many days to go back each to his registered home and enrol at the county. The foreigners were to wait for the county to send them on; when the time came someone would come to collect them, and they would all go together. The monastery's property and articles would wait till the county came again to take them in.

When he had finished, he looked at Jingfu, as if there were something more to say. He opened his mouth, and in the end said only: "Get yourselves ready."

Then he went.

The scribe gathered up his registers, very slowly. When everyone else had gone out of the courtyard gate he was still in the gallery. He put the registers under his arm, stood up, went over to Jingfu, and lowered his voice.

"That place of origin of yours," he said. "I wrote Xizhou."

Jingfu looked at him.

"In the west, Xizhou is the only place I know," said the scribe. "When I was young I had an uncle who did garrison duty out there, and died there."

When he had said it, without waiting for Jingfu to say anything, he went, the registers under his arm.

The seals were pasted on by the warden.

First he sealed the hall doors: two strips, pasted crosswise into an X. Then he sealed the scripture cupboard. Last he sealed the monastery gate.

The monastery gate was sealed from outside. The people of the monastery stood inside it and watched the gate close a little at a time. Before it closed, the warden looked at Jingfu once through the crack.

"The lamp," said the warden. "Best not light it."

Jingfu did not answer.

The gate closed. From outside came the sound of paste being brushed on, then the sound of a palm slapping paper. Pa, pa, pa.

The people of the monastery stood behind the gate a while, and went their separate ways.

Only An Yan did not go. He went to the middle of the front courtyard and stood before the stele. It was nearly dark, the characters on the stele could no longer be seen, so he reached out his hand and felt for them. He felt his way to the side of the stele, where the Syriac letters were carved, line after line of names, running from top to bottom. He felt his way to one of the lines, and stopped.

That line he knew. When he was small, Jingjing had taken his hand and run it over this line once, and said: this is my father.

"Master," said An Yan, "this stele—what will they do with it?"

Jingfu came over and stood beside him.

"I don't know."

"The few temples south of the city that were pulled down," said An Yan, "their steles were all carted away. Some were smashed and burned for lime, some taken to shore up the banks of the canals. I went and looked. They're laid writing-side down, and carts drive over them."

Jingfu said nothing.

An Yan's hand was still pressed on that line, pressed a long time.

"It's ten years younger than I am," he said.

That night, Xiaoman would not sleep. He sat on the steps of the back courtyard with his arms round his knees and asked Jingfu whether they had to leave.

Jingfu said: some will go, and some won't.

Xiaoman asked: will I go?

Jingfu said: you won't.

Xiaoman asked: will Master go?

Jingfu did not answer. He sat on the steps, sat together with Xiaoman. After a while, Xiaoman leaned against his arm and fell asleep.

Jingfu carried him back into the room, laid him on his pallet, and tucked the quilt round him.

Then he went out by the back door.

The back door had not been sealed. The sheriff had said the monastery's people had to carry water, so the back door was left.

Jingfu came out of the back door and along the narrow lane outside the monastery wall, round half the circuit, round to the front of the monastery gate.

There was no one left in the street. The ward gate had long since shut; the ward was so quiet you could hear a dog barking far off. The moon had not yet come up; the sky was black, and the monastery gate was black. The seal on the gate, a patch of white, showed as a shape in the dark.

On the iron hook by the gate, the lamp still hung. The saucer was empty; the oil of the night before had burned dry, and the wick had burned down to a short black stub.

Jingfu took the lamp down. From his coat he took a small oil jar and poured oil into the saucer, then took out a new wick and changed it. He struck his flint; he struck three times before it caught.

The flame rose.

He hung the lamp back on the iron hook. The lamplight shone on the monastery gate, on the seal. The characters on the seal were written by the county, written very neatly; the ink was not yet quite dry, and shone under the lamplight.

He stood before the gate a while.

At the mouth of the lane a lantern lit up, and came swaying nearer. It was the warden on his night round.

The warden came to the front of the monastery gate and stopped. He looked at the lamp, looked at the seal, then looked at Jingfu standing before the gate.

Neither of them spoke.

The warden looked a while, took up his lantern, and walked on.
