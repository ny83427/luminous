Volume Nine　The Translation Hall

Chapter 57　Baaluo

In the winter of the first year of Yuanhe, the people of the south of the city told a thing. Few in the monastery knew it, and those who knew were old and never spoke of it. Baaluo heard it from those two households of old people from the old Persian monastery of the Liquan Ward; the two households were Tocharians, in Chang'an since the Tianbao years, from the same village as the old Tocharian who had once recognized Yonan as "Bishop." Baaluo told it in the straw shed in the south of the city before thirty-odd households, and when he had told it, the people of the south of the city came and stood at the monastery gate again and told it on the Lord's Day. They said the papash of Chang'an, Jingjing, was the son of Yonan, bishop of Chang'an in the Tianbao years; Yonan had vowed, vowed not to marry, broke the vow, and had this child by a woman of the back lane behind an eating-house in the West Market; the Patriarchate recalled Yonan, and the child was thrown to Yisi to raise; they said the son of a vow-breaking bishop had become papash of Chang'an; the stele was written by him, the scriptures translated by him, the Son of Heaven's names carved by him, the monastery's rules changed by him; this monastery was unclean from its roots.

That day everyone in the hall heard it. Worship was half sung, the voices outside the gate drowned the voices in the hall, and some in the hall stopped and looked round; a few young monks kept their heads down and dared not look toward the front. Sabrisho was eighty that year; he stood at the front, his face iron-green, and turned to go out; Jingjing caught his sleeve. When the worship was sung and the offering bread had gone round once, Jingjing did not let them disperse; he came down from the front and stood in the middle of the hall and told the whole hall: what the people outside the gate say is true. He said he was the son of Yonan and A-Lian, born in the fifth year of Tianbao; Priest Yisi and his wife carried him home and raised him; he went to the Patriarchate at ten, and at fifteen, and only at twenty learned that while his Ma lived she had knelt with him in this very hall; his father Elder Yonan died by the well and was buried outside the Kaiyuan Gate, and his Ma was buried beside him, a foot apart. He said Elder Yonan broke his vow; the Patriarch Hnanisho released him from his vow, removed his standing as former bishop, and did not cut him off; the letter came from Baghdad, and Elder Samaan's accounts record it. He said these things he had hidden from no one, and told no one; no one asked, so he did not say; today someone had asked, so he said. He said that he was papash because the Patriarch's letter wrote it, and the laying on of hands had not yet been done; he knew himself he was the son of a vow-breaking bishop, the son of a woman of the back lane behind an eating-house in the West Market; he did this work unworthily, he knew; if for this you are unwilling to receive the bread he breaks, he will not blame you; the bread can be broken by Lingbao, broken by Elder Sabrisho.

Not one person in the hall left. After a few breaths an old woman in the last row stood up: a sesame-cake seller of the south lane of the West Market; when A-Lian kept her shop in the south lane, she was next door; she said that shop of A-Lian's—she'd been there how many times; A-Lian let her have flour on credit and never pressed her; the day A-Lian died, all those who owed her came to pay, and she came too; she said: your Ma was a good woman. When she had said it she sat down again. Sabrisho at the front, his beard still trembling, trembled and trembled, and stopped, and said he had kept the rule all his life, and the rule has no article that says the son of a vow-breaker may not be papash; to his death he would not acknowledge such a thing. When he had said it, he went over, took the basket of offering bread from Jingjing's hand, and pressed it back into Jingjing's hand, and said: you break it.

That day's accounts Samaan wrote several lines of; his hands shook badly, the characters were written very large, and one page held only a few lines.

That winter there were thirty-odd households in the garden in the south of the city. Baaluo was past sixty; his body had long been hollowed out by miasma and the cangue, but his voice was still the same; when he opened his mouth the whole garden could hear. He laid down rules: those entering the garden must first pour cold water over themselves at the well for three days, and only then might enter the straw shed; in the straw shed no Tang might be spoken, no Son of Heaven's name enshrined; no sitting at one table with the monastery's people; no going in at an official's gate; when men came from the yamen, the gate not to be opened; he said that in Lingnan he had seen the Dharma King Baolu in iron chains, and the Dharma King Baolu said the monastery in Chang'an was unclean, must be made pure, made pure from the beginning. He said too that the monastery's stele was an idol, a stone enshrining the Son of Heaven, and must be smashed; smash it, and the Lord would come back.

When this reached the monastery, the young people of the monastery were all alarmed. Sabrisho went to Jingjing and said this time he couldn't be let be; he means to smash the stele; smashing the stele is smashing the Son of Heaven's thing; if the government knows, they'll say it was the monastery's people who smashed it, and the monastery won't be kept; he said he would go to the Commissioner of Merit's office: that garden in the south of the city gathers crowds with seditious talk; let the government go and disperse it; once the government disperses it, the monastery is quiet, and the stele is kept. Jingjing said no. Sabrisho said why not. Jingjing said: that year when Baaluo poured water on the altar of the fire-temple, the old fire-keeper was beaten to death, the Metropolitan Prefecture sentenced him to exile in Lingnan, and it was Pa who went to the Chunming Gate to see him off; Pa slipped money to the escorts, and Baaluo cursed Pa for buying smiles from the escorts with money; he said: now he's come back and says the monastery clings to the government; if the monastery goes to the government to disperse him, it'll be finishing that sentence for him. He said if Baaluo wanted to smash the stele, let him come and smash it; the stele was in the front courtyard; he'd keep watch on it; he couldn't smash it; if he wanted to curse, let him curse.

Sabrisho looked at him and said: if your Pa were here, would he go to the government? Jingjing thought and said: Pa wouldn't; Pa would go to the south of the city and stand at his door.

Before he had gone, the people of the south of the city came first. On the night of the twelfth of the twelfth month, past the third watch, the guard brothers on night patrol heard a noise outside the west wall of the front courtyard; Sargis's son, past sixty himself that year, took his staff and went to look; four men had already come over the top of the wall: three young men and one lame, carrying two iron hammers and a crowbar. The guard brothers struck the board, and the monastery's people all got up; by the time Jingjing came out with his robe thrown over him, the four were already standing before the stele, and the nine guard brothers had made a half-circle, staff-ends outward, hemming them in between the stele and the west wall. Sargis's son said: Master, take them; take them and hand them to the ward headman.

Jingjing did not answer him; he went in through the gap in the half-circle, up to the stele, turned round, his back to the stele, and stood between the four men and the stele. He recognized the lame one: one of the two soldiers who had come back from Huaixi that year, who heard the scripture a year sitting beside Lord Mi, and after Lord Mi died came seldom, and later was heard to have gone to the south of the city. Jingjing said to him: you've come. The man had not thought he would be recognized; the hammer in his hand dipped, and rose again, and he said: Elder Baaluo says this stone is an idol; smash it, and the Lord will come back. Jingjing said: if you want to smash it, smash it; he won't stop you, nor will the guard brothers; but he's standing here and won't leave; if you want to smash the stele, smash past him first. And he told the guard brothers: put down your staves. Sargis's son would not; Jingjing looked back at him once, and he put his staff down, and the nine staves were laid one by one on the ground.

The young men looked at one another, and not one moved. The lame soldier stood at the front with the hammer raised, his hand shaking; shaking, he suddenly gritted his teeth and swung it down, and what he struck was not Jingjing, nor the body of the stele, but the southeast corner of its base; one blow, sparks flew, and a piece broke off the base, the size of a fist, and rolled into the snow. Jingjing did not move; chips of stone spattered his robe, and he did not look down. When the soldier had struck that blow, the hammer fell from his hand; he looked at the gap as if he did not know what he had done, turned and ran for the wall; lame, he could not get over; the young men pushed him up, and the four of them went over the wall and fled.

The guard brothers wanted to give chase; Jingjing said: no chasing. Sabrisho came out helped by someone, stood before the stele looking at that gap, his beard trembling in the snow, and said: this time, surely, the Master will go to the government. Jingjing said: no. He bent down, picked that broken piece of stone out of the snow, and gripped it in his hand; the stone was cold, its edges biting into his hand. He said: tomorrow have the stonemason come and look; the body of the stele isn't hurt; the base has lost a corner; don't mend it; let it stay missing; this stone, put it in the scripture cupboard with Elder Samaan's account books. That night Samaan wrote a line in the accounts, his hands shaking badly, the characters very large: Night of the twelfth of the twelfth month: four from the south of the city came over the wall and struck the stele with a hammer, damaging the southeast corner of the base; the Master stood before the stele; no pursuit; not reported to the government.

Those few mornings, the character on Jingjing's board was bai, tares. What he read was the parable the Lord told: a man sowed good seed in his field, and his enemy came by night and sowed tares among the wheat; the servants wanted to go and gather them up; the householder said: nay, lest while ye gather up the tares ye root up also the wheat with them; let both grow together until the harvest. He read it several days, kept reading this passage, and on the fifth day got up and went to the south of the city.

That day, before light, he took Lingbao out of the Yining Ward and walked south, to that garden west of the Qujiang Pool, as the edge of the sky was just turning grey. By the well in the garden stood a row of people, men and women, old and young, each stripped to the waist, pouring cold water over their heads, one dipper and one line recited, and when they had poured, standing in the snow shivering, and when they had shivered, pouring another dipper. Baaluo stood on the well platform ladling dipper after dipper, at each dipper singing a line. An Yan was in that row too; he had grown thin, his face blue with cold; seeing Jingjing come into the garden, the dipper in his hand stopped.

Jingjing went to the well and stood in the snow and came no further. Baaluo saw him, put down the dipper, and said: the papash has come; has the papash come to lead them back? Jingjing said he had come to say a few things, and when he had said them he would go; what he said, if you're willing to hear, hear; if not, go on pouring. Baaluo looked at him and said: then say it.

So Jingjing stood in the snow and said it. He said: on the stele the Son of Heaven's names are many and the Lord's few; he wrote it; when he wrote it, what he thought was that the stele is written for those who don't come in at the gate—written for the government to see, for the censors to see, for those who will come later to pull the monastery down; the stele doesn't say how the Lord died, but on its head a cross is carved, carved above all the characters; that is the place he left for the Lord. He said: in the Tang scriptures, characters borrowed from the Buddhist monks and Daoists—he borrowed them; Elder Alopen borrowed them too in his day; borrowing characters is living in another's house; living there, you must remember in your heart it's not your own house; every character he borrowed, he put a dot beside it; for thirty years he's put dots, thousands of dots; they're all in the monastery's scripture room; whoever wants to see them, he'll show them. He said: the monastery's dealing with the government, making registers, asking people to put in a word, treating the wounds of the Shence Army's soldiers—he decided it; when he decided it what he thought of was that pot of gruel in the west of the city, the few hundred souls who didn't starve through a winter; doing these things, his face did not look well, and his heart was not easy either; but he did them, and he owns them. He said: that the Dharma King Baolu went into the city of Rome in iron chains is true; that the Dharma King Baolu, at Athens, stood before their temple and took a line from their temple to preach the Lord, is true too.

When he had said these things he said further: you pour cold water here; you keep yours; he won't stop you, nor go to the government; if you think it's pure here, stay here; if one day you feel cold and want to go back, the monastery's gate is open. He said he was afraid of cold water, had been since he was small; one mouthful and his stomach was upset all night; you pour it; he won't; it's not that he keeps the rule worse than you; it's that his stomach is worse than yours.

Someone in the snow laughed once, and quickly held it in. Baaluo did not laugh. He stood on the well platform and said: have you finished? Jingjing said he had. Baaluo said: then go. Jingjing turned and walked out; at the garden gate he heard footsteps behind him, turned, and it was An Yan, stripped to the waist, ice crusted in his hair, running up, standing before him, saying: Master, he'll come back with you. Jingjing took off his own outer robe and put it round him, and the three of them walked north.

That winter seven households came back from the south of the city, twenty-odd souls. There were also those who did not come back, twenty-odd households, who stayed on in the garden. Samaan entered a line in the accounts: Returned from the south of the city, seven households; the rest not returned; the Master went to speak; did not compel them. When he had written this line his hands shook so he could not hold the brush; Lingbao took the brush from him and at his dictation added another line: The Master says: tares and wheat—let them grow together.

When spring opened, Baaluo fell ill. He had poured cold water all winter, poured it into the second month, and broke into fever, coughed blood, lay in the straw shed unable to rise; the people of the garden sang scripture round him, sang seven days, and he burned hotter and hotter. An Yan heard of it and came to tell Jingjing; Jingjing had Lingbao pack the medicine bag—smoked plum, coptis, a packet of ginger—and had Siyuan push that cart along, and went to the south of the city. At the garden gate the people of the garden stopped them and would not let them in, saying the elder sees no one from the monastery. So Jingjing stood at the garden gate, stood half an hour, and someone came out from inside and said the elder bids him come in, only him alone.

The straw shed was very dark, very cold; Baaluo lay on a reed mat covered with two ragged jackets, his face burned purple, his lips cracked, his eyes open. He saw Jingjing come in and said: you've come to watch me die. Jingjing squatted beside him and reached out to feel his forehead; the forehead burned the hand; he said he had brought medicine, smoked plum, coptis, ginger; boil a bowl, drink it, and the fever can come down somewhat. Baaluo said he wouldn't drink the monastery's medicine; the monastery's medicine was bought with the government's money. Jingjing said the medicine was let to the monastery on credit by Shopkeeper Wang's son of the West Market; on credit thirty years; not a coin of the government's money in it. Baaluo looked at him and said nothing more. Jingjing had those outside heat water, squatted by the fire himself boiling the medicine, and when it was boiled let it cool to warm, brought it over; Baaluo would not open his mouth, so Jingjing held it, held it until the medicine was nearly cold, and Baaluo opened his mouth and drank two mouthfuls, and when he had drunk them turned his head aside.

That night Jingjing did not leave. He sat at the door of the straw shed listening to the coughing inside, fit after fit, and in the small hours the coughing grew lower. Baaluo called him; he went in; Baaluo said: that year at the Chunming Gate, in the cangue, Master Yi slipped money to the escorts, and he cursed Master Yi, and when he had cursed him walked toward Lantian, walked and sang, sang till at Lantian his voice was hoarse, and looked back, and Master Yi was still standing under that willow outside the Chunming Gate, and hadn't gone. He said that in thirty years in Lingnan, every time he had fever, burning to delirium, he saw that willow. He said to this day he still thought your monastery unclean; that stele is an idol; the characters you borrow are Buddhist monks' and Daoists'; the men you ask favours of are the eunuchs' men; to his death he would not acknowledge it. When he had said it he gasped for breath, and when his breath was even, said he wanted to hear that one, the one he sang outside the Chunming Gate; he couldn't sing it any more; you sing it.

So Jingjing sang in the straw shed. My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me? why art thou so far from helping me, and from the words of my roaring? His voice was poor, and he sang it out of tune; when he came to "I am poured out like water, and all my bones are out of joint," the people of the garden outside the straw shed began to sing too, twenty-odd households standing in the snow, following line by line. When he came to the end—"For he hath not despised nor abhorred the affliction of the afflicted; neither hath he hid his face from him"—Jingjing stopped; Baaluo's eyes were still open, looking at that hole in the straw shed's roof where the wind came in, and beyond the hole the sky was whitening.

Baaluo was buried outside the Kaiyuan Gate, beside Narsai. The people of the south of the city wanted to bury him in the garden; Jingjing told them: those rows of stakes outside the Kaiyuan Gate were set up one by one by Elder Narsai; the day Baaluo came back to Chang'an, it was Elder Narsai who recognized him in the grass outside the Chunming Gate; buried beside Elder Narsai—those two, they knew each other. The people of the south of the city talked it over a night and agreed. On the day of the burial the people of the south of the city came, and the monastery's people came, the two parties standing on either side of the burial ground with those rows of stakes between them. Jingjing recited the scripture, in Syriac, and did not recite it in Tang. When it was recited he had Siyuan bring a new stake, and wrote on it himself in charcoal, wrote the three characters Ba-a-luo, and a line of small characters too: son of a tanner of the West Market of Chang'an, his name from the Dharma King Baolu. When he had written it, a young man from the south of the city's side came over, looked at the stake, looked at Jingjing, said nothing at all, and went back.

That summer Samaan died. He was past seventy; he had kept the accounts forty years, kept them till at the last his hands shook so he could not hold the brush, and had Lingbao write for him: he said a line, and Lingbao wrote a line. The day before he died he had Lingbao bring the account book, turn to the last page, and had Lingbao write; what he wrote was: Samaan, Warden of the Rule, hands over the accounts to the deacon Lingbao. When it was written he looked at the line, looked for several breaths, and had Lingbao add one more; Lingbao asked what to add; he said: add: in the accounts there are several places where the Master did not follow the rule, and Samaan held it improper; all recorded; let those who come after look, and having looked, think for themselves. Lingbao wrote it so. Samaan called for Jingjing too; Jingjing came; he drew his two hands out of his sleeves and took Jingjing's hands and said: Master, that year you said tares and wheat, let them grow together; he recorded it, but to this day he hasn't worked out which is tares and which is wheat. Jingjing said he hadn't worked it out either; the Lord says wait for the harvest; at the harvest the Lord will divide them himself. Samaan nodded, drew his hands back into his sleeves and folded them, and the next morning died just like that, his hands folded.

Samaan was buried outside the Kaiyuan Gate, beside Azarel. That leather-bound account book passed into Lingbao's hands: forty years, nine books filled, the last still half empty. Lingbao locked it in the scripture cupboard and hung the key at his own waist. That evening Jingjing went to the gate to light the lamp, lit it, stood a moment beneath it, went back to the scripture room, wiped the character tares off the board, meant to write something, sat till the wood was struck, and in the end what he wrote was still that same character.
