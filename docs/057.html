Volume Nine　The Translation Hall

Chapter 57　Baaluo

In the winter of the first year of Yuanhe, the people in the south of the city told a thing. Few at the monastery knew it; those who knew were old, and never spoke of it. Baaluo had heard it from the two old Tokharian households at the old Persian monastery in the Liquan Ward. He told it in the straw shed in the south of the city before thirty-odd households, and when he had told it, the people of the south of the city came and stood again at the monastery gate and told it at the offering once in seven:

"Jingjing, Father of the Monks of Chang'an, is the son of Yonan, bishop of Chang'an in the Tianbao years! Yonan took the vow—took the vow not to marry, broke the vow, and had this child with a woman from the lane behind the eating house in the West Market. The Patriarchate called Yonan back, and the child was thrown to Yisi to raise. The son of a bishop who broke his vow has become Father of the Monks of Chang'an! The stele, he wrote it; the scriptures, he translated them; the Son of Heaven's names, he carved them on; the monastery's rules, he changed them. This monastery is unclean from the root!"

The voices outside the gate drowned the voices in the hall. Some in the hall stopped and turned to look; a few young monks bowed their heads and dared not look at the front. Sabrisho was eighty that year. He stood at the front, his face livid, and turned to go out, and Jingjing caught his sleeve.

When the worship had been sung through and the offering bread had gone round once, Jingjing did not dismiss them. He came down from the front and stood in the middle of the hall.

"What the people outside the gate say is true," he said. "I am the son of Yonan and A-Lian, born in the fifth year of Tianbao. Priest Yisi and his first wife took me in and raised me. At ten I went to the Patriarchate, at fifteen went again, and only at twenty did I learn this. While my Ma lived, she knelt in this hall together with me. My father, Elder Yonan, died by the well and is buried outside the Kaiyuan Gate, and my Ma is buried beside him, a foot apart. Elder Yonan broke his vow, and the patriarch Hnanisho allowed him to be released from it, took from him his standing as former bishop, and did not strike him from the register. The letter came from Baghdad; it's recorded in Elder Samaan's ledger."

As he said this, the whole hall was looking at him.

"These things I never hid from anyone, nor told anyone either. No one asked, so I didn't say. Today someone has asked, so I've said. I am Father of the Monks because the patriarch's letter says so; the laying on of hands has not been done. I am the son of a bishop who broke his vow, the son of a woman from the lane behind an eating house in the West Market, and I am not worthy of this; I know it myself. If because of this you're unwilling to take the bread I break, I won't blame you. From now on the bread can be broken by Lingbao, or by Elder Sabrisho."

Not one person in the hall left. After a few breaths an old woman in the last row stood up. She sold sesame cakes in the South Lane of the West Market.

"Your Reverence, in the years your Ma kept her shop in the South Lane, I was next door to her," she said. "That shop of hers—I don't know how many times I went there. She let me have flour on credit and never pressed me. On the day she died, everyone who owed her came to pay, and I came too. Your Ma was a good woman." When she had said it she sat down again.

Sabrisho stood at the front, his beard still trembling; it trembled, and stopped.

"I've kept the rules all my life," he said. "There's not one rule that says the son of a man who broke his vow can't be Father of the Monks. There's no such rule, and to my death I won't accept that there is." He went over, took the basket of offering bread from Jingjing's hands, and pushed it back into Jingjing's hands. "You break it."

Samaan wrote several lines in the ledger that day. His hand shook badly, the characters were very large, and a page held only a few lines.

That winter there were thirty-odd households in the garden in the south of the city. Baaluo was past sixty. His body had long since been hollowed out by the miasma and the cangue, but his voice was still the same; when he opened his mouth the whole garden could hear. He set rules: those who entered the garden must first pour cold water over themselves at the well for three days, and only then might they enter the shed; in the shed no Tang was to be spoken, no Son of Heaven's name was to be venerated, no one was to eat at the same table as the monastery's people, no one was to go in at an official's gate. He said too that the stele in the monastery was an idol, a stone on which the Son of Heaven was venerated, and it must be smashed; when it was smashed, the Lord would come back.

When this reached the monastery, the young ones were all alarmed. Sabrisho went to Jingjing. "This time we can't leave him be. If he smashes the stele, that's smashing the Son of Heaven's thing, and when the government hears of it they'll say the monastery's people smashed it, and the monastery won't be saved. I'll go to the Commissioners of Merit's office: the garden in the south of the city gathers crowds and spreads uncanny talk; have the government break it up. Once the government's broken it up, the monastery will be at peace, and the stele will be safe."

"No," said Jingjing.

"Why not?"

"The year Baaluo threw water on the altar of the fire-temple and beat the old fire-keeper to death, the Metropolitan Prefecture sentenced him to exile in Lingnan, and it was Pa who went to see him off at the Chunming Gate. Pa pushed money into the escorts' hands, and Baaluo cursed Pa for taking money to buy the escorts' smiles. Now he's come back, saying the monastery leans on the government. If the monastery goes to the government to break him up, we'll be finishing that sentence for him. If he wants to smash the stele, let him come and smash it; the stele's in the front courtyard, and I'll keep watch over it. If he wants to curse, let him curse."

Sabrisho looked at him, and after a while asked, "If your Pa were here, would he go to the government?"

Jingjing thought. "Pa wouldn't. Pa would go to the south of the city and stand at his door."

Before he could go, the people of the south of the city came first.

On the night of the twelfth of the twelfth month, after the third watch, the guard brothers on their night round heard a noise outside the west wall of the front courtyard. Sargis's son, himself past sixty that year, went over with his staff to look. Four men had already climbed in over the wall top: three young fellows and one with a lame leg, carrying two iron hammers and a crowbar. The guard brother struck the wooden board, and the whole monastery was up. By the time Jingjing came out with his robe thrown over him, the four were already standing before the stele, and the nine guard brothers had formed a half-circle round them, staves pointing outward, penning them in between the stele and the west wall.

"Master, let's take them," said Sargis's son. "Take them and hand them to the ward headman."

Jingjing did not answer him. He walked in through the gap in the half-circle, up to the stele, turned round, and stood with his back against the stele, between the four men and the stele. He recognized the lame one. He was one of the two soldiers back from Huaixi that year, who had heard the scriptures for a year, sitting beside Lord Mi, and after Lord Mi died hadn't come much, and later was said to have gone to the south of the city.

"You've come," said Jingjing.

The man had not expected to be recognized. The hammer in his hand dropped a little, and then rose again. "Elder Baaluo says this stone is an idol, and when it's smashed the Lord will come back!"

"If you want to smash it, smash it. I won't stop you, and the guard brothers won't stop you," said Jingjing. "But I'm standing here and I'm not moving. If you want to smash the stele, you'll smash past me first." He turned his head and said to the guard brothers, "Put the staves down."

Sargis's son would not. Jingjing looked at him again, and only then did he put his staff down, and the nine staves were laid on the snow one by one.

The young fellows looked at one another, and not one moved. The lame soldier stood at the very front, hammer raised, his hands trembling. They trembled and trembled, and suddenly he clenched his teeth and brought it down. What he struck was not Jingjing, and not the body of the stele, but the south-east corner of the stele's base. The hammer came down, sparks flew, and a piece the size of a fist broke from the base and rolled into the snow. Jingjing did not move. Chips of stone spattered his robe, and he did not look down. When the soldier had struck that one blow, the hammer fell from his hand. He looked at the broken place as if he did not know what he had done, turned, and ran for the wall. With his lame leg he couldn't get over, and the young fellows pushed him up, and the four of them went over the wall and ran.

The guard brothers wanted to give chase, and Jingjing said not to. Sabrisho had himself helped out and stood before the stele looking at the broken place, his beard trembling in the snow. "This time, surely, the Master will go to the government."

"No." Jingjing bent and picked the broken stone out of the snow and held it in his hand. The stone was cold, and its edges dug into his palm. "Tomorrow have a stonemason come and look. The body of the stele isn't hurt; the base has lost a corner. Don't mend it; let it stay broken. This stone, put it in the scripture cabinet, together with Elder Samaan's ledgers."

That night Samaan made a line in the ledger, in very large characters: Night of the twelfth of the twelfth month; four from the south of the city came over the wall, hammered the stele, damaged the south-east corner of the base; the Master stood before the stele; not pursued; not reported to the government.

Those mornings the character on Jingjing's board was bai, tares. He was reading the parable: a man sowed good seed in his field, and his enemy came by night and sowed tares among the wheat, and the servants wanted to go and gather them up, and the master said, nay, lest while ye gather up the tares, ye root up also the wheat with them; let both grow together until the harvest. On the fifth day of reading it he rose and went to the south of the city.

That day, before it was light, he took Lingbao out of the Yining Ward and walked south, and when he reached the garden west of the Qujiang Pool the edge of the sky was just turning grey. By the well in the garden stood a row of people, men and women, old and young, one after another stripped to the waist and pouring cold water over their heads—a ladleful, a line of prayer—and when they had poured it stood shivering in the snow, and when they had finished shivering, poured another ladleful. Baaluo stood on the well curb ladling it out, ladle by ladle, singing a line with each. An Yan was in the row too, thinner, his face blue with cold. When he saw Jingjing come into the garden, the ladle in his hand stopped.

Baaluo saw him and set down the ladle. "The Father of the Monks has come. Has the Father of the Monks come to lead them back?"

"I've come to say a few things." Jingjing stood in the snow and did not go forward. "When I've said them I'll go. If you're willing to listen, listen; if not, go on pouring."

"Then say them."

"On the stele there are many of the Son of Heaven's names and few of the Lord's; that's what I wrote. What I thought when I wrote it was that the stele is written for those who don't come in at the gate—written for the government to read, for the censors to read, for those who will come one day to tear the monastery down. The stele doesn't say how the Lord died; but at the head of the stele a cross is carved, carved above all the characters, and that's the place I left for the Lord. The Tang scriptures borrow the Buddhists' and Daoists' words; I borrowed them, and Elder Alopen borrowed them in his day too. Borrowing words is living in someone else's house; while you live there, you must remember in your heart that it isn't your own house. Every word I borrowed, I put a dot beside it. Thirty years of dots, thousands of dots; they're all in the scripture room, and whoever wants to see them, I'll show them."

Everyone by the well had stopped and stood listening.

"The monastery's dealings with the government—the registration, asking people to put in a word, treating the wounds of Shence Army soldiers—I decided those. What I thought when I decided them was that pot of gruel, and the few hundred mouths who didn't starve to death in a winter. These things don't look well on me, and I'm not easy about them in my heart; but I did them, and I own them. The Dharma King Baolu went into the city of Rome in iron chains; that's true. The Dharma King Baolu in Athens stood before other people's temple and took a line from their altar to preach the Lord; that's true too."

He drew a breath and went on: "You pour cold water here and keep your rule, and I won't stop you, and I won't go to the government either. If you think it's clean here, stay here. If one day you feel the cold and want to go back, the monastery's gate is open. I'm afraid of cold water. I always have been; one mouthful and I've bellyache all night. You pour it and I don't—not because I keep the rule less well than you, but because my stomach is worse than yours."

Someone in the snow laughed once, and hastily held it in. Baaluo did not laugh. "Have you finished?"

"I've finished."

"Then go."

Jingjing turned and walked out. When he reached the garden gate he heard footsteps behind him and looked back. It was An Yan, stripped to the waist, ice crusted in his hair, who ran up and stood before him. "Master, I'll come back with you."

Jingjing took off his outer robe and put it round him, and the three of them walked north.

That winter seven households came back from the south of the city, twenty-odd people, and twenty-odd households stayed on in the garden. Samaan made a line in the ledger: Returned from the south of the city, seven households; the rest not returned; the Master went to speak; did not compel them. When he had written the line his hand shook so that he couldn't hold the brush. Lingbao took the brush from him and, as he dictated, added another line: The Master said: the tares and the wheat, let both grow together.

That winter Babai died. His eyes had long since dimmed and he could not copy scripture. His last years he sat every day in the sun at the scripture-room door listening to Lingbao read, and if Lingbao read a word wrong, he could hear it with his eyes closed. He died at the scripture-room door, leaning against the door frame, the dirt under his fingernails still black—a lifetime's ink. From then on, when Jingjing said the names in the middle of the courtyard before light, he said one more: Babai.

When spring came, Baaluo fell ill. He had poured cold water all winter, poured it into the second month, and took a fever, and coughed blood, and lay in the shed unable to rise. The people of the garden gathered round him singing the offices; they sang seven days, and he burned hotter and hotter. An Yan heard and came to tell Jingjing. Jingjing had Lingbao pack the medicine bag—black plum, coptis root, a packet of ginger—and had Siyuan push the cart along behind, and set out for the south of the city. At the garden gate the people of the garden stopped them, saying the Elder would not see the monastery's people. So Jingjing stood at the garden gate. He stood half an hour, and someone came out from inside and said the Elder bade him come in—him alone.

The shed was very dark and very cold. Baaluo lay on a straw mat under two ragged padded jackets, his face purple with fever, his lips cracked, but his eyes open.

"You've come to watch me die," he said.

Jingjing crouched beside him and felt his forehead. "I've brought medicine. Black plum, coptis, ginger. Brew a bowl and drink it, and the fever will come down a little."

"I won't drink the monastery's medicine. The monastery's medicine is bought with the government's money."

"The medicine comes on credit to the monastery from the house of Shopkeeper Wang in the West Market. They've given it on credit since his grandfather's generation, thirty years of it, and not one coin of government money."

Baaluo looked at him and said nothing more. Jingjing had the people outside heat water, crouched by the fire himself and brewed the medicine, and when it was brewed and cooled to warm, brought it over. Baaluo would not open his mouth, so Jingjing held it, held it until the medicine was nearly cold, and then Baaluo opened his mouth and drank two mouthfuls, and when he had drunk them, turned his head away.

That night Jingjing did not leave. He sat at the door of the shed listening to the coughing inside, fit after fit, and in the second half of the night the coughing died down. Baaluo called him, and he went in. Baaluo was lying with his eyes on the roof of the shed.

"That year at the Chunming Gate, in the cangue, Master Yi pushed money into the escorts' hands, and I cursed him. When I'd cursed him I walked off toward Lantian, singing as I walked, sang till Lantian, sang my voice hoarse, and looked back, and Master Yi was still standing under that willow outside the Chunming Gate. He hadn't gone." He panted a few breaths. "Thirty years in Lingnan, and every time the fever came and I burned out of my wits, I saw that willow. And to this day I still think your monastery is unclean, and that stele is an idol, and the words you borrow are Buddhists' and Daoists' words, and the people you ask favours of are the eunuchs' people. To my death I won't accept it."

"I know," said Jingjing.

"I want to hear that one. The one I sang outside the Chunming Gate. I can't sing any more. You sing."

So Jingjing sang, there in the shed: "My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me? why art thou so far from helping me, and from the words of my roaring?" His voice was poor and he sang off pitch. When he reached I am poured out like water, and all my bones are out of joint, the people of the garden outside the shed began to sing too, twenty-odd households standing in the snow, following line by line. When they reached the end—For he hath not despised nor abhorred the affliction of the afflicted; neither hath he hid his face from him—Jingjing stopped. Baaluo's eyes were still open, looking at the hole in the shed roof where the wind came in, and beyond the hole the sky was turning white.

Baaluo was buried outside the Kaiyuan Gate, beside Narsai; the people of the south of the city talked it over all night before they agreed. On the day of the burial the people of the south of the city came, and the monastery's people came too, the two groups standing on the two sides of the burial ground with those rows of stakes between them. Jingjing read the scripture, only in Syriac, not in Tang.

That summer Samaan was failing. He was past seventy and had kept the accounts forty years, and at the end his hand shook so that he couldn't hold the brush, and he had Lingbao write for him, he saying a line and Lingbao writing it.

The day before he died he had Lingbao bring the ledger and turn to the last page. "Write: Samaan, Warden of the Rule, hands over the accounts to the deacon Lingbao."

Lingbao wrote it. Samaan looked at the line for several breaths and said, "Add another line."

"What shall I add?"

"Add: In several places in the accounts the Master did not follow the rules; Samaan judged this wrong; all are recorded. Let those who come after read them, and think for themselves."

Lingbao wrote it as he said. Samaan sent for Jingjing too. When Jingjing came he drew both hands out of his sleeves and took Jingjing's hand. "Master, that year you said, the tares and the wheat, let both grow together, and I recorded it. But to this day I haven't worked it out—which is the tares, and which the wheat."

"I haven't worked it out either," said Jingjing. "The Lord said wait for the harvest. At the harvest, the Lord will sort them himself."

Samaan nodded, drew his hands back into his sleeves, and tucked them in. The next morning he died like that, his hands tucked in.

The leather-bound ledgers came into Lingbao's hands: forty years, nine books filled, and the last half empty. Lingbao locked them in the scripture cabinet together with the broken stone, and hung the key at his own waist.

The evening Samaan died, Jingjing wiped the character tares off his board and meant to write another. He sat until the clapper, and what he wrote in the end was the same character.
