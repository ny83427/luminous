Volume Eleven　Huichang

Chapter 74　Three Days

The decree was promulgated in the eighth month. That morning early the Jingzhao prefecture's men posted notices at every ward gate, and the ward headmen beat gongs and cried it street by street, calling every household out to hear. Headman Liu of Yining Ward stood under the ward gate holding a copy and read it to those who gathered round, read it stumbling; some characters he didn't know, and when he reached them he slurred past. Jingfu stood in the crowd, listening. The decree ran several hundred characters and spoke of the Buddhist monasteries of the empire, said the Buddha's Law was originally a teaching of the west and since entering the Middle Kingdom had consumed the state and corrupted its customs; said four thousand six hundred-odd monasteries were demolished in the empire, two hundred and sixty thousand-odd monks and nuns returned to lay life and taken in as households of the Two Taxes; forty thousand-odd lesser shrines and hermitages demolished, tens of millions of qing of rich upper fields taken in, and a hundred and fifty thousand slaves taken in as households of the Two Taxes. When he reached the last few sentences Headman Liu paused, looked once into the crowd, saw Jingfu, and bowed his head again to read: Three thousand-odd of the Daqin, the Magi and the Zoroastrians are ordered back to lay life, that they may not mingle with the ways of China.

When he had finished, the ward's people dispersed; some said good, the monks should have been dealt with long ago; some went off with their heads bowed. Headman Liu rolled up the copy, came over, and said to Jingfu: Leader, you heard it. Jingfu said he had heard it. Headman Liu said: three thousand-odd—the Daqin, the Magi and the Zoroastrians in Chang'an together don't come to three thousand; this means the whole empire; Chang'an's—your monastery's dozen-odd—are all in it. He said the notice didn't say when they'd count; the inspectorate would send men; when they came, he couldn't stop them.

The inspectorate's clerks came that afternoon—an old clerk with two men; they did not come in at the gate, but pasted a notice at the monastery gate, and the notice said: The Daqin Monastery, in accordance with the decree: its community returned to lay life, its buildings confiscated; the articles within the monastery await inventory and taking in; they are not to be moved privately, nor sold privately; offenders to be treated as stealers of government property. When it was pasted, the old clerk told Jingfu: the day of inventory awaits the inspectorate's dispatch; when the dispatch comes down, they'll come to count. Jingfu asked roughly what day. The old clerk said: soon; the Buddhist monasteries are nearly counted; then it's the turn of you few hu monasteries.

That evening Jingfu told the monastery's people what the notice said. Joseph and Elijah sat under the colonnade, and when they had heard it Elijah asked: foreigners sent back to their own places—sent where? Jingfu said: their own places—your own countries; Merv; the Patriarchate. Elijah said he came in the fourth year of Yuanhe, thirty-six years ago; over in Merv his Pa and Ma were long dead; going back, back to where? Joseph looked at the stele in the middle of the courtyard and said: and the stele? The notice says the articles within the monastery are not to be moved—is the stele an article within the monastery?

That night Qushen came. Salang was keeping watch inside the gate; at dusk Jingfu had lit the lamp, and he sat beside it with the wooden staff across his knees. In the second watch, when the ward street was empty, he saw that man standing again at the foot of the ward wall of Jincheng Ward opposite, in a grey cloth Daoist robe, both hands folded in his sleeves, looking this way. This time the man did not leave; he stood a while, crossed the street, came to the monastery gate, came up to the lamp, and stopped. Salang stood up and looked at him, did not stop him and did not call him; the man stood before the lamp, and the lamplight lit his face; it was Qushen. He had grown thin; his cheeks were sunken; under his eyes were dark patches. He said: Brother Salang, he must see the leader.

Jingfu came out. Qushen stood before the lamp and did not come in at the gate. He said he wouldn't come in; he had slipped out of the temple; the ward gate was shut, and he had come over the wall; he'd go as soon as he'd said it. He said today the Daoist Lu had gone to the Chang'an county office with the abbot's card and begged a county document, and the document said: As the Daqin Monastery of Yining Ward has been abolished by decree, the one stone stele within the monastery, erected by a hu teaching, its text insulting to Laozi, ought not to be preserved; the Tongzhen Temple is directed to take it away, for use in the embankment of the Yong'an canal. He said that stretch of embankment was just east of Jincheng Ward; last summer a stretch of it was washed out by the water; the temple meant to repair it and lacked stone; the Daoist Lu said that stele of the Daqin Monastery, so big and thick, would be just right laid under the embankment. He said once the Daoist Lu had the document he went back to the temple and told the abbot: as soon as the inspectorate's men had inventoried the monastery and it was confiscated, he'd take men to haul it; three or five days at most.

Jingfu listened, standing inside the threshold. Qushen said today in the temple, when the Daoist Lu brought that document to show the abbot, he was beside them copying scripture and saw it; he saw those characters, for use in the embankment of the Yong'an canal, and the brush in his hand fell on the scripture roll and the ink spread in a great blot; the Daoist Lu looked round at him and asked what was the matter; he said his hand shook. He said he finished copying that page, tore up the sheet the ink had spread on, copied it again, and when he'd finished waited till dark, waited till the temple's people were asleep, and came over the wall.

When he had said it, he took a bundle from his robe—not a big one, wrapped in an old cloth—and handed it to Jingfu. He said in here are the eleven rolls he took away last year; he said the paper he left said the new rolls go with me; he had carried them with him more than a year; today he was bringing back what he carried. He said these more than a year, copying Daoist scriptures in the temple, he'd light a lamp at night and take out these eleven rolls to look at, and looking, he came to those dots, the dots Master put, and he thought: these characters are borrowed to live in; borrowed to live in—the owners are still there; he'd gone to live in someone else's house; his own house is on this side. He said he didn't know where the monastery would go after this, and didn't know whether he could come back; so he was bringing the scriptures back; if the scriptures are in the monastery, they're at home.

Jingfu took the bundle and held it in his arms. He said: Qushen, come in. Qushen shook his head and said he wouldn't come in; he wore a Daoist robe; he'd come over the wall, and must climb back before light; if he didn't go back, the Daoist Lu would know it was he who had passed the word. He said he'd go back and still copy scripture in the temple; if anything stirred over at the temple, he'd come and tell them again. When he had said it, he looked once at the lamp at the gate, turned, and went off toward the ward street. He went a few steps, looked back, and said: Leader, the stele—you'll have to find a way yourselves; this is as far as he can go.

That night no one in the monastery slept. Jingfu told them what Qushen had said, and when he had told it, those sitting in the small room—Lingbao, Joseph, Elijah, the Shi brothers, Salang, Rijin, and He Siniang—all looked at him.

Joseph spoke first. He said: this is taking Master's stele to pave a canal bank, to be trodden on, soaked by water, buried in mud; the characters on the stele, Elder Alopen's name, Great Worthy Yisi's name, Patriarch Hnanisho's name, the cross on the stele's head—all laid under the canal. He said he'd rather the monastery's people all went together and knelt at the county office gate till the magistrate changed that document. The Shi eldest said kneeling was no use; the county's document is the county's; if the monastery's people knelt, the magistrate wouldn't change it; and if he did, above him is the Governor of Jingzhao, and above the Governor of Jingzhao is Zhao Guizhen. Salang said: then keep watch; the stele's in the courtyard; whoever comes to haul it, he'll stand in front of the stele; his grandfather's father said hold your ground and there's no need to fight. Jingfu said: Brother Salang, you can hold one day, not three; those who come will be the county's men, with a document; if you stand in front of the stele, that's resisting officials; resist officials and they'll chain you, and haul the stele off just the same.

Rijin, sitting by the door, said then that in his years south of the city he had seen how it went when Buddhist monasteries were torn down: the bronze images were smashed and carried off to be melted; the stone images were pushed over, some hauled off to pave roads, some chiselled into millstones; stone can't run away; when the people run, the stone is someone else's. He said if the stele could be hidden—hidden where no one could find it, and when this was over, set up again. Jingfu said: a stone that big—where would you hide it? Taller than a man, thousands of jin; the dozen-odd of the monastery can't lift it, can't haul it; out of the monastery gate, it's a whole city's eyes.

Lingbao sat in the corner and waited till they had all spoken; the lamp-wick sparked with a pop, and he spoke. He said he couldn't think of a way. He said he was seventy-eight, his eyes couldn't see; the stele he had touched all his life; they couldn't haul it, couldn't hide it, couldn't hold it; he couldn't think of a way either. He said: but he remembered something, something his Pa told him when he was small. Pa said there was a woman in the Patriarchate's scriptures called Esther, the daughter of a captive Jew, who later became the queen of the king of Persia; at that time there was a minister in the palace who meant to kill all the Jews; the day was set, and the king's decree had gone out; once the decree had gone out, it could not be changed. Esther meant to go in to the king to plead for her people; but by the rule of that time, whoever went in to the king unsummoned was put to death; before she went, she had all the Jews in the city fast for her three days, neither eating nor drinking, and she and her maidens fasted too; after three days she went in to the king, and said: if I perish, I perish. He said when Pa got this far, he asked him: Esther fasted three days—what was she asking for? He was small then and said: asking the king not to kill her. Pa said: she was asking for what she herself didn't know; she didn't know whether the king would hold out the golden sceptre, didn't know whether she would die, didn't know whether the Jews would live; she knew nothing; she knew only that she must go, and that before she went, everyone must be with her, three days.

He said he knew nothing today either. He said a few days ago he went to the inspectorate with Elder Samaan's accounts to see a man; the man promised him nothing, and he didn't know whether the man would do anything, or could; he knew nothing. He said what he thought was: from tomorrow, those of the monastery who are willing fast three days—drink water, eat no food; the children eat half a meal, the old and the sick one meal; in the three days, stand morning devotion as usual, recite the scriptures as usual, open the gruel shed as usual and give it to the people of the ward street to eat; we ourselves don't eat. He said: in the three days, what to ask for he doesn't know; whether the stele is hauled, whether the monastery is torn down—he won't tell anyone to ask for those; ask for one thing only: ask the Lord to see to it; let the Lord see to it, and we watch; and after three days, whatever we see, that's what it is.

Jingfu said: good. He said: he's the leader; he says fast three days, from dawn tomorrow. Joseph said he'd fast. Elijah said he'd fast. Salang said he'd fast. Rijin said he'd fast too; in his years south of the city he kept the monks' fasts, no food after noon; he was used to hunger. He Siniang said she'd fast; Xiaoman and Ayi she'd watch over, and give them half a meal.

The first day, morning devotion was nineteen. When they had stood, Jingfu went into the scripture room and read a passage of scripture, a psalm, the one that goes "I will lift up mine eyes unto the hills"—his Master had once written the character hills in the frost on the well curb; his Syriac reading was poor; he read a line, and Joseph beside him read it right for him a line. When he had read it, he took up the charcoal and wrote on the old board the character san, three. The gruel shed opened as usual, one pot; Jingfu and Rijin carried the pot out and set it in the ward street; the Shi Second ladled; the queue stretched from the pot to the corner of the ward street; an old woman, receiving her gruel, saw Jingfu standing by the pot and asked him: Great Worthy, why aren't you eating today? Jingfu said: today we don't eat. The old woman said: your monastery's to be torn down; the ward all knows; if you don't eat, save the rice, there'll still be some later. Jingfu said: the rice is cooked for you; later's business, later.

That day no one came from the inspectorate, and no one came from the county. Early on the second day people came from the Tongzhen Temple, four or five, driving an empty cart loaded with rollers and thick ropes; they stopped outside the monastery gate, unloaded the rollers one by one, and stacked them against the wall outside the gate, stacked them half a man high. The leader was a disciple of the Daoist Lu, twenty-odd, who stood at the gate and peered in through the crack, saw the stele in the courtyard, had someone bring out a long pole marked with measurements, stood outside the gate and held the pole upright, gauging the stele's height against it, calling out figures for another to note down. Salang stood inside the gate, his staff on the ground; the disciple saw him, smiled, and said: brother, we won't come in; we're measuring from outside; the rollers we'll leave here for now; in a few days we'll come and haul it; saves running back and forth. They left; the rollers stayed at the foot of the wall, stacked neat and square; the ward's people passing the gate all saw them.

The second day Joseph was dizzy and stood morning devotion holding a pillar of the colonnade; when they had stood he sat on the steps at the hall door and could not get up. Elijah sat beside him, and the two of them recited psalms in Syriac; when one was finished they recited another. Lingbao, at the south end of the ward, did not come; He Siniang came and said he'd coughed half the night, and getting up in the morning could not stand; he'd told her to come and tell the leader he was standing at home, standing under the pomegranate tree, facing toward the monastery. Xiaoman and Ayi came with He Siniang; Xiaoman ate half a sesame cake; Ayi would not eat; she said her Ma in the Manichaean monastery went several days a month without eating, called it a fast; she had fasted with her Ma; she wasn't hungry. He Siniang could do nothing with her, and made her drink two bowls of water.

That day Salang kept watch inside the gate all day; at dusk he saw the man in the grey Daoist robe across the ward street standing at the foot of the ward wall again; he stood a while, did not cross the street, and left. That day still no one came from the inspectorate. In the ward street people said the county's men had gone to the Tongzhen Temple, and the temple's Daoists were getting ropes and rollers ready.

The third day a light rain fell; those at morning devotion stood in the rain; the rain was not heavy; it fell on their heads, fell on their shoulders, and no one went under the colonnade to shelter. Jingfu stood in the middle of the courtyard, and when the sky greyed looked back and counted: nineteen, not one fewer; Lingbao had come too, He Siniang holding him up; he stood under the jujube tree, his face grey, his lips so dry they had cracked. When morning devotion broke up, Jingfu had someone help Lingbao to the scripture room to lie down; Lingbao would not; he said he'd sit under the jujube tree, sit in the place he had stood seventy-odd years.

That day the gruel shed opened as ever; the pot was carried out; the rain stopped; in the mud of the ward street the queue formed. That day around the hour of si Old Mother Kang came from the south end of the ward on her bamboo stick; she was eighty-one and had eaten nothing for three days either; Rijin urged her to eat and she would not; she walked to the monastery, walked to the side of the gruel shed, took the ladle from the Shi Second's hand, and said she'd ladle; she said when she was young in the south of the city she'd drawn gruel at a gruel shed a dozen-odd years; now she'd come to ladle it; she ladled one ladleful and poured it into someone's bowl, ladled one, poured one, her hands shaking, and spilled not a drop. In the evening, someone came from the inspectorate.

It was a minor clerk on a thin horse, who got down at the monastery gate with a dispatch in his hand and asked whether the leader was in. Jingfu came out; the clerk handed him the dispatch and said: a dispatch of the Commissioners of Merit's office of the Right Street, with the inspectorate judge's mark; let the leader act by it; he had to go on to the next house; and when he had said it, mounted and left.

Jingfu held the dispatch and stood before the lamp at the gate. The lamp was not yet lit; it was still light; he unfolded the dispatch. Its characters were regular script, written very upright, copied by an inspectorate scribe; the last line bore the office's seal, and beside the seal a mark, the mark of the inspectorate judge Yang Shousu.

He read it. He stood at the gate, and the monastery's people all gathered round: Joseph and Elijah rose from the steps; Salang stood behind him; Rijin, the Shi brothers; He Siniang helping Lingbao over from under the jujube tree; Xiaoman and Ayi following behind. He read it character by character:

The Commissioners of Merit's office of the Right Street, to the Daqin Monastery of Yining Ward. In accordance with this year's decree of the eighth month, the Daqin, the Magi and the Zoroastrians are ordered back to lay life, that they may not mingle with the ways of China. As for the Daqin Monastery of Yining Ward: its community to return to lay life, its buildings to be confiscated, to be disposed of by the decree. The inventory and taking in is deferred to the eighteenth of this month: three days in all. The scriptures, images, articles and stele within the monastery: the leader to dispose of them as he judges fit; let them not be destroyed, let them not be sold privately. Of the community, the Tang to be sent back to their native places and entered on the household registers; the foreigners, as is customary, to be sent back to their own places to be taken in charge there, and permitted to travel with merchant caravans going west, to be passed on inspection by the prefectures and counties along the way. On receipt of the dispatch, act accordingly. The Commissioners of Merit's office of the Right Street; mark of the inspectorate judge Yang Shousu.

When he had read it, he read it again; he came to deferred to the eighteenth of this month: three days in all, came to the stele: the leader to dispose of them as he judges fit, and his hand shook, and the dispatch paper rustled. Joseph asked him: as he judges fit—what does it mean? Jingfu said: it means the leader is to see to it himself. Joseph asked: and the county's document—the Daoist Lu's? Jingfu said: the county manages the wards' affairs; the inspectorate manages the monasteries'; the articles and stele within the monastery—the inspectorate's dispatch has said, the leader to dispose of as he judges fit; the county's document must wait till the inspectorate has inventoried it and the monastery is confiscated before its turn comes; the inspectorate has deferred it three days; these three days the monastery is still the monastery, and the stele is still the monastery's stele.

When he had said it, he turned to look at the old man under the jujube tree. Lingbao stood there, He Siniang holding him up; he could not see the dispatch; he leaned his head forward, listening. When he had heard it, his lips moved and he said something, his voice too low; Jingfu did not catch it. He Siniang, holding him up, bowed her head and leaned close to listen.

Headman Liu came that evening too; he said he'd heard from the headman of Liquan Ward that the Zoroastrian shrine over there had received the same dispatch, also deferred three days; and the Magi, he'd heard, the same; the Commissioners of Merit's office had sent out several today. Jingfu folded the dispatch and tucked it into his robe. He turned and went in at the gate, walked to the middle of the courtyard, walked up to the stele, and put out his hand and pressed it on the stele. The stele had been rained on; it was wet, and cold. Pressing the stele, he said to those behind him: three days.
