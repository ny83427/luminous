Volume Eleven　Huichang

Chapter 74　Three Days

The edict was issued in the eighth month.

Early that day men of the Metropolitan Prefecture posted notices at the gate of every ward, and the ward headmen, beating gongs, went street by street calling every household out to listen. Headman Liu of the Yining Ward stood under the ward gate with a copy and read it to those who gathered round. He read haltingly; some characters he didn't know, and when he reached them he slurred over them.

Jingfu stood in the crowd and listened.

The edict ran to several hundred characters and spoke of the Buddhist monasteries of the realm. It said the law of the Buddha was originally a teaching of the west, and since it entered the Middle Kingdom had eaten away at the state and corrupted its customs. It said four thousand six hundred and more monasteries of the realm were torn down, two hundred and sixty thousand and more monks and nuns returned to lay life and taken as households under the Twice-a-Year Tax; forty thousand and more lesser temples and hermitages torn down, several tens of millions of qing of the richest fields confiscated, and a hundred and fifty thousand slaves made households under the Twice-a-Year Tax.

When he reached the last few sentences, Headman Liu's voice dropped. He raised his head, found Jingfu in the crowd, lowered his head again, and read:

"The three thousand and more of Daqin, the Magi and the fire-temples are ordered back to lay life, that they not mingle with the ways of the Middle Kingdom."

When he had read it the people of the ward dispersed. Some said good, the monks should have been dealt with long ago; some lowered their heads and walked away.

Headman Liu rolled up the copy and came up to Jingfu.

"Director, you've heard."

"I've heard."

"Three thousand and more. The Daqin, the Magi and the fire-temple people in Chang'an all together don't come to three thousand; this means the whole realm," said Headman Liu. "Chang'an's—the dozen-odd of your monastery—are all in it. The notice doesn't say when they'll count. The inspectorate will send someone, and when they come I can't stop them."

The inspectorate's clerks came that afternoon.

The one who came was an old clerk with two men. He did not come in at the gate, but posted a notice on the monastery gate:

The Daqin Monastery, by the edict: the monastery's members return to lay life; the monastery's buildings are confiscated; the articles in the monastery await inventory and receipt; they are not to be moved without leave, nor sold privately; offenders will be dealt with as for theft of government property.

When he had posted it, the old clerk said, "The day of the inventory—wait for the inspectorate's dispatch. When the dispatch comes down, we'll come to count."

"About what day?" asked Jingfu.

"Soon. The Buddhist side is nearly counted, and then it's the turn of you few hu temples."

That evening Jingfu told the monastery's people what the notice said.

Joseph and Elijah sat in the gallery. When they had heard it, Elijah asked, "Foreigners sent back to their own countries—sent where?"

"Your own countries means your own lands. Merv, the Patriarchate."

"I came in the fourth year of Yuanhe. Thirty-six years," said Elijah. "In Merv my Pa and Ma died long ago. If I go back, back to where?"

Joseph was looking at the stele in the middle of the courtyard.

"And the stele? The notice says the articles in the monastery aren't to be moved. Is the stele an article of the monastery?"

No one answered him.

That night Qushen came.

At dusk Jingfu had lit the lamp at the gate. Salang kept watch inside the gate, sitting beside the lamp with the wooden staff across his knees. At the second watch, when there was no one left in the ward street, he saw across the way at the foot of the Jincheng Ward's wall that man standing there again, in a grey cloth Daoist robe, hands tucked in his sleeves, looking this way.

This time the man did not leave. He stood a while, crossed the street, came to the monastery gate, came up to the lamp, and stopped.

Salang stood up, and did not stop him, and did not call out. The lamplight shone on the man's face. It was Qushen. He had grown thin, his cheeks sunken, a bruise of blue under his eyes.

"Brother Salang," said Qushen. "I must see the Director."

Jingfu came out. Qushen stood before the lamp and did not come in at the gate.

"I won't come in. I've slipped out of the temple. The ward gates are shut; I came over the walls. I'll say it and go," he said. "Today Daoist Lu went to Chang'an County with the abbot's card and got a document from the county."

"What document?"

"The document says: The Daqin Monastery of the Yining Ward, having been abolished by the edict, has in it one stone stele, set up by a hu religion, whose text insults Lord Lao and ought not to be preserved; the Tongzhen Temple is to take it away, to be used for the embankment of the Yong'an Canal."

Jingfu stood inside the threshold and did not move.

"That stretch of embankment on the Yong'an Canal is just east of the Jincheng Ward; last summer the water broke down a length of it. The temple means to repair it and is short of stone," said Qushen. "Daoist Lu says the Daqin Monastery's stele is big and thick, just right to lay under the embankment. He got the document, came back to the temple and told the abbot that as soon as the inspectorate has inventoried the monastery and it's confiscated, he'll bring men to haul it away. Three to five days at most."

"How do you know?"

"He showed the abbot that document, and I was beside them copying scripture and saw it," said Qushen. "When I saw those words—to be used for the embankment of the Yong'an Canal—the brush fell from my hand onto the scripture roll and the ink spread in a great blot. Daoist Lu turned and asked me what was the matter. I said my hand shook. I finished copying that page, tore out the sheet with the ink blot, copied it again. When I'd copied it I waited till dark, waited till the temple's people were asleep, and came over the wall."

He took from his breast a bundle, not large, wrapped in a piece of old cloth, and held it out.

"In here are the eleven rolls I took away last year," he said. "That paper of mine said the new rolls go with me. I've carried them with me more than a year; today I've brought back what I carried."

Jingfu took the bundle.

"This year and more I've been copying Daoist scriptures at the temple. At night I lit a lamp and took out these eleven rolls to look at. Looking, I came to those dots—the dots the Master put," said Qushen. "And I thought: these words are borrowed, to live in. Borrowed to live in, and the owner is still there; I'm the one who's gone to live in someone else's house. My own house is on this side."

He looked at the bundle in Jingfu's arms.

"I don't know where the monastery will go from here, and I don't know whether I can come back. So I've brought the scriptures back. If the scriptures are in the monastery, they're at home."

Jingfu held the bundle to him.

"Qushen, come in."

Qushen shook his head.

"I'm wearing a Daoist robe. I have to get back over the wall before daylight. If I don't go back, Daoist Lu will know it was I who passed the word," he said. "I'll go back and keep copying scripture at the temple. If anything stirs over there, I'll come and say."

He turned and went off along the ward street. He had gone a few steps when he looked back.

"Director, the stele—you'll have to find a way yourselves. This is as far as I can go."

That night no one at the monastery slept.

Jingfu told them what Qushen had said. In the little room sat Lingbao, Joseph, Elijah, the Shi brothers, Salang, Rijin, and He Siniang, all looking at him.

Joseph spoke first.

"They mean to take the stele to bed a canal bank. To be trodden on, soaked by water, buried in mud," he said. "The characters on the stele—Elder Alopen's name, Master Yisi's name, the patriarch Hnanisho's name, the cross at its head—all bedded under a canal. I'd rather the monastery's people all went and knelt at the county office gate until the magistrate changed that document."

"Kneeling's no use," said the elder Shi brother. "The county's document is the county's. If the monastery's people kneel, the magistrate won't change it. If he did change it, above him there's the Metropolitan Governor, and above the Metropolitan Governor there's Zhao Guizhen."

"Then we guard it," said Salang. "The stele's in the courtyard. Whoever comes to haul it, I'll stand in front of the stele. My grandfather said: hold the gate, and you needn't fight."

"Brother Salang, you could hold out a day; you couldn't hold out three," said Jingfu. "The ones who come will be the county's men, with a document. Stand in front of the stele, and that's resisting officers. Resist officers, and they'll chain you, and haul the stele off all the same."

Rijin, sitting by the door, spoke now.

"Those years in the south of the city, I saw what happened when Buddhist monasteries were torn down. Bronze images smashed and carried off to be melted. Stone images pushed over; some hauled off to bed roads, some chiselled into millstones," he said. "A stone can't run away. If the people run, the stone is someone else's. If the stele could be hidden, hide it, hide it where they can't find it, and when this has passed, set it up again."

"A stone that size—where would you hide it?" said Jingfu. "Taller than a man, thousands of jin. The dozen-odd of us in the monastery can't lift it or haul it. Once out of the monastery gate, there are the whole city's eyes."

No one in the room spoke any more. The lamp-wick sputtered.

Lingbao, sitting in the corner, spoke now.

"I can't think of a way," he said. "I'm seventy-eight, my eyes can't see. That stele I've felt all my life. We can't haul it, can't hide it, can't hold it; I can't think of a way."

His two hands rested on his knees.

"But I remember something. Pa told it to me when I was small," said Lingbao. "In the Patriarchate's scriptures there's a woman named Esther, a daughter of the Jews who had been carried captive, who later became queen to the king of Persia. In those days there was a minister in the palace who meant to kill all the Jews; the day was fixed and the king's decree had gone out. Once the decree had gone out, it couldn't be changed."

Joseph raised his head.

"Esther meant to go in to the king to plead for her people. But the rule in those days was that whoever went in to the king without being called was to die. Before she went, she had all the Jews of the city fast for her three days, neither eating nor drinking, and she and her maidens fasted too. After three days she went in to the king. She said: if I perish, I perish."

Lingbao stopped and drew a breath.

"When Pa told it this far, he asked me: when Esther fasted three days, what was she asking for? I was small, and said: asking the king not to kill her. Pa said: she was asking for what she herself didn't know. She didn't know whether the king would hold out the golden sceptre, didn't know whether she'd die, didn't know whether the Jews could live. She knew nothing. She knew only that she must go, and before she went, everyone must be with her, three days."

He turned his face toward the people in the room.

"Today I know nothing either. A few days ago I went to the inspectorate with Elder Samaan's accounts to see a man. That man promised me nothing. Whether he'll do anything, what he can do, I don't know."

"Uncle Lingbao…" said Jingfu.

"I think that from tomorrow, those of the monastery who are willing should fast three days," said Lingbao. "Drink water, eat no food. The children eat half a meal, the old and the sick one meal. In the three days the morning is stood as always, the scriptures recited as always, the gruel shed opened as always—opened for the people of the ward street to eat, and we ourselves don't eat."

"Asking for what?" asked Joseph.

"I don't know. Whether the stele's hauled away, whether the monastery's torn down—I won't have everyone ask for that. Everyone asks for one thing only: ask the Lord to see to it as he will," said Lingbao. "The Lord sees to it, and we watch. After three days, whatever we see, that's what it is."

"Good," said Jingfu. "I'm the Director. Three days' fast, from daybreak tomorrow."

"I'll fast," said Joseph.

"I'll fast," said Elijah.

"I'll fast," said Salang.

"I'll fast too," said Rijin. "Those years in the south of the city I followed the monks in not eating after noon. I'm used to going hungry."

"I'll fast," said He Siniang. "Liu'er and Ayi I'll watch over; I'll give them half a meal."

On the first day nineteen stood at the morning.

When it was done Jingfu went into the scripture room and read a psalm—I will lift up mine eyes unto the hills. The Master had once written the character hill in the frost on the well curb. Jingfu's Syriac was poor; he read a line, and Joseph beside him read it correctly for him. When he had read it he took up the charcoal and on the old board wrote the character san, three.

The gruel shed opened as always, one pot. Jingfu and Rijin carried the pot out and set it in the ward street, and the younger Shi brother ladled the gruel. The queue stretched from the pot to the corner of the ward street.

An old woman, having got her gruel, saw Jingfu standing by the pot.

"Your Reverence, why aren't you people eating today?"

"Today we don't eat."

"Your monastery's to be torn down. Everyone in the ward knows," said the old woman. "If you don't eat, keep the rice, and you'll have some later."

"The rice is for cooking for you," said Jingfu. "What comes later, we'll speak of later."

That day no one came from the inspectorate, and no one came from the county.

Early on the second day men came from the Tongzhen Temple, four or five of them, driving an empty cart loaded with rollers and thick ropes, and stopped outside the monastery gate. They unloaded the rollers one by one and stacked them against the wall outside the gate, stacked them half a man high.

Their leader was a disciple of Daoist Lu, twenty or so. He peered in through the crack of the gate, saw the stele in the courtyard, and had someone bring a long pole. The pole was notched with measures. He stood it upright outside the gate, sighted the height of the stele against it, called out figures, and had another man write them down.

Salang stood inside the gate, the wooden staff planted on the ground.

The disciple saw him and smiled.

"Brother, we won't come in; we'll just measure outside. We'll leave the rollers here for now and come to haul it in a few days. Saves running back and forth."

They left. The rollers stayed at the foot of the wall, stacked neat and square, and the people of the ward passing the gate could all see them.

That day Joseph was dizzy. He stood the morning holding a gallery pillar, then sat on the steps at the hall door and couldn't get up. Elijah sat beside him, and the two of them recited psalms in Syriac; when one psalm was done, they recited another.

Lingbao did not come. He Siniang came to tell Jingfu: "He coughed half the night, and when he got up this morning he couldn't stand. He told me to tell the Director he's standing at home, under the pomegranate tree, facing toward the monastery."

Liu'er and Ayi came with He Siniang. Liu'er ate half a sesame cake. Ayi wouldn't eat.

"My Ma, in the Manichaean temple, went several days a month without eating; she said it was a fast," said Ayi. "I fasted with Ma. I'm not hungry."

He Siniang could do nothing with her, and made her drink two bowls of water.

At dusk Salang saw the man in the grey Daoist robe standing again at the foot of the ward wall across the street. He stood a while, did not cross, and left.

That day, again no one came from the inspectorate.

On the third day a light rain fell.

Those at the morning stood in the rain. It was not heavy; it fell on their heads and shoulders, and no one went into the gallery to shelter. Jingfu stood in the middle of the courtyard, and when the sky greyed he looked back and counted: nineteen, not one fewer. Lingbao had come too, on He Siniang's arm, standing under the jujube tree, his face grey, his lips so dry they had cracked.

When the morning broke up, Jingfu had people help Lingbao to the scripture room to lie down.

"I won't go," said Lingbao. "I'll sit under the jujube tree. Sit in the place I've stood these seventy-odd years."

That day the gruel shed opened all the same. By the time they carried the pot out, the rain had stopped, and the queue stood in the mud of the ward street.

Toward the hour of si, Mother Kang came from the south end of the ward on her bamboo stick. She was eighty-one and had eaten nothing these three days; Rijin had urged her to eat and she wouldn't. She came to the gruel shed and reached out to take the ladle from the younger Shi brother's hand.

"I'll ladle."

"Granny, you rest."

"When I was young in the south of the city, I drew gruel at the gruel shed for ten-odd years," said Mother Kang. "Now I'll ladle it."

She ladled a ladleful and poured it into someone's bowl, ladled and poured, ladled and poured. Her hands shook, and not one drop was spilled.

Toward dusk, someone came from the inspectorate.

The one who came was a minor clerk riding a thin horse. He dismounted at the monastery gate with a dispatch in his hand.

"Is the Director here?"

Jingfu came out. The clerk handed him the dispatch.

"A dispatch from the Right Street Commissioner of Merit's office. The inspectorate's assistant has put his mark on it. The Director is to act accordingly. I've still to go on to the next house."

He mounted and left.

Jingfu stood with the dispatch before the lamp at the gate. The lamp was not yet lit; it was still light. He unfolded the dispatch. The characters were in a regular hand, written very neatly, copied by a clerk of the inspectorate; the last line bore the seal of the Commissioner's office, and beside the seal a mark, the mark of the inspectorate's assistant, Yang Shousu.

The monastery's people had all gathered round. Joseph and Elijah had risen from the steps; Salang stood behind him, and Rijin, the Shi brothers; He Siniang came from under the jujube tree with Lingbao on her arm, Liu'er and Ayi following behind.

"Read it," said Joseph.

Jingfu read it word by word:

The Right Street Commissioner of Merit's office, by dispatch, to the Daqin Monastery of the Yining Ward. In accordance with the edict of the eighth month of this year: Daqin, the Magi and the fire-temples are ordered back to lay life, that they not mingle with the ways of the Middle Kingdom. As to the Daqin Monastery of the Yining Ward, its members return to lay life and its buildings are confiscated, to be disposed of according to the edict. The inventory and receipt are deferred to the eighteenth of this month, three days in all. The scriptures, images, articles and stele of the monastery the Director is to dispose of as he judges fit, not allowing them to be destroyed, not allowing them to be sold privately. The monastery's Tang members are to be sent to their native places and entered on the registers as households; its foreigners, by precedent, are to be sent back to their own countries to be taken in charge, and may travel with caravans going west, the prefectures and counties on the way to examine and let them pass. On receipt of the dispatch, act accordingly. The Right Street Commissioner of Merit's office; marked by the inspectorate's assistant, Yang Shousu.

When he had read it he looked it over again from the beginning. When he saw deferred to the eighteenth of this month, three days in all, when he saw the stele the Director is to dispose of as he judges fit, his hand shook once, and the paper rustled.

"Dispose of as he judges fit—what does it mean?" asked Joseph.

"It means the Director is to see to it himself."

"And the county's document? Daoist Lu's?"

"The county manages the ward's affairs; the inspectorate manages the monastery's. The monastery's articles and stele—the inspectorate's dispatch says the Director is to dispose of them as he judges fit," said Jingfu. "The county's document has to wait until the inspectorate has inventoried and the monastery's been confiscated before its turn comes. The inspectorate has deferred it three days. These three days, the monastery is still the monastery, and the stele is still the monastery's stele."

He turned his head to look at the old man under the jujube tree.

Lingbao stood there, He Siniang holding his arm. He couldn't see the dispatch; he leaned his head forward, listening. When it was done his lips moved; he said something, too low, and Jingfu didn't catch it. He Siniang bent her head close to hear.

Headman Liu came that evening too.

"The headman of the Liquan Ward told me the fire-temple there has got the same dispatch, also deferred three days. The Magi too, I hear," he said. "The Commissioner's office sent out several today."

Jingfu folded the dispatch and put it in his breast. He turned and went in at the gate, walked to the stele in the middle of the courtyard, reached out, and laid his hand on it. The stele had been rained on; it was wet, and cold.

With his hand on the stele, he said to those behind him: "Three days."
