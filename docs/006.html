Volume Two　Li Guangbi

Chapter 6　Parthian Incense

When Yisi came round, the first thing he smelled was medicine.

The smell was very strong, bitter with something rank in it, like some root and some animal fat boiled down together. He opened his eyes and saw above him dark rafters hung with cobwebs. He was lying on a straw pallet with a felt blanket over him, a very old one that smelled of horse sweat.

He tried to sit up, and as soon as his right shoulder moved the pain made him gasp.

"Don't move," said someone beside him.

He turned his head. Squatting beside the pallet was an old soldier, past forty, not tall, a little stooped, his face deeply lined, as if carved by wind and sand. In his hands was a clay bowl of black medicine.

"You've slept two days," the old soldier said. "Any longer and we'd have carried you out and buried you."

Yisi opened his mouth, but his throat was too dry to make a sound. The old soldier put down the medicine and took up a bowl of water instead, held the back of his head, and gave him a few mouthfuls.

"The letter?" Yisi asked.

"Long since in the governor's hands," said the old soldier. "Before you went down you kept muttering that sentence—in the fourth month through Jingxing—must have said it a dozen times."

Yisi closed his eyes and let out a long breath. Then suddenly he opened them again and caught hold of the old soldier's sleeve. "Those two men on the riverbed—"

"Brought in," said the old soldier. "The night you went down, the men on the wall reported what you'd said. The next night the governor sent five men out of the city in the dark, and they went by what you'd told them, found the big rock, found the mark on the stone. Both of them still alive. Brought them back across a horse."

Yisi's hand let go.

"The spearman—they got the arrow out of his leg, and he swore all the while they were doing it, swore half the night, till the physician didn't want to treat him," said the old soldier. "The young one—they got the arrowhead out of his back. He ran a fever two days, talked nonsense, and yesterday the fever broke and he drank half a bowl of gruel. The physician says if he gets through the next few days, he'll live."

Yisi lay there a long while without speaking. After a time he asked: "On the big road—who was it got back?"

"Fellow called Zhao Cheng'en—the camp calls him Zhao Three," said the old soldier. "A Changshan man, went out with the Shuofang army. Got here two days before you. He says his party walked into an ambush under the Tumen Pass, and he's the only one got away."

The old soldier picked up the medicine again. "Drink. The physician says the cut on your shoulder is long, but luckily it didn't touch the bone—you just lost a lot of blood. The one under your ribs is only skin deep. Twenty days' rest and the arm's still yours."

Yisi drank it. It was very bitter, so bitter the root of his tongue went numb.

For the next few days Yisi lay on the pallet and could not get up, and the old soldier came twice a day to change his dressings.

The old soldier did not talk much; yet as he changed the dressings his hands never stopped, and neither did his mouth, asking this and that, as if idly. Where was he from? How had he ended up in Lord Guo's camp? How did the track through the Jingxing hills go, which day had they reached where, behind which ridge was the empty village, at which stretch had they crossed the Mianman? Did Hu people eat pork? Did they pray facing east or west? Sometimes he would ask again, in different words, a question he had asked the day before.

Yisi answered each one. He could see the old soldier was trying to draw him out, and did not say so. He answered slowly; but however he had said a thing one day, he said it the same way the next.

Once the old soldier asked about outside the city: in the half night he had lain in the graveyard before creeping to the wall, had he seen anything? Yisi thought, and mentioned only one thing. Before dawn a troop of rebel horse had come out of the big camp east of the city and gone down to the Hutuo River to water their horses—a lot of men and horses, going back and forth for about the time it takes to eat a meal—and for that while the camp east of the city had looked half empty.

The old soldier said, "Oh," and pulled the strip of cloth he was binding a little tighter, and asked no more.

When Yisi asked about the city, the old soldier answered him too, in bits and pieces. The governor had come into Changshan in the second month. On the day he entered, the militia of Changshan had just tied up a rebel general called An Siyi and opened the gates to surrender. The governor questioned An Siyi at the city gate, two questions in all. The first was whether he knew he deserved to die; An Siyi said nothing. The second was: you've rolled about on battlefields all these years—look at these men of mine; can they stand against Shi Siming or not? Tell me something that makes sense, and I won't kill you. An Siyi told him, and the governor had him untied, and that same day moved his troops into the city.

"One question to frighten him, one to use him," said the old soldier. "Once he'd used him—that man's still in the camp now, looking after the grain."

Later, when Shi Siming came up with more than twenty thousand horse to surround them, the governor opened the east gate and sent troops out, and the crossbowmen on the wall were divided into four squads, one loosing while the next stepped up, so that the arrows never broke off; he also set out a wall of spears along the Hutuo River, and the rebel horse charged several times and more than half of them went down. The next day an old man from a village came to report that five thousand rebel foot had come up from Raoyang, a hundred and seventy li in a day and a night, and were resting at a place called Fengbi south of Jiumen. The governor sent two thousand foot and two thousand horse, banners furled and drums muffled, along the river; when they reached Fengbi the rebels were sitting with their bowls in their hands, eating.

"Not one left," said the old soldier.

After that Shi Siming only kept up the siege and did not attack hard. In the city the grain had run out first, and then the fodder, so that the horses chewed even the straw mats laid on the floors.

One day, as he gathered up the strips of cloth after changing the dressings, the old soldier said, as if in passing: "The day the governor read Lord Guo's letter, he said one thing. Six by the little road, one got through, two more brought in after; six by the big road, one got back."

Yisi looked at him.

The old soldier looked back, and grinned. "He keeps count, in there."

On the fourth day, Zhao Cheng'en came to see him.

Zhao Cheng'en was twenty-seven or -eight, of middling height, with a round face and a fresh scab on his left cheek where something had grazed it. He came in carrying a small pot of thin gruel, which he said he had begged from the cookhouse. He squatted down by the pallet and looked at Yisi, and the rims of his eyes went red.

"Master Yi," he said, "Brother Nine is gone."

Yisi looked at him.

Zhao Cheng'en told what had happened on the big road. They had done as Old Iron told them: banners out by day, a ring of fires at night, and sure enough the rebels had been drawn to them and followed them for three days. On the fourth day, under the Tumen Pass, the rebels had come down suddenly from the hills on both sides, hundreds of them. Feng Shouyi told everyone to fall back, and stayed behind with two men to hold the rear. Feng Shouyi had shouted at him to run, Zhao Cheng'en said, and he had run, and as he ran he looked back once and saw Feng Shouyi ringed by several spears, falling from his horse. He had got into the hills and hidden for four days, gnawing bark when he was hungry and eating snow when he was thirsty, never daring to light a fire at night, before he worked his way round to the walls of Changshan.

He told it in great detail: which day in which ravine, under which rock he had hidden at night—everything clear. As he told it he began to weep, his shoulders heaving.

"Brother Nine was a good man," he said. "All the way, he shared his rations with me."

Yisi's eyes were wet too. He thought of the western mouth of Jingxing: Feng Shouyi taking Old Iron's coin without a word and putting it away inside his coat. He thought of Feng Shouyi saying, Same for you.

"Old Iron is gone too," said Yisi.

Zhao Cheng'en stopped short, then wept harder. He took something out of his coat and put it in Yisi's palm.

It was the Kaiyuan coin.

"Brother Nine pushed it into my hand before he died," said Zhao Cheng'en. "He told me to get back and give it back to Old Iron. And now Old Iron too…"

Yisi held the coin in his fist a long time. Zhao Cheng'en tucked the blanket round him and said, Master Yi, you rest; the city's short of hands, tomorrow I'm going out with the fodder carts; I'll come and see you when I'm back.

Zhao Cheng'en left. Yisi put the coin inside his coat, next to Old Iron's letter.

On the fifth day Yisi could get up and walk a few steps.

He went out of the room holding on to the wall, and went first to the wounded men's quarters. Ma Six lay on a pallet near the door, his leg wrapped up like a rice dumpling. As soon as he saw Yisi he started bawling: cursed him for being slow, cursed the city's physicians for their heavy hands, and when he had cursed his fill he asked whether Old Iron had been buried. Yisi said no; he had covered him with his cloak. Then Ma Six stopped cursing and turned his face to the wall. Thirteen was asleep on a pallet further in, his face still white, but sleeping deeply, one hand clutching a corner of the blanket. Yisi stood by his pallet a while and did not wake him.

From the wounded men's quarters he went to the crossbowmen's camp at the north of the city. He asked the men there whether there was one called Tie Qianshan. They pointed to a corner.

Tie Qianshan was sitting on a wooden stake, fitting a crossbow string. He was about twenty, a head taller than his father, thin, long in the arm and leg, his hands all calluses from bowstrings. He looked up at Yisi, and there was no smile on his face at all. He looked nothing like his father, only in the eyes; when he narrowed them, they too became slits.

Yisi sat down beside him and told him about Old Iron. He told how Old Iron had tossed the coin, how he had taken the little road, how in that empty village he would not rest for anything, and said a wrong you can't name is deadlier than a wrong you can; told how Old Iron had called him Hawk, told of the riverbed by the Mianman, told the last words Old Iron had said before he died, the one about the mutton too.

"He said," said Yisi, "to tell you your pa didn't shame you."

Tie Qianshan had not said a word the whole time, nor put down the string in his hands. When it was finished he kept his head down for a long time before he asked one thing: "When my pa died—was he laughing?"

Yisi thought about it and said: "He was laughing."

Tie Qianshan nodded. Then he laid the string across his knees and covered his face with both hands.

Yisi sat beside him, and sat on until he took his hands away.

When Yisi came out of the crossbowmen's camp, a crowd had gathered by the city gate.

In the middle of the ring knelt a guard of twenty-odd, his hands tied behind him, his face grey. Beside him stood several of the military police, and further out a ring of off-duty soldiers, their faces set, not one of them speaking. Yisi asked the man next to him what had happened. The man said under his breath that last night, keeping a crenel on the east wall, this lad had fallen asleep leaning against the parapet, and when the officer checking the watch came right up to him, he was still snoring.

This was the first time Yisi saw Li Guangbi.

He rode up from the far end of the street and reined in outside the ring, and did not dismount. He was very tall, a good stretch higher than anyone else on horseback. He had not taken off his armour; the plates were dusted with grime and spots of dried blood. His face was long, his eyebrows heavy, his eyes not large, and when he looked at anyone his eyelids hung half closed, as if he were thinking of something else.

Suddenly a soldier pushed out of the crowd and dropped to his knees in front of the horse with a thud, knocking his head on the ground again and again. He said he and the guard were from the same village and had grown up together, and begged the governor for mercy. He said the east wall had been short of men these last days, and the lad had stood three watches running, three days and nights without closing his eyes; he simply could not stand any more; he had not meant it. Beat him with the rod, he begged the governor—beat him to death if you like—only spare his life.

At every knock of his head he said one sentence, and he knocked his forehead bloody.

Li Guangbi had been looking at the kneeling guard all the while, not at the one pleading. When the pleader had finished, he did not answer him either. He only lifted his hand once.

The blade came down.

The pleading soldier was still kneeling in front of the horse, his mouth open, and not a sound came out. Li Guangbi turned his horse and rode off, neither fast nor slow, just as he had come.

That same day the head was hung over the gate tower of the east gate.

Yisi stood outside the ring and watched it go up. In his months with the army he had come to understand the reasoning. The city had been besieged forty days and more; everyone was holding on. One man asleep, the rebels creep up, and it is a whole city that dies. Spare him today, and tomorrow ten men will dare to sleep. Mercy does not command an army—he had heard the saying in Chang'an. He thought of Lord Guo's camp, where a man had dozed on watch too, and got twenty strokes of the rod, and Lord Guo said, "Be careful next time," and the man limped back to his post and never dozed again. Both ways worked.

Only, watching that very straight back ride away on its horse, he understood in his heart that he and this man were not likely to be of one road.

Tie Qianshan had come up beside him at some point, and was looking at the head over the gate tower too.

"Here," said Tie Qianshan, "it's like that." He paused, and said, "When my pa sent me here, he said with Governor Li you live longer."

On his way back from the gate, Yisi went round by the stables to see his chestnut.

Every horse in the city was skin and bone, and the chestnut had lost flesh too. When it saw him it snorted and pushed its head into his chest. Yisi stroked its neck and thought he would find his saddle and look it over. In the tack room beside the stable everyone's saddlery was piled together, dark, smelling of leather and horse sweat. He rummaged about in the dark a while and pulled out a felt saddle pad; when he carried it to the light of the doorway he saw he had the wrong one. It was not his.

He was about to put it back when a smell came to him.

It was very faint, so faint it was almost not there, held inside the felt, smothered by horse sweat and dust; he had to put his face to it before he could catch it.

Benzoin. Parthian incense.

Yisi turned the pad over and put his nose to it again. There was no mistake. Felt drinks smoke more than anything; sit by a fire a few nights and the smoke gets into the wool and is hard to wash out. A man can change his clothes, wash his face, wash every trace of a smell off his body; the piece of felt under his saddle, nobody thinks to wash.

He asked the old soldier who looked after the horses whose saddle this was.

"The one who came back by the big road. Zhao Three's," said the old soldier.

Yisi put the pad back where it had been, just as he had found it.

There was no benzoin in the city of Changshan. The city had been besieged forty days and more; people could not get enough gruel; who had money to spare for incense? And if anyone did, it would be sandalwood burned in a Buddhist temple. Benzoin was what the Hu burned—burned in the tents of fire-worshipping Hu soldiers, burned where they kept their fire. Zhao Cheng'en said he had hidden in the hills four days, never daring to light a fire at night. How could his saddle pad be steeped in the incense of a Hu soldiers' tent?

Unless he had sat by a Hu fire more than one night.

Yisi stood in the stable doorway with his hand pressed on the coin inside his coat, and went over again, sentence by sentence, what Zhao Cheng'en had said that day. Feng Shouyi was a man of few words, but careful. When Old Iron said give it back when you get back, Feng Shouyi would come back and give it back himself. And even dying, even with no time left, a careful man would have known that Old Iron had taken the little road, which was more dangerous than his; whoever he gave the coin to might never get it into Old Iron's hands. And Zhao Cheng'en had told those four days in such detail—which ravine, which rock—as if he had rehearsed them over in his mind several times. And he had wept so very fittingly.

That day, listening, Yisi had not suspected one thing.

Yisi did not go to Li Guangbi. He knew what he was: a foreign monk from Lord Guo's service, in the city a few days, whom the governor had never so much as looked at straight. To go up to the gate tower and say a Tang soldier was a spy, on the strength of a thread of scent in a piece of felt that nobody else could smell.

That evening, when the old soldier came to change his dressings, Yisi told him what had happened in the tack room.

When he had heard it, the old soldier's hands stopped on the strip of cloth. He looked at Yisi a long time and said nothing; he finished the dressing and left.

Only later did Yisi learn that Li Guangbi had known these things before he did.

On the very first day Zhao Cheng'en came into the city, Li Guangbi had had Tie Qianshan watch him in secret. Of twelve riders only two had come back, and he believed neither of them. When the governor sent men out of the city to bring in the two wounded, it was not only to save them. When they were brought in, and the spearman had cursed his fill, they asked him about the road, and what he said matched what Yisi had said from beginning to end, down to the jar of fire in the empty village and the new ice on the Mianman. The questions the old soldier who changed Yisi's dressings had been asking back and forth these days had been checked against each other too, and reported up: what this foreign monk says holds together, and his wounds are real wounds. But on Zhao Cheng'en's side, Tie Qianshan had watched him for days and turned up nothing.

Zhao Cheng'en was no fool. He had been a scout, and knew that someone who had "escaped" back from under rebel swords could not fail to be suspected by somebody in the city. Since coming in he had behaved himself: gone to bed early at night, scrambled by day for the most dangerous work, gone out of the city with the fodder carts, gone up on the walls with the labourers carrying stones, so that everyone who saw him said Zhao Three was a real man. One night he got up in the middle of the night and went off alone toward the northwest corner, and Tie Qianshan followed at a distance with his heart in his mouth; Zhao Cheng'en walked to the foot of the wall, undid his trousers and pissed, looked about him, and walked slowly back to bed.

Tie Qianshan said afterwards that that time he had very nearly been seen.

That night the old soldier went up to the gate tower and reported the saddle pad. The next day the men watching Zhao Cheng'en went from one to three.

Two nights later, at the second watch, Tie Qianshan slipped into Yisi's room.

"He's out," he said. "Gone toward the northwest corner. This time not to the foot of the wall. He's gone up the ramp."

Yisi threw on his clothes and followed him out. The night was very dark, no moon, a high wind. There were no lamps in the city, only a torch at intervals along the wall, jerked about by the wind, and now and then one went out. The two of them went along close under the walls, very slowly. Yisi's right arm was still in a sling across his chest; his left hand felt along the wall.

At the northwest corner there was a stretch of wall that had fallen and been patched, where the fewest guards stood, and tonight it was the change of the watch, one party going and the next coming, with a little gap between. That was the gap Zhao Cheng'en had chosen. They crept to the foot of the wall and saw a figure crouched behind the parapet, head bent, winding something round an arrow.

The other two watchers had arrived too, and lay low under the ramp.

They waited. They waited until the man had wound the cloth tight and nocked the arrow, and only then did Tie Qianshan spring.

He sprang fast and hard, like a wolf. The figure started, spun round and made to leap down off the wall, and Tie Qianshan caught him by the back of the collar and slammed him down on the bricks. The man struggled and groped for the knife at his belt, and Yisi arrived and stamped on his wrist.

A torch was brought over. It was Zhao Cheng'en.

On the arrow in his hand a piece of cloth was wound. Yisi unwound it and spread it out under the torch. There were a few lines written on it, crooked and clumsy: In the city fewer than thirty thousand arrows. Grain for one month. No fodder for the horses. Wall weak at the northwest corner, not more than a hundred guards.

Zhao Cheng'en lay on the ground and took a few breaths, and suddenly stopped struggling. He looked at Yisi and spoke, and his voice was steady still. "Master Yi, listen to me. What's written on this is false. I wanted to draw the rebels to the northwest corner, so the governor could ambush them here, and avenge Brother Nine…"

Tie Qianshan put his foot on his chest and did not let him finish.

He hauled Zhao Cheng'en up off the ground, twisted both his arms behind him, and looked back at Yisi.

"Hawk," he said. "The name my pa gave you—he got it right."

That night Li Guangbi questioned Zhao Cheng'en in the gate tower.

Yisi did not go up. He heard it the next day from Tie Qianshan, who had stood at the door of the gate tower all that night.

At first Zhao Cheng'en gave the same story: the cloth carried false intelligence, he had meant to lure the enemy, to win merit and atone, to avenge Feng Shouyi—told with tears and sobs. Li Guangbi sat behind his table, eyelids half lowered, and did not put in one word. When the man had finished, he asked: "When the people outside the city get your letter, how do they know you wrote it, and weren't forced to?"

Zhao Cheng'en said they knew his hand.

"What else?"

"Nothing else."

Li Guangbi said nothing. He put out two fingers, turned the cloth that had been taken off the arrow round on the table, pushed it in front of Zhao Cheng'en, and pointed at its corner. In the corner three tiny holes had been pricked with a needle, in the shape of the character 品, a little triangle; unless you looked close you would take them for flaws in the weave.

Zhao Cheng'en's face went white all at once. He opened his mouth and shut it again, and only after a good while said in a low voice: "Three holes means it's true. Two holes means I've been taken, and the letter's not to be trusted."

This time, he told the truth.

Their party had walked into an ambush under the Tumen Pass, and it had not been scattered; it had been surrounded, and not one of the six got away. The rebel in command there was a general called Li Lijie, one of Shi Siming's men. Li Lijie asked them who had sent them and what letter they carried. Feng Shouyi would not say, and was beheaded. The rest were questioned one by one, and beheaded one by one. When they came to Zhao Cheng'en, someone recognised his Changshan accent, and Li Lijie asked where his family lived.

He told them. His family lived in Gaocheng, and Gaocheng was in rebel hands then. His father, his mother, his wife, his two children—all in Gaocheng.

Li Lijie kept him in the Hu soldiers' camp three nights, taught him how to write it, how to shoot it, how to make the secret mark, what to say when he went into the city; then gave him a horse and let him go back to Changshan. If he did not write, or wrote false, the five of them in Gaocheng, not one would live. The coin—Feng Shouyi had still been clutching it in his hand when they cut off his head. Zhao Cheng'en had prised it out of his fingers.

"Come back with this coin," said Zhao Cheng'en, "say Brother Nine gave it to me, and you'd believe me."

When Li Guangbi had heard it all, he told him to write another.

"The crossbowmen on the east wall were moved to the north gate the day before yesterday. The east wall is out of arrows; fewer than three hundred guards; the labourers are pulling down roof beams to whittle arrows, and the arrows they whittle all go to the north gate."

Li Guangbi said it a sentence at a time, the clerk wrote it down a sentence at a time, and Zhao Cheng'en copied it onto cloth, in his own hand. His hand shook; he spoiled it twice, and only on the third try got it done. Li Guangbi took it, laid it side by side with the first cloth and looked, and handed him a needle.

"Three holes."

Zhao Cheng'en held the needle and pricked three times at the corner of the cloth. Li Guangbi bent to the lamp and looked once, had the cloth wound on an arrow, and had it shot out, from the northwest corner, just as before.

Tie Qianshan said the arrow flew off into the night without a sound.

The next day, Yisi watched Li Guangbi set out a whole city afresh.

At daybreak half the banners on the east wall were taken down. Squad after squad of crossbowmen came down the ramps of the east wall with their crossbows on their shoulders, crossed the main street and went toward the north gate, without hurrying, in plain sight of the slopes outside the city. On the north wall the banners were set thicker than usual, and the drums were beaten more often. The guards left on the east wall were few and far between, one man to every several crenels, and at some crenels straw men had simply been stood up in armour.

Yisi stood on the north wall and saw, far off on an earth bank east of the city, a few small black dots lying flat, which had not moved all morning.

When dark came, the crossbowmen who had gone to the north gate came back, squad after squad. They did not use the main street, but the narrow lanes under the wall; no lights, no talking. Back on the east side they lay low under the parapets, lay low inside the barbican, packed in layer on layer. Tie Qianshan's squad lay under the seventh crenel to the left of the east gate. Yisi went up on the wall with them; nobody sent him away, and nobody paid him any attention, so he found a spot beside Tie Qianshan and sat down.

That night there was movement outside the city.

Not the main force: a few hundred riders, who came up in the dark outside the east gate and rode back and forth under the wall shooting up at it, very thickly. There were only a handful of guards on the wall; they shot back a few scattered arrows, and some of them were hit and fell.

Of the crossbowmen lying low under the parapets, not one stood up. The governor's order was that not one crossbow was to show its head.

A guard was hit and pitched off a crenel and fell right among the lying crossbowmen, clutching his neck, the blood welling between his fingers, his legs kicking at the ground, kick after kick. The crossbowman next to him, his face twisted, put his hands to the ground to get up, and the squad leader planted his foot on the man's back and pushed him down again, and set his own teeth, and did not make a sound.

Yisi was very close. One of the guard's kicking feet struck his leg. He put out his left hand and pressed the man's hand, pressed it on the welling wound in his neck. He could not hold it in. After a while the man's legs stopped kicking.

The rebel horsemen below rode round a few times, saw that hardly any arrows came back from the wall, gave a whistle, and withdrew.

That night, seven guards died on the east wall.

Yisi sat against the parapet until daylight. He knew those few hundred riders had been sent by Shi Siming to test them; if a thousand crossbow bolts had flown off the wall, there would have been nothing tomorrow. He knew too that those seven had been set on the wall for Shi Siming to see. He could reckon that account. He had only never seen a man reckon like that.

Another day passed, and at first light Shi Siming came.

Yisi was still on the east wall. He saw from a distance Li Guangbi go up to the gate tower, and as he stepped onto the ramp take a short knife from a guard and push it into his own boot. Tie Qianshan said the governor always did that before a big battle: if the city fell, he said, he would sooner finish himself than fall into Shi Siming's hands.

On the plain outside the city the rebel horse were drawn up, more than ten thousand of them, a dense black mass, like a slab of iron. Far behind there was another great mass, its banners still, not coming forward.

The drums sounded. The rebel horse charged.

The east gate opened, and five thousand foot went out of the city and drew up, on the south side of the plain east of the city along the Hutuo River, in a wall of spears—long spears one against the next, their butts grounded and their heads slanting outward, like a thorn hedge. The rebel horse reached the hedge and the horses would not run onto the spear points; they checked, the front ones stopped, the ones behind rammed into them, and they jammed together in a mass.

Just then, the crossbows on the wall spoke.

The crossbowmen who had lain low a day and a night stood up from under the parapets, rank on rank. The first squad loosed and fell back to span its bows, the second stepped up; the second loosed, the third stepped up. Volley followed volley with never a pause, like a rain that would not stop. The jammed horsemen tried to get away north—north was the city wall; tried to break south—south was the wall of spears; tried to fall back—the men behind were still pushing forward.

Yisi saw that at the left corner of the spear wall an officer led his company back some dozens of paces. On the gate tower, Li Guangbi beckoned a guard, unhooked his own sword from his belt, handed it to him, and pointed that way.

The guard took the sword and spurred off.

Yisi saw the guard reach the officer. The officer looked round, and his face changed. He shouted something at the top of his voice, pointing at an arrow sticking out of his horse's chest. The guard reined in and looked back toward the gate tower. The officer had already jumped down, seized a horse from a trooper beside him, swung up onto it, and led his company back again, charging, to the very front.

When the sun was a pole's height in the sky, the rear of the rebel army was suddenly in confusion.

At first Yisi could not see what had happened. He saw that under that great mass of motionless banners at the rebel rear a cloud of dust had risen, and in the dust there was shouting, coming closer. Then he saw clearly: it was Tang troops, two thousand or so, no banners, no drums, who had worked their way up from downstream along the riverbed of the Hutuo. It was just the hour when the rebels watered their horses at the river, and their camp was half empty.

He thought of the horses he had counted that day in the graveyard, and of the old soldier's hand drawing the strip of cloth a little tighter.

The rebel line wavered; first the rear fell back, then the front. Shi Siming's great banner moved north, moved fast.

Li Guangbi did not go out of the city to pursue.

The battle was over by noon.

On the plain outside the city lay men and horses, patch after patch. One horse with several arrows in it lay outside the spear wall, not yet dead, struggling up again and again, getting halfway up and falling back. The man on it had long since stopped moving.

Yisi stood by a crenel on the east wall, not far from the gate tower. He heard several officers go up to report the tally of the slain; they gave a number. Li Guangbi said "Hm," and then: "Today, I've killed about three thousand." He said it in the same voice as he had said that morning that the wind was strong.

One officer said something else in a low voice; Yisi caught only the word Gaocheng.

Li Guangbi was looking out over the city and did not turn his head.

"The lives of his family," he said, "are Li Lijie's affair."

After a while Yisi saw him come down from the gate tower. Passing the place where the seven guards lay, he stopped a moment, looked down at them, had their names written down to be reported to their families as killed in action, and walked on.

Shi Siming had withdrawn, but where he had withdrawn to, nobody knew.

That night Li Guangbi sent a few riders out of the city to look at the rebel camp: were they breaking camp and going north, or gathering their stragglers to come again in a few days? Tie Qianshan led them, and Yisi was among them. It was Tie Qianshan who brought word that Yisi was to go; the governor had named him, he said.

They went out at the third watch, leading their horses, the horses muzzled, their hooves wrapped in cloth, and felt their way northeast along the riverbed of the Hutuo. After ten li or so the rebel camp lay ahead. There were fires in it, many more than usual, one heap beside another, scattered like stars over a great stretch.

They lay down behind an earth bank.

The wind was blowing from the camp. Lying on the bank, Yisi caught a smell. It was not as it had been in the shrine, nor as in the saddle pad, so faint that you had to put your nose to it. This time it was very strong, strong enough to be sweet, beating against his face in gusts; it could have been smelled several li off.

Benzoin.

He raised his head and looked into the camp. None of those many fires were large, and by each sat a few men, heads bowed, not moving. When the wind dropped you could hear someone over there chanting, very low, a droning, the words not clear; but Yisi knew the cadence. When someone died in the fire-worshipping households of Balkh, this was how they chanted. On a high slope behind the camp a row of white things had been laid out, one beside another, a long line of them, and men with torches were walking up and down among them.

They were the dead, wrapped in white cloth, unburied.

Tie Qianshan, lying beside him, asked under his breath: "What are they doing?"

"Keeping vigil," said Yisi.

The fire-worshippers say that when a man dies his soul does not leave at once; it stays beside the body three nights, and at dawn on the fourth day crosses the bridge to the other side. For those three nights the living keep the fire, add incense to it, and chant for him, so that he may go in peace. The dead are not put in the ground. The fire-worshippers think the dead unclean and fear they will defile the earth, so they lay them on high places, and gather the bones later.

"Three nights?" said Tie Qianshan.

"Tonight is the first," said Yisi. "In those three nights they won't break camp."

Tie Qianshan said "Mm," looked a while longer, made a sign with his hand, and the few of them slowly backed down off the bank.

Yisi went last. Before he went down he looked back once more at that field of fires. He thought of the Hu boy by the Mianman who had cried out for his mother, the beard on his face still soft. He had closed his eyes for him, but no one had kept three nights by him, no one had added a pinch of incense to a fire for him. He had left in such haste that he did not even know the boy's name.

On the way back to the city, no one spoke.

Early the next morning, Zhao Cheng'en was beheaded at the city gate.

That night the old soldier who changed Yisi's dressings came very late.

When he had changed them he did not leave at once, but sat by the pallet, took a little wine gourd from inside his coat, took a drink, and held it out to Yisi. Yisi shook his head. The old soldier did not press him, and drank again himself.

"The governor heard from Tie Qianshan," said the old soldier, "about the three nights. He's ordered that not one post on the wall is to be withdrawn these three nights." He paused, and said, "Win or lose, he's the same."

Yisi said nothing.

"And another thing," said the old soldier. "That time you said, in passing, about the hour they water the horses—it got used. The governor said: that foreign monk's nose is useful."

When he had said it he grinned. "Just that, nothing more. Don't go expecting him to say anything else."

Yisi opened his left hand. In the palm lay the Kaiyuan coin, which he had held in his fist so many days that it had worn bright. The crescent mark on its back glinted once in the lamplight.

The old soldier looked at the coin, and the grin left his face. After a while he asked: "You monks—when you've killed a man, what do you have to do?"

Yisi was silent a long time.

"Own it," he said.

"And when you've owned it?"

Yisi did not answer.

The old soldier did not ask again. He put the wine gourd back inside his coat, stood, brushed the dirt off his knees, and said: "Get some sleep. Lord Guo will be here soon."

As he reached the door, Yisi suddenly called him back.

"Old brother," said Yisi, "what's your name?"

The old soldier turned round and grinned. "When Lord Guo comes and we're drinking together, I'll tell you."
