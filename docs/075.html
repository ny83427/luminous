Volume Eleven　Huichang

Chapter 75　Burying the Stele

Jingfu stood before the stele holding the dispatch and said three days, and when he had said it everyone in the courtyard stood still. It was nearly dark; he looked back: Joseph, Elijah, Salang, Rijin, the Shi brothers, He Siniang holding up Lingbao, Xiaoman and Ayi hiding behind He Siniang—each of them looking at him. He said: eat first. He said: the three days' fast is done, the dispatch has come; from tonight we'll need our strength; on empty stomachs we can't work; eat first, and when we've eaten, talk.

He Siniang went to the stove and boiled a pot of gruel and baked a stack of sesame cakes; Old Mother Kang worked the bellows by the stove; Rijin went home to the south end of the ward and carried back a sack of rice, saying his Ma had saved it up these years. They sat under the colonnade of the back courtyard holding their bowls and drank the gruel very slowly; after three days without food their stomachs could hold little. Lingbao drank half a bowl and put it down, saying he was full. Xiaoman ate a whole sesame cake; Ayi ate two, and when she had eaten them reached for another, held it in her hand, did not eat it, and tucked it into her robe.

They talked it over before the stele, with two lamps lit, one set at the stele's foot and one hung in the jujube tree. Jingfu said he had thought it over: the stele couldn't be hauled away, couldn't be hidden, couldn't be held; the dispatch said the leader to dispose of as he judges fit; the disposal he had in mind was to bury it. He said: the stele is here, in the monastery, in the place it has stood sixty-four years; dig a pit, lay it down, put it in, bury it with earth, level it, and leave nothing on top. He said when the Daoist Lu comes with the county's document to haul it away, what he's hauling is a stele standing up; if there's no stele, what will he haul?

Lingbao, sitting under the jujube tree, spoke at this. He said no. He said: that year the Daoist Lu came to measure the land, Jingfu wanted to cover the stele with a cloth, and he wouldn't allow it; he said covering it would be the monastery itself having a guilty conscience; now Jingfu wants to bury it, and burying is more thorough than covering; covering is hanging a cloth; burying is taking the stele out from under heaven; those characters Pa stood before the stele and read for a whole watch—from now on, no one will see them. He said: if the stele stands here and the Daoist Lu hauls it off to pave a canal bank, it's been spoiled by others; but what's been spoiled is a thing the Tang's own Sons of Heaven wrote and bestowed; later, someone walking by the Yong'an canal treads on that stone, turns it over, sees the characters, and knows there was once such a monastery in Chang'an; bury it, and there's nothing at all.

Jingfu did not argue with him. He squatted before Lingbao and said: Uncle Lingbao, you're right; covering is a guilty conscience, and burying is a guilty conscience too; he admits it. He said: but covering was fear of people seeing; burying is fear of people spoiling it; that year he wanted to cover it because he feared the Daoist Lu would take a sentence from the stele to accuse us in the palace; what he feared was the monastery's people being punished; what he wanted to protect was people; today he wants to bury it, and the people can't be protected any more; tomorrow the monastery is confiscated; what he wants to protect is the stele. He said: laid under a canal bank, soaked by water, buried in mud, ground by ox-carts—a few years of grinding and the characters are gone; buried in the monastery, lying on sand, covered with felt, the characters stay whole. He said he didn't know whether anyone would ever dig it up; he knew only that buried, the characters remain; hauled away, the characters are gone.

Lingbao sat there with his hand on his knee, fingers tapping. Joseph beside him said: Great Worthy, what Jingfu says—these are two kinds of guilty conscience. Lingbao said: two kinds of guilty conscience; he could hear it. He said he couldn't see the stele any more; he had touched it all his life, and from now on couldn't touch it; he couldn't bear to part with it; those reasons he gave were the reasons of not being able to bear it. He said: bury it; when you bury it, have him beside it; he'll read it to the stele.

Salang asked where to bury it. Jingfu said: right beside the stele, the patch to the west of it; the ground is level, no tree roots; dig down, dig longer than the stele, wider than the stele, deeper than the stele is thick, deep enough that with the stele in, three feet of earth can still go on top. He said: lay the stele down, lying flat, characters up; sand underneath, felt on top, then earth; stone in the earth doesn't rot.

Joseph asked: and the base? The stele stood on a stone base, a single block of bluestone, the stele's foot set in a groove in the base; the base's southeast corner was missing a piece, smashed with a hammer in the Yuanhe years by that lame soldier from the south of the city; the broken piece Master hadn't thrown away but kept in the scripture cabinet, more than thirty years now. Jingfu said: bury the base too; first lift the stele out, then lift the base out; put the base at the stele's foot, buried together with the stele; and the missing corner—take that broken stone out of the scripture cabinet and set it back.

Rijin asked whether there were enough people. The stele was nearly ten feet high, thousands of jin; the base too was over a thousand jin; of the monastery's people who could put out strength—Salang, the Shi brothers, Rijin himself, Jingfu—five or six; Joseph and Elijah were past fifty and counted as half. Jingfu said not enough; find more. He said: find people of the ward; but if the inspectorate learned of it, if the county learned of it, the ward's people would answer for it; he wouldn't call anyone; those willing to come, let them come.

That night no one went to call anyone, and people came of themselves. First Old Mother Kang went back to the south end of the ward and told the households of old believers there; in the second watch four young men came from the south end of the ward, sons and grandsons of those households, carrying spades, carrying picks, one carrying a coil of hemp rope. Then came two helpers the Shi Second had had in his corpse-cart days, from the east end of the ward, unbelievers, who came too, saying: they'd pushed the cart a dozen-odd years, and the monastery had given gruel a dozen-odd years; tonight they'd come to pay back a few spades of earth. In the third watch someone came down off the back courtyard wall, landed, fell, and picked himself up—it was Qushen, with a coil of thick rope on his back, twice as thick as the south-end coil, used at the temple for hoisting beams when the hall was built; he said he'd taken it from the temple storeroom; he must return it before light; when they were done he'd carry it back.

In the fourth watch someone knocked at the monastery gate; Salang went to open it, and outside stood Lü Shao, changed into a short coat, a lantern in his hand; he said today in the office he heard the inspectorate had sent the Daqin Monastery a dispatch deferring three days, and guessed the rest; he said he couldn't dig, but he could write; he'd come to watch; later, if anyone asked, he'd remember.

The digging of the pit began that night. Lingbao sat under the jujube tree; He Siniang moved a low stool for him and put a lined robe over his shoulders. He could not see; he could hear the sound of spades driving into the earth, picks striking stone, men panting; someone swore under his breath, and someone laughed. He sat and listened to a few spadefuls, and then spoke, reciting in Syriac; what he recited was the text of the stele, from the head of the stele.

Joseph stopped his spade, listened a few lines, and said: the Great Worthy is reciting the stele. Lingbao did not stop, but recited on; when he had recited the Syriac passage he recited in Tang, the stele's first sentence: Behold: the Eternal, true and still, before all firsts and Himself without beginning; the Unfathomable, spirit and void, after all lasts and wondrously enduring. He recited very slowly, sentence by sentence, and after each sentence paused, then recited the next. He said the day Pa raised the stele, he stood before it and read it from beginning to end, read it a whole watch; he was fourteen that year, standing beside Pa; today he couldn't see the stele, but he knew it by heart; he'd recite, and you dig; by the time he'd finished reciting, you'd be about done digging.

So he recited all night. When he reached The true and constant Way is wondrous and hard to name; its works shine so plainly that we are forced to call it the Luminous Teaching, the pit was waist-deep. When he reached Taizong, reached Alopen, reached The office concerned at once built a Daqin monastery in the Yining Ward of the capital and ordained twenty-one monks, he paused and said: this sentence—all these years they'd counted it every day. When he reached Gaozong, Xuanzong, Suzong, Daizong, reached the Jianzhong emperor, Sage, Divine, Cultured and Martial, reached the great benefactor, Grand Master of Splendid Happiness with Gold and Purple, Vice-Commissioner of Shuofang, acting Director of the Palace Administration, granted the purple kasaya, the monk Yisi, his voice went hoarse a moment, and he recited on; when he reached among the white-robed Luminous scholars, now we see the man, he stopped, sat there, drank a mouthful of the water He Siniang handed him, and when he had drunk recited on.

When the sky was nearly light, the pit was a man deep. Qushen climbed back over the wall to the temple; before he went, he left the coil of thick rope behind, saying the temple wasn't short of this rope; there was more in the storeroom; he'd go back and say it was lost; a coil of rope lost, and the Daoist Lu wouldn't look into it.

The second day, the monastery gate stayed shut. The inspectorate's notice was still pasted on the gate—not to be moved privately, not to be sold privately—and inside the gate people were digging a pit. The ward's people passing the gate heard spades inside; some stopped and peered through the crack; some didn't look, and hurried by. Headman Liu passed the gate twice; the second time he stood at the gate and knocked; Salang opened a crack; Headman Liu looked in once, saw the pit in the courtyard, saw the earth heaped beside the pit, saw the stele still standing, and told Salang: if the ward's people ask, he'll say the monastery is putting its things in order; the inspectorate's dispatch tells the leader to dispose of them; what he sees is disposal, and that's what he'll say; he said too: if the inspectorate's men come and ask him what the monastery dug, he'll tell it as it is; he won't hide it for you; your own affairs, you answer for yourselves. When he had said it, he left.

By day they dug on, dug till the sun slanted west, and the pit was done: a foot longer than the stele, a foot wider than the stele, so deep that a man standing on its floor with his arm stretched up could not reach its rim. The Shi Second jumped in and trod the floor of the pit level; Ayi and Xiaoman carried buckets, drew water from the well and poured it on the pit floor; the water soaked down; the ground was trodden firm; Rijin pushed the corpse cart and brought three cartloads of sand from the sand heap at the south end of the ward, tipped them into the pit, and spread them level, half a foot thick.

Then the lifting of the stele. The stele's foot was set in the groove in the base, and the groove had been filled with lime mortar; sixty-four years, and the lime mortar was as hard as stone. Salang and Rijin took chisels and chiselled along the seam of the groove bit by bit, chiselled more than a watch, and loosened it. They sank two thick wooden posts to the east of the stele and tied to the posts the coil of thick rope Qushen had left; the rope's other end went round the stele's head and was looped beneath the cross on the head; the young men from the south end hauled on that rope, and the Shi brothers on another; Jingfu stood to the west of the stele steadying its body; he called once, they hauled once, the stele leaned a little to the east; he called again, they hauled again, and the stele's foot came out of the groove inch by inch.

It came out; the stele hung, its foot resting on the base's rim, several ropes holding its head from the east, seven or eight men hauling, letting it down slowly to the west; the head tipped westward toward the pit's mouth; the ropes slackened bit by bit; the head went into the pit first and sank down bit by bit, the foot still resting on the pit's rim. Jingfu jumped into the pit and stood on the sand, his hands up to hold the stele's head; the head came down on the sand and he dodged aside; those above prised the stele's foot with wooden levers, prised it off the rim, and the foot slid down the pit wall and landed with a thud on the sand, and the sand splashed up, splashed all over Jingfu's face. The stele lay down, characters up, the cross on its head toward the east.

Everyone in the courtyard stood at the pit's edge, looking down. The stele lay on the pit floor, smaller than when it stood, greyish white, line after line of characters on it lit by the daylight, the strokes visible, the two dragons at the head visible, the row of Syriac letters at the foot visible—cut by the Great Worthy Yisi.

Jingfu climbed out of the pit, his face covered in sand. He said: the base. The base was harder than the stele. The base was one block, with no groove, no seam; they could only dig, dig out the earth beneath the base, hollow out under it, prise it with wooden levers, prise it up a little, push in a roller, prise again, push again; after a watch of prising the base shifted; they rolled it to the pit's edge; several men pushed together; the base turned over once and slid down the pit wall, and landed at the stele's foot with a boom, and the sand on the pit floor jumped up in a layer. When the base came down, the gap at its southeast corner was facing up.

Jingfu went back to the scripture room, took out that cloth packet from the lowest shelf of the scripture cabinet, and opened it; inside was the broken stone, the size of a fist, flat on one face, jagged on the other, the white mark of the hammer still visible on the jagged face. He took the stone, jumped into the pit, went to the base, and pressed the stone into the gap, break against break, fitting seamlessly; he pressed it, pressed once more, and the stone held; it was set back, as if it had never fallen.

Joseph, standing at the pit's edge, watched him and said: Master didn't mend it back then; he said the broken stone goes into the scripture cabinet, not mended. Jingfu said: Master didn't mend it because the stele was still standing; the stele standing, missing a corner, people would see it and know this stele had been smashed, and smashed still stood; now the stele is lying down and is to be buried; buried in the earth, no one will see it; the missing corner—mend it, and the stele is whole.

The felt was laid by Ayi and Xiaoman. He Siniang carried two felts from her house at the south end of the ward, the ones laid down the year she married Lingbao; white when new, grey after fifteen years. Xiaoman and Ayi each carried one, went down the ladder Jingfu had set up from the pit's edge, went to the stele, spread out the felts, and laid them from the stele's head to its foot, Xiaoman laying the head half and Ayi the foot half; the two children squatted on the stele and smoothed the felt down bit by bit. When Ayi reached the foot she saw that row of Syriac letters, reached out and touched it, and asked Jingfu what these letters were. Jingfu said they were the Patriarchate's letters. Ayi said they looked like Uighur letters. Jingfu said the Uighur letters had been learned from this side. Ayi touched them once more, and covered them with the felt.

Lingbao was the last to go down into the pit. He had He Siniang and Rijin help him down the ladder rung by rung to the pit floor; he stood on the sand; he could not see, and reached out to feel, felt the felt, felt the stele beneath the felt. He lifted a corner of the felt, the corner at the stele's foot, slipped his hand in, felt the face of the stele, felt that row of Syriac letters, felt them one by one; at one place he stopped—that was Pa's name, Yazdbozid, cut by Pa's own hand. His hand stayed on those letters, stayed and stayed; then he let the felt down, smoothed it, and told Rijin: help him up.

The earth went in from dusk till midnight. A dozen-odd people, a dozen-odd spades, shovelling into the pit spade by spade; first filling the two sides of the stele, then over the stele; the earth fell on the felt, thump, thump, and after a few dozen spadefuls there was no more sound; the felt was covered and could no longer be heard. Lü Shao stood at the pit's edge holding his lantern, lighting the pit; he could not dig; he gave light; when someone called to him to shine it this way, he shone it this way; when someone called that way, that way.

Halfway through the filling, the young man from the south end who had brought the rope stopped and asked Jingfu: Leader, will it ever be dug up again? Jingfu said he didn't know. The young man said: and if it's never dug up? Jingfu said: then it stays here for ever. He said: what's written on the stele is this monastery's story, Chang'an's story, a thing Sons of Heaven bestowed and wrote—eighteen hundred characters; the characters are cut in stone, the stone is buried in earth; the characters won't run off; one day someone will dig it up and read it and know that Chang'an once had such a monastery and such people, and that's enough. The young man thought, and went on filling.

They filled it full, filled it level, filled it to the height of the ground beside. Salang and Rijin took wooden rammers and rammed it stroke by stroke till it was firm; the Shi brothers carried a few old bricks from the back courtyard and laid them on top, then a layer of loose earth, and swept it level. The place where the stele had stood was empty, only a square mark left, the earth round the mark darker in colour; Jingfu had someone sweep it with a broom several times, and swept it even.

At midnight the wind rose again. It came from the west and blew harder and harder, blew the jujube tree's branches wildly, blew out the lanterns under the colonnade one by one, blew the loose earth of the courtyard into the air and into people's eyes. Jingfu sent everyone indoors and went himself to the gate to see to the lamp. The lamp at the gate had been lit at dusk by Rijin; Jingfu had been in the pit that day and had had no time, and Rijin lit it for him. He went to the gate; the lamp was on the stone block, its flame bent over in the wind, bent nearly to the rim of the lamp-dish; Salang sat beside the lamp; the bamboo-and-paper shade that covered the lamp had long been snatched away by the wind and had rolled into the ward street; he had his two hands cupped, shielding the lamp's windward side. Jingfu went over and shielded it with him. The wind blew and dropped, blew and dropped; when it blew, the flame bent down, bent till it nearly went out; when the wind dropped, the flame stood up again. The two of them squatted before the stone block, four hands round that lamp, and shielded it till the sky was nearly light and the wind died down.

At daybreak it rained; that day was the seventeenth of the eighth month. The rain was not heavy, fine and thin, falling in the courtyard, falling on the patch of ground where the stele had stood, falling on the loose earth swept anew; the loose earth was wetted and changed colour, the same colour as the ground beside. Last night's many footprints, spade marks, rammer marks were softened one by one by the rain and smoothed away.

Morning devotion was stood in the rain. Jingfu stood in the middle of the courtyard, east of the place where the stele had stood, and when the sky greyed looked back and counted: nineteen, not one fewer. Lingbao stood under the jujube tree; he hadn't gone back to the south end of the ward last night, had lain two watches in the scripture room, and before light had come out again.

Lu Shouchong came that afternoon, two days earlier than he had told the abbot. Qushen sent word afterwards through Salang that early that day the Daoist Lu had heard from the county's people that the Commissioners of Merit's office had deferred the Daqin Monastery three days and told the leader to dispose of the stele as he judged fit; he struck the table in the temple and said: deferring three days is leaving the hu monastery time to move the stele; we can't wait for the inventory; that same day he went to the county to beg the county sheriff, and dragged the sheriff along. He brought the county's men—a sheriff and four or five county clerks—and a dozen-odd people of the Tongzhen Temple carrying ropes, carrying rollers, driving a big cart yoked to two oxen. He stood at the monastery gate and called for it to be opened. Salang opened the gate; Lu Shouchong walked in first, walked to the front courtyard, and stopped.

He saw the empty ground. He looked back at the sheriff, and the sheriff too was looking at the empty ground. Lu Shouchong asked Jingfu: the stele? Jingfu stood before him and said: buried. Lu Shouchong said: buried? He said: you've some nerve; the inspectorate's notice is pasted on the gate—the articles within the monastery not to be moved privately; you've buried the stele; that's stealing government property; he turned and said to the sheriff: Shaofu, this is defiance of orders. The sheriff was a man of forty-odd in a blue robe; he looked at Jingfu. Jingfu took the dispatch from his robe and handed it to the sheriff, and said: a dispatch from the Commissioners of Merit's office, arrived the evening before last; the dispatch says the scriptures, images, articles and stele within the monastery: the leader to dispose of them as he judges fit; let them not be destroyed, let them not be sold privately. He said he was the leader; he judged, he disposed; the stele was not destroyed, not sold; it was buried.

The sheriff took the dispatch and read it, came to the office's seal at the end, came to the mark beside the seal, read it again, and handed it back to Jingfu. Lu Shouchong beside them said: dispose of as he judges fit means for him to hand the stele over, not to bury it. The sheriff said: the dispatch doesn't say to whom. Lu Shouchong said: the county's document says: to the Tongzhen Temple, for the embankment of the Yong'an canal. The sheriff said: the county's document is the county's; the office's dispatch is the office's; the articles and stele of the monastery are managed by the Commissioners of Merit's office; the office's dispatch came first; the dispatch tells the leader to dispose of them as he judges fit; the leader has disposed of them; the county's document doesn't reach this far.

Lu Shouchong's face changed, and he said: then dig it up and haul it away by the county's document. The sheriff ignored him and turned to ask Jingfu: Leader, where is the stele buried? Jingfu stood before him and said: buried in the monastery. The sheriff asked: which part of the monastery? Jingfu said that he wouldn't say. He said: Shaofu asked where the stele is, and he said: buried; Shaofu asked where it's buried, and he said: in the monastery; if Shaofu asks further under which patch of ground, he won't say; he doesn't lie, and he won't say; if Shaofu wants to arrest him, he'll go with Shaofu. He said: if Shaofu wants to dig, the monastery's ground is all here, front, back, left and right; tomorrow the monastery is inventoried, and after that it's government land; whether to dig or not is the government's affair.

The sheriff looked at him, looked at the empty ground, looked at the stretch of loose earth in the courtyard soaked by the rain; the loose earth was level, one colour; nowhere could it be seen to have been dug. He did not have Jingfu arrested, and did not have anyone dig. Lu Shouchong said: Shaofu, you'll just stand and watch? The sheriff said he had come to watch; the county sent him to watch the Daqin Monastery hand over the stele; the stele is gone; the dispatch says the leader has disposed of it; he'll record it as it is. He had a county clerk behind him take out a register and a brush, and said: record: one stone stele of the Daqin Monastery of Yining Ward, disposed of by the leader Jingfu in accordance with the dispatch of the Commissioners of Merit's office: buried. When he had said it, he had the clerk add after it the phrase as he judged fit. The clerk wrote: buried, as he judged fit.

The sheriff put his mark on the register and said to Lu Shouchong: Daoist, the county is done with the stele. Lu Shouchong stood at the edge of the empty ground, and the dozen-odd temple people behind him, carrying ropes, carrying rollers, looked at him. He said he would go and speak to the Commissioners of Merit's office. The sheriff said: let the Daoist ask the office. Lu Shouchong turned and walked out of the gate; his people drove the empty cart after him, the oxen's hooves sinking in the mud of the ward street step by step; the stack of rollers piled at the foot of the wall they did not take, and left it there. The sheriff had the clerk put away the register, and left too.

That evening Jingfu went to the gate to light the lamp. Rijin followed behind him, watching him tip out last night's dregs, wipe the lamp-dish clean with a cloth, ladle in a spoon of new oil, draw out a little length of wick, trim it even, strike the flint, light it, and set it back on the stone block. When Jingfu had lit it, he stood up and looked back at the courtyard. The empty ground in the courtyard, rained on all day and then blown by the evening wind, had dried a skin on top, the same as anywhere else in the courtyard. He looked a while, turned, and shut the monastery gate.
