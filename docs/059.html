Volume Nine　The Translation Hall

Chapter 59　Li Su

In the spring of the seventh year of Yuanhe, Jingjing began to translate the Sutra of Profound Peace and Joy. Of the Syriac scriptures brought from the Patriarchate it was the hardest. It told of Mishihe answering questions from his great disciple Simon: Simon asked how a man could attain peace and joy, and Mishihe answered at great length, saying a man must put away desire, keep stillness, not contend, not cling to being, and not cling to non-being either. Elder Babai had read it through to him once in his day, and when he had read it said, "This one, you translate when you're old." He was in his thirties then, and didn't understand why Babai said so. Now he was sixty-six. He opened it and read it through and understood: for what this scripture said, the Tang books already had ready-made words; in the Daoist books they were waiting, sentence by sentence. If he used those words, a Tang reader would understand his translation at once, and having understood, take it for just another Daoist scripture. If he did not use them, a Tang reader would not understand one sentence.

Lingbao held the brush for him. Lingbao was forty-five that year. David had laid hands on him and he was a priest; the monastery's accounts and the key to the scripture cabinet were in his hands; but the brush in the scripture room he still meant to hold. The two brothers sat facing each other across a long table. Jingjing read a sentence of Syriac, thought, and said a sentence in Tang; Lingbao wrote it down, and when it was written read it back to him; he listened and checked it against the Syriac; what matched stayed, and what did not they thought over again. It was the method of walking back from Alopen's book. He had used it more than twenty years.

At the line without desire, without action Jingjing stopped, and Lingbao's brush hung waiting for him. He thought for as long as a meal takes, and said, "Write it. Wuyu, wuwei." Lingbao wrote it and put a dot beside it with the brush. At Those who can be at peace and joy must first put away desire, another dot. At Like a mirror reflecting things: the thing goes, the image is gone, another dot. When a page was done, Lingbao counted: a hundred-odd characters, and eleven dots beside them.

"Brother, in this one there are more dots than characters."

"Dot them," said Jingjing. "Dot them, and those who come after will see and know this place was borrowed. If they can find words of their own, they'll change them; if they can't, they'll live here for now."

The character he wrote most on his board that year was quan, the fish trap. The line on the stele he had written himself: Its words are not prolix; its reasoning, once grasped, can do without the trap—praising, as Taizong's edict did, Alopen's teaching: the words not many, the reasoning reached, and then the trap can be forgotten. The quan is a bamboo basket for catching fish; once the fish is caught, the basket can be thrown away. When he wrote it he took it for just a fine phrase. Now every morning he faced the character and thought: all his life, the fish not yet caught, and the basket borrowed. Throw away the borrowed basket, and there would be nothing at all.

In those years Marya went first. He had preached his voice hoarse and been hoarse forty years, and before he died his voice suddenly cleared, and lying in bed he sang a hymn, the one in Tang, In the highest heavens, deep reverence and praise; the great earth holds the thought of peace for all. When he had sung it he said to Jingjing, "This one you translated and I examined. When I examined you, you were only twenty. That day I thought: this child's Tang is better than my Syriac."

The last was Sabrisho.

He was nearly ninety. He lay in bed remembering nothing—not people, not things, not his own name—remembering only the rules, reciting them one by one: the rules of the offering once in seven, the rules of baptism, the rules of deliberation. Jingjing went every day and sat a while. Sabrisho recited and Jingjing listened, and when he got a rule wrong, Jingjing did not correct him.

The day Jingjing went, he had come to the offering once in seven. "The bread is to be broken in the hall," he said, eyes closed, "eaten in the hall, not carried out of the hall door… the one who breaks the bread, if there is a bishop… if there is no bishop, a priest…" Here he knitted his brows; the rule after that he could not remember at all.

"Elder," Jingjing called.

Sabrisho opened his eyes and looked at him, his eyes clouded; whether he knew him or not, there was no telling. He looked a good while and said, "You break it."

Jingjing thought he had recognized him, and called again, Elder. Sabrisho still looked at him, and still said those three words: "You break it."

In the winter of the first year of Yuanhe, when the people of the south of the city laid bare Jingjing's birth at the monastery gate, that day in the hall Sabrisho had taken the basket of offering bread from his hands and pushed it back into them, and what he said was these three words.

Jingjing took from his breast a small piece of the offering bread, left over in the hall that morning, broke off half, and put it in his mouth. Sabrisho held it in his mouth, held it, and closed his eyes. He had kept the rules all his life and argued with Jingjing thirty years; he had forgotten everything, and the last thing he remembered was who ought to break this bread.

Of the seven who had come east from Ctesiphon in those days, by the tenth year of Yuanhe not one was left.

In the eleventh year of Yuanhe, Jingjing began to write a list of the scriptures. The scriptures brought from the Patriarchate, in Syriac, came to five hundred and thirty in all, piled in the scripture room in a few cases of beipi, bark-skin. The cases Alopen had brought from the west long ago: the leather had stiffened, the brass clasps rusted, and when you opened them they gave a squeak. How many he had translated in his life he had never counted himself; Lingbao counted for him, and it was thirty. So, by Lingbao's count, he wrote them one by one: the scripture's name, its Tang name, its number of rolls. When he reached the end he stopped, thought, and wrote a line after it: The scriptures of the original religion of Daqin number five hundred and thirty in all, all in palm-leaf Sanskrit sounds; Jingjing has translated the above thirty; the great remainder lies in the bark-skin cases, not yet translated.

Lingbao, beside him, looked at the line. "Brother—five hundred, and thirty."

"Thirty, and five hundred."

"The five hundred after—who will translate them?"

"You will. When you can't finish, Jingfu will; when Jingfu can't finish, Jingfu's disciples will."

Lingbao looked at those bark-skin cases and said nothing more. Jingfu was twenty-one that year. He was sitting at the door teaching a few of the ward's children Tang characters—water, fire, gate, road—using the board Jingjing had once whittled for him.

Those mornings Jingjing wrote the character yu, remainder, and read from Ecclesiastes: What profit hath a man of all his labour which he taketh under the sun?

In the autumn of the twelfth year of Yuanhe, the scripture room was sealed.

It began in the palace. The Son of Heaven these years loved the immortals and sought long life; many Daoists went in and out of the palace, and one surnamed Wang, out of the Zhongnan Mountains, who said he could make the golden elixir, had found favour. In the seventh month he put a word in before the throne: in the city of Chang'an there was a hu temple whose hu monks had copied out the scriptures of Lord Lao, changed their heads and faces, and written them up as their own scriptures; their scriptures spoke of non-action, of peace and joy, of putting away desire and keeping stillness, every word and sentence the Daoists' own words, yet they said they came from Daqin; this was stealing Lord Lao's Way, and he begged for an edict to search out and forbid them. The Son of Heaven neither said yes nor said no; he said only to have the office concerned go and take a look.

Early in the eighth month an administrative assistant came from the Commissioners of Merit's office with six men, came in at the monastery gate and went straight to the scripture room, carried out the Tang scriptures cabinet by cabinet, heaped them in the courtyard, searched them half the day, and turned up that roll of the Sutra of Profound Peace and Joy. The assistant held it and read out a few lines: "Without desire, without action; able to be clear, able to be still." When he had read them he looked at Jingjing. "Master Jing, these lines—are they Lord Lao's words, or your Lord's?"

"They're our Lord's words, using Lord Lao's characters."

"Using Lord Lao's characters makes them Lord Lao's words." The assistant had the scriptures all put back in the cabinets, pasted seals on the cabinet doors, and pasted a seal on the door of the scripture room too. "We'll see when the palace's decree comes down."

That night no one at the monastery slept; they sat at the scripture-room door. Lingbao said he would go and find people. "There are still a few names in Official Pei's book. General Luo's son is in the Shence Army, and the Mi grandson too."

"Find them," said Jingjing. "One at a time."

He himself did not go to find anyone. He sat at the scripture-room door all night, and toward dawn rose and went to the middle of the courtyard and stood telling his knots. When he had told them, he couldn't get into the scripture room. The scripture he knew by heart, so he stood in the courtyard and recited it; he recited I will lift up mine eyes unto the hills. When he had recited it there was no board—the board was locked in the scripture room—so with his finger he wrote the character hill in the frost on the well curb, and when it was written, the frost melted, and the character was gone.

Half a month later the assistant came again. "The palace wants the monastery to make it clear itself: which are your words and which are Lord Lao's."

"The scripture room is sealed. I can't get in," said Jingjing. "If you want to see, Assistant, please take off the seals, and I'll bring them out to you roll by roll."

The assistant had the seals taken off. Jingjing carried the scriptures out roll by roll, spread them on the long table in the courtyard, turned to every page, and pointed to the little dots beside the characters. "Everything with a dot is a borrowed word—borrowed from the Buddhists, from the Daoists, from the Confucians. I've been putting dots thirty years, and not missed one. What has no dot is our own word: Aluohe, Mishihe, the Luminous Honoured One, the Three-in-One, the Pure Wind—these words are not in Lord Lao's scriptures. I've never said a borrowed word was our own. When I've borrowed, I've recorded that it was borrowed. Borrowing words isn't stealing. A thief doesn't put dots."

The assistant held the roll of the Sutra of Profound Peace and Joy, looked at the eleven little dots on that page, and turned the pages over one by one, and at last raised his head and asked, "These dots of yours—what are they for?"

"They're marks. So that those who come after will know this place is borrowed. When they find words of their own, they'll change them."

The assistant asked nothing more. He had the scriptures put back in the cabinets, did not paste the seals on again, and said he would go back and report.

Only after the scripture room was unsealed did Lingbao tell him that a few days before he had gone to the Astronomical Bureau. The people there said Lord Li was ill, but having heard of this, he had sent a word into the palace: "This autumn Mars trespasses on the Heart lodge; by the signs of the stars it is not fitting to destroy scriptures and books; if they are destroyed, it portends sickness in the palace." Lingbao said, "Whether that word reached the Son of Heaven, I don't know. I only know that the people at the Astronomical Bureau said when Lord Li sent it he could no longer get up, and had his son hold him up to write it."

The night the scripture room was unsealed, the two brothers quarrelled for the first time, there in the scripture room.

"Brother, this time we've dodged it, but it'll come again," said Lingbao. "That Daoist is still in the palace. It didn't come off this year; he'll speak again next year. From today, borrowed words—let's not borrow them any more. Aluohe, write Aluohe; wuwei, don't write wuwei, write the sound. If the Tang can't understand it, they can't understand it. At least those who can't understand it won't say we stole Lord Lao's things."

"The scriptures speak to those who have come in at the gate," said Jingjing. "Those who've come in at the gate have someone to teach them; they can read even transliteration. But those outside the gate read once in their lives, and if they can't understand one word, they'll never come in at the gate all their lives."

"And if those outside read and understand, and understand it as Lord Lao, then the gate they come in at is Lord Lao's, not ours."

Jingjing could not answer. After a few breaths he said, "This is how I've translated all my life. I can't change now."

"Then I'll change."

The two brothers sat facing that lamp and neither looked at the other. At last Jingjing said, "Then you change. The remaining rolls of the Sutra of Profound Peace and Joy, translate by your method—transliteration, no borrowed words. The rolls before, by my method—keep them. Put both in the scripture room. Those who come after can read whichever they understand."

Lingbao agreed. That night, on the board he had got back after half a month locked away, Jingjing wrote the character liang, two, and when he had written it thought: this character is borrowed too—borrowed from Lingbao.

In the winter of the twelfth year of Yuanhe, Li Su fell ill. He was seventy-four that year. He had spent his whole life at the Astronomical Bureau and risen to Director of it, and the names of all his sons had the character jing in them; when he named them he had said to Jingjing, "That character on your stele is a good one. I'm borrowing it." He lay at home, the room still piled with books—Tang, Persian, Indian—leaving only a crack to walk through, and the bed set up at the end of that crack.

Jingjing went to see him, leaning on his stick, and squeezed sideways in between the piles of books to the bedside. Li Su saw him and smiled. "You've grown old too, and you can still squeeze in."

Jingjing told him about the scripture room and thanked him for the word he had sent. Li Su waved his hand. "Mars trespassing on the Heart is true. This autumn Mars really did go along beside the Heart lodge. I didn't make it up. I only took a true word and picked a day to say it."

He rested a while and went on: "These days I can't sleep. At night I lie with my eyes open. There's no window in the room and I can't see the sky, so I look in my heart: where the Dipper is, where the Purple Palace is, where the Wolf Star is—one by one they're all there, the same as forty years ago when I watched them in Guangzhou, the same as that year I sat on the roof of your monastery and watched with Elder Yonan. That day Elder Yonan said to me, watched from here or from anywhere else, they're the same stars. I didn't understand then: a Persian runs off to Chang'an to watch the stars—is he watching Persian stars or Chang'an stars? Later I understood. The stars don't divide; it's the people watching them who divide. Forty years I read the auspicious and inauspicious, the portents and calamities, for the Son of Heaven at the Astronomical Bureau, and only when I was old did I know that all that auspicious and inauspicious is men's business. The stars themselves don't care at all."

"It's the same with me," said Jingjing. "All my life I've borrowed words, thousands of them, and put thousands of dots. Only now I'm old do I know that when the Tang read, they don't care at all whether a word is borrowed or our own. If they understand, they understand; if they don't, they don't."

"Do you regret it, then?"

"No. Only sometimes I wonder, if Elder Alopen in his day hadn't borrowed that Heavenly Worthy, what he would have borrowed."

"Something else," said Li Su. "Whatever you borrow is borrowed. Your Pa said it: living in someone else's house."

They both laughed.

Li Su died in the twelfth month of that year. On the morning someone came from the Astronomical Bureau with the news, Jingjing wrote on his board the character xing, star, and read: The heavens declare the glory of God; and the firmament sheweth his handywork… There is no speech nor language, where their voice is not heard.

On the day of the funeral the palace sent people to condole, some in purple, some in scarlet, most of whom Jingjing did not know. He waited at the back of the crowd while they went in and came out group after group, and only then went in, stood a moment before the spirit seat, made the sign of the cross, turned, and came out.

On the way back Jingfu held his arm. When they had walked a stretch Jingjing said, "Of those my age, there's only me left."

A few steps further he said, "Siyuan doesn't count. Siyuan's younger than me."

"Brother Siyuan is nearly fifty too," said Jingfu.

"Still younger than me."

That night he sat in the scripture room until the second watch, and when he came out looked up at the sky. There were clouds, and not a single star could be seen.
