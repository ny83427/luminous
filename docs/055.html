Volume Ten　Huichang

Chapter 55　Three Generations

In the third month of the fourth year of Kaicheng, the willows of Chang'an turned green.

That year, Jingjing had been dead twenty-one years.

There were still old people in the Yining Ward who remembered him. When the old people spoke of Jingjing, they always said first, "the one who wrote the stele," and then, "the one with the bad stomach," and only then anything else. And the anything else they could not remember clearly. Twenty years after a man dies, that is probably about all that is left of him in other people's mouths: those two things.

The Daqin monastery was still in its old place in the Yining Ward. The gate was the same gate; the courtyard wall had been patched twice; the flagstone at the gate had been trodden into a hollow in the middle, where a little pool collected on rainy days. Beside the flagstone hung a small lamp.

The lamp was lit every evening and put out only at daybreak.

The man who lit it was called Jingfu.

Jingfu was forty-four that year, and had been head of the Daqin monastery twenty-one years.

He was neither tall nor short, neither fat nor thin. His face—you could call it Tang, or you could call it hu. The flatbread sellers in the West Market spoke hu to him, the silk sellers in the East Market spoke Tang to him, and neither was wrong. He himself made no distinction. He had been led in at the monastery gate by his master at five or six, and of what came before he remembered little. He remembered only that the day was very cold, his master's hand was cold, and his master's sleeve smelled of ink.

Everyone at the monastery knew Jingfu had one gift his master had never had in all his life.

His stomach was good.

Cold rice he ate, leftover soup he drank, water fresh drawn from the well he picked up and gulped. Raw turnip he crunched, overnight flatbread hard enough to drive nails he soaked a little and got down. When he had eaten, he wiped his mouth and went about his business, and never once ran clutching his belly to the back courtyard.

While Jingjing lived he often watched him drink cold water, watched him and frowned, and did not like to say anything. Once he could not help himself and said: "That stomach of yours—the Lord gave it you as a separate gift."

Jingfu said: "The Master gave me a gift too."

"What did I give you?"

"Letters," said Jingfu.

Jingjing thought, nodded, and said no more. He drew his own bowl of hot soup a little closer.

In his lifetime Jingjing told him many things. Told of Lingzhou, of Xizhou, of Ctesiphon, of a one-armed uncle, of an old soldier with a sword scar on his face, who when he laughed moved only half his face. What he told of most was his pa, Yisi. The things Yisi had said, Jingjing told him one by one, and more than once.

One thing, Jingjing told only once. The year Jingfu was nine, he broke wind at the morning office, so loud it took a turn. At noon Jingjing called him into his room, poured two bowls of hot water, and said that the year he was ten, in Lingzhou, at the dinner table, he had done the same, with an outsider sitting at the table too. He had said it was shameful, and his pa had said: what shames a man is a lie, not wind.

Jingfu had remembered those words forty years and more.

After that time at nine, he had never had occasion to use them.

In twenty-one years, Chang'an had four Sons of Heaven.

Xianzong died in the palace, they said from taking elixirs. Muzong played polo, took a fright on the polo ground, had a stroke, and within two years was dead too. Jingzong went hunting foxes with eunuchs at night, and when the hunting was over drank wine, and when the wine was drunk was killed by the eunuchs. The people of the monastery heard all these things in the West Market, and having heard them, spoke of them a little at table, and having spoken, each ate his own food.

That time four years ago was different.

That winter something happened in the palace: the chief ministers meant to put the eunuchs to death, failed, and instead the eunuchs led the Shence Army out of the palace killing. The killing went on several days. There were men lying in the streets, some in purple, some in scarlet, some in plain cloth, who had worn nothing wrong, only gone out of doors that day. The gate of the Yining Ward was shut three days, and in those three days no one dared go into the streets.

The Daqin monastery shut its gate too.

In those three days, every evening as always Jingfu opened the gate a crack, put his hand out, lit the lamp at the gate, and closed the crack again. An Yan behind him said: what are you lighting a lamp for at a time like this—it draws attention. Jingfu said: it's always been lit.

On the fourth day, the ward gate opened. It took three days of sweeping before the streets were clean.

After that, people in Chang'an spoke a little more quietly. The Son of Heaven was still the Son of Heaven, sitting in the palace; it was said he often drank alone, and when he had drunk, wept. The people of the monastery kept the offering once in seven days as before, struck the wood as before, spoke at table as before of what they had heard in the West Market—only before speaking they would glance to see whether the door was shut.

The earliest riser at the monastery was An Yan.

An Yan was a Sogdian, born in Chang'an, grown up in Chang'an, sixty-eight that year. He was the monastery's old deacon, in charge of the lamp oil, the keys, and the striking of the wood.

Before it was light he was up. First he dressed in the dark, smoothed his grizzled beard with his hand, then went to the front courtyard, took the wooden board down from the gallery, and struck it with the mallet.

Bang, bang, bang.

One stroke after another, not hurried, not slow. The Daqin monastery used no bell; it used wood. When An Yan was little he had asked why they did not use a bell. The old deacon who taught him said: the bell is the Buddhists'. He asked again: then whose is the wood? The old deacon thought a long time and said: wood is everybody's.

When he had struck the board, he went to look at the stele.

The stele stood in the middle of the front courtyard, taller than two men, a blue-black stone, a cross carved at its head, clouds beneath the cross, a lotus beneath the clouds. The body of the stele was covered close with characters, Tang on the front, Syriac on the sides and at the bottom, curling and twisting like little worms.

Every morning An Yan wiped the stele with a cloth. Where he could reach, he stood and wiped; where he could not, he stood on a little stool. When he had wiped it, he folded the cloth and tucked it in his belt.

The year the stele was raised, he was ten.

He remembered that a great many people came that day, more than the courtyard could hold, so he climbed onto the top of the wall to watch. He saw an old man standing before the stele, not tall, very broad-shouldered, with a scar a little left of the middle of his brow, the skin over it puckered. The old man looked at the stele a long time, and said not a word. Someone said: that's the Great Virtue Yi, of Lord Director Guo's command.

Then the old man walked away from the stele to the foot of the wall, looked up and saw him. An Yan got a fright, thinking he would be scolded. But the old man took half a flatbread from his coat, reached it up to him, and said two words.

"Slowly."

He said it very slowly, biting each sound clear, as if afraid of biting it wrong.

An Yan took the bread and squatted on top of the wall and ate it all. To this day he had not worked out whether the old man meant eat slowly or come down slowly.

Three years later, the old man died. On the day of the funeral, among the men carrying the coffin was a monk-soldier tall as a tree, weeping like a child.

That monk-soldier's name was Sa.

Salang came to the monastery only after the sun was up.

He lived in a lane south of the West Market, and every day before light went to the West Market to carry goods; he carried until the hour of chen, and then, having a mealtime's break, ran over to carry water for the monastery.

Salang was thirty-two that year, very tall, so that he had to duck to come in at the monastery gate. His grandfather was called Sargis, who in those days had guarded the seven teachers on their way east, then stayed in Chang'an, and died in Chang'an. His father had been a monk-soldier too, married a Tang woman who sold curds in the West Market, and had him. By his generation the monastery could no longer afford to keep monk-soldiers. So he carried goods.

He carried goods well. Where others carried one bale, he carried two, and walked steadier than they did. The hu merchants of the West Market all liked to hire him, saying: this man is strong, says little, and doesn't steal.

On the wall of his room hung a wooden staff. The staff was shoulder-high, shod with iron at both ends, the iron worn shiny, the wood marked by the grip of hands with greasy band after band. It was his grandfather's. When his grandfather died he handed it to his father; when his father died he handed it to him.

When his father handed over the staff, he said only one thing.

"Hold, and there'll be no need to fight."

Salang asked: "Hold what?"

His father could no longer speak.

Salang thought later that it probably meant hold the gate. So every seventh day, on the day of worship, he took this staff and stood at the monastery gate. He did not stop anyone, nor drive anyone off; he only stood. Those who came to worship passed by him, and each looked up at him once. And he nodded to them.

He had stood there a dozen years and more, and never once used the staff.

When he had finished carrying water, he washed his face at the well and called to An Yan, who was wiping the stele: "Old An, I'm off."

An Yan did not turn round, but waved.

Salang called again towards the back courtyard: "Xiaoman, eaten yet?"

From the back courtyard a child's voice answered: "Yes!"

"What did you eat?"

"Master Jingfu's leftovers!"

Salang laughed, shouldered his carrying pole, and went.

Xiaoman was six that year.

Three summers before, Jingfu was coming back from outside the city, and outside the Jinguang Gate saw a child sitting in a dirt ditch by the road. The child looked about three, barefoot, his face all dirt, not crying, not speaking; when he saw someone coming he shrank into the ditch.

Jingfu stood at the edge of the ditch a while and asked him his name. The child did not say. Asked where his home was. The child did not say. Asked whether he was hungry. The child nodded.

So Jingfu carried him home.

That day was the day of Xiaoman, the Lesser Fullness of Grain. Jingfu thought all the way and could not think of a good name; when he came in at the monastery gate, An Yan asked whose child this was, and he said: "Xiaoman."

So he was called Xiaoman.

Taking in orphans at the monastery had once been Brother Siyuan's charge. Siyuan had had charge of it forty years, and died five winters ago; before he died he gave Jingfu a register in which names were recorded one by one—which household he was sent to as apprentice, which household fostered him, where he was buried. The last page was blank. The first name Jingfu wrote on the blank page was Xiaoman.

Xiaoman was a Tang child, black-haired, black-eyed, his nose flattish. In his first year at the monastery he did not say one word. In the second year, he began following An Yan to strike the board. In the third year he said everything, said it without stopping, and even talked in his sleep at night in whole runs.

Sometimes Jingfu looked at him and thought of himself. He thought: when the Master led me in at the gate, he probably looked at me like this.

In one thing only he was not like him.

Xiaoman's stomach was bad. One mouthful of something cold and it was upset.

Jingfu was much troubled. He did not know how to raise a child with a bad stomach. He himself had no such trouble; the Master had, but the Master was gone. He thought a long time, and remembered the Master saying that his pa in Lingzhou had always made him eat ginger.

So he planted a bed of ginger in the back courtyard.

That year, at the Daqin monastery's offering once in seven days, seventy or eighty came.

An Yan counted every time. He stood by the door of the hall, and for each one who came he made a note in his head. When he had counted, he told no one else, only Jingfu.

"Seventy-nine."

Jingfu nodded.

Seven days later An Yan said again: "Seventy-six."

Jingfu nodded again.

What An Yan did not say was that when he was little the hall could not hold them all and people had to stand out in the courtyard. How many there were then he had never counted. Then he did not yet know how to count such things.

The people in the hall were of every sort. There were Sogdian merchants in robes with turned-back collars, a string of little knives and flints at their belts. There were old men come from Persia, their beards white as wool, who chanted loudest. There were Tang people, the men in soft caps, the women with their hair piled high, sitting at the back, chanting along, and where they could not say a word right, slurring over it. There were a few of mixed Tang and hu blood, like Jingfu, who could be called anything at all.

At the end of the service they all sang a hymn together. It was the one Jingjing had translated in his day, whose first two lines were: The highest heavens all deeply revere and praise; the great earth longs again for peace and harmony. The old Persians sang it to the Syriac tune, the Tang people sang the Tang words, and the two did not fit well, one fast, one slow, and by the end, they fitted.

An Yan sang loudest. His voice had long since gone hoarse, and on the high notes it cracked, and when it cracked he did not stop, but went on up.

Xiaoman sat at his feet, and at the third line fell asleep against his leg.

Jingfu stood at the front looking at these people below him. He saw the white beards, fewer every year. He saw the black heads, a few more, but more very slowly. He saw Salang's shadow at the door, tall, blocking half the doorway, keeping out the sun.

He thought of when the Master was alive, and he too sat at someone's feet and fell asleep singing. When he woke the people in the hall had gone, and the Master was sitting beside him writing on a wooden board. He asked the Master what he was writing. The Master said: just writing for fun.

Only later did he learn that what the Master wrote over and over was often the same character. It was the character 父, father.

There was one other man who always sat in the last row, against a pillar, with a roll of scripture in his hands, reading a while, chanting a while.

That man was called Lü Shao.

Lü Shao was Tang, thirty-nine that year, a regular-script copyist in the Imperial Library, whose task was copying books. He wrote a very upright hand, every stroke level and plumb, as if written against a rule.

His grandfather was Lü Xiuyan. The Tang characters on the stele in the front courtyard had been written by Lü Xiuyan.

Whether Lü Xiuyan himself believed when he wrote the stele, no one in the family could say. When Lü Shao was small he asked his father, and his father said: "Your grandfather said: once the characters are written on the stele, they're the stele's business." Lü Shao did not understand. His father did not explain further.

By his father's generation, they believed. As for Lü Shao, on the seventh day after he was born he was carried into the monastery and baptised.

Every time Lü Shao came to the monastery, he stood a while before the stele. He did not look at what was written on it; those characters he had long since learned by heart. What he looked at was the strokes. He looked at how his grandfather began a horizontal, how he ended a vertical, how a dot fell, how a hook flicked. He had looked for decades, and his own hand grew more and more like the hand on the stele.

His colleagues at the Imperial Library teased him, saying: Brother Lü, your writing smells of stone.

He only smiled.

Besides copying books at the Imperial Library, at night he copied scriptures at home. The thirty-odd scriptures Jingjing had translated in his day—the monastery needed them, believers' households needed them too, and he copied them all. When he had copied one, he took it to the monastery, An Yan received it and put it in the scripture cupboard.

These last few years, fewer came asking for scriptures.

Lü Shao did not copy fewer. When he had copied them, he kept them one by one at his own house, stacked up, stacked until they were half a man high. His wife said: copying so many—who's to read them? Lü Shao said: someone will read them.

That day when the service was over and the people in the hall had not yet dispersed, a merchant named Kang from Luoyang came to find Jingfu.

Merchant Kang was about forty, traded silk between the two capitals year in year out, and every time he came to Chang'an came once to worship at the monastery. He drew Jingfu out into the gallery, lowered his voice, and told him something.

He said that ten years before, the brethren in Luoyang had set up a stone pillar for an old lady, beside her grave. The pillar was eight-sided, and a scripture was carved on it, and the names of those who set it up too—some named An, some named Mi, some named Kang; one of his own uncles was among them.

"This year I went to look at it again," said Merchant Kang. "It's still standing."

Jingfu asked: "What scripture is carved on it?"

Merchant Kang scratched his head. "I can't say. It begins with the two characters Xuanyuan. I don't read much. My uncle could, and he died last year."

An Yan, listening beside them, put in a question: "The monastery in Luoyang—how many are there now?"

Merchant Kang thought, held up one hand, then the other, and turned them over once.

"Twenty or thirty," he said. "At worship."

An Yan asked no more.

Jingfu said nothing either. He thought a while and said: "Carved in stone—that's good."

Merchant Kang said: "Yes. Carved deep."

That evening, Jingfu went to light the lamp.

The lamp hung on an iron hook beside the door, a bronze saucer only half a palm across, its wick twisted from hemp, to be trimmed once a day. Jingfu first took the lamp down, tipped out the dregs of last night's oil, wiped it clean with a cloth, then ladled a spoonful of new oil from the jar and added it.

Xiaoman followed at his heels, watching.

"Master," asked Xiaoman, "nobody comes at night anyway—who are you lighting the lamp for?"

Jingfu did not answer at once. He drew the wick out a little and cut it even with the scissors.

"Your grand-master lit it," he said.

"Why did Grand-master light it?"

"Grand-master's pa lit it."

"Why did Grand-master's pa light it?"

Jingfu thought. He did not know either. The Master had never told him that. The Master had told him so many things, and just that one he had not told. Perhaps he had told it, and he had forgotten.

"People walk the roads at night," he said.

Xiaoman thought, and seemed to understand, and seemed not to. He said: "Then when I'm big I'll light it too."

"First eat your ginger," said Jingfu.

Xiaoman pulled a face and ran back to the back courtyard.

Jingfu struck the flint and lit the wick. The flame rose, first blue, then yellow, leaping and leaping. He hung the lamp back on the iron hook, stepped back a pace, and looked at it once.

It was already dark. The gate of the Yining Ward would soon be shut, and people in the street walked fast. A man with a carrying pole passed the gate, stumbled, looked down at the hollowed flagstone at the gate, and by the lamplight stepped over it.

Jingfu turned and went in at the monastery gate.
