Volume Nine　The Translation Hall

Chapter 55　Shiyiniang

When spring opened in the tenth year of Zhenyuan, the He family's grandson came to take his leave. Granny He had been dead more than twenty years; in her day she had waited under the locust tree at the gate for Yisi to change her dressings; early in Tianbao Yisi had treated her old man's left leg; in the first year of Qianyuan when the city fell, the deacon left in charge of the monastery was cut down by rioting soldiers, and it was she and her old man who buried him. Her grandson was named He Jingzong; as a child he came to worship with his grandmother, sat in the last row of the hall, and dozed, and dozing, toppled into his grandmother's lap. Later he studied, sat for the Classics examination, sat three times, passed last year, and the Ministry of Personnel appointed him a county sheriff, at Lantian; he was to take up his post when spring opened. He came to the monastery in a new blue robe, bowed before the hall, went into the scripture room to see Jingjing, and spoke very courteously: he was going to Lantian to be an official; the county had much business; from now on he probably could not come on the Lord's Day; he said too that his colleagues and superiors in the county were all scholars, most of them Buddhist, some Daoist; a county sheriff, if he kept a cross in the county office and people asked, he could not answer. He said his grandmother's name—he begged the Great Virtue Jing in future, when he prayed, to say it for him; he himself would not say it. Jingjing said he would, and asked him: do you still remember that line your grandmother used to say? He Jingzong thought and said he remembered: Alaha; his grandmother said she'd said it forty years, and what she thought of in her heart was still the Lord of Heaven. When he had said it he laughed first himself, and Jingjing laughed too.

When He Jingzong had gone, Jingjing sat in the scripture room; Samaan came in, turned the account book to the page of the register of persons, and asked: this one—how shall I record him? Jingjing said: as it is. What Samaan wrote was: He so-and-so, gone to office at Lantian, will not come again; asks prayers for his grandmother, Mistress He. When he had written it he turned back a few pages and said: since the spring opened this year, this is the fifth recorded so. The first was the one south of the city who asked for a son: last winter he came to the monastery for a cross to hang on the door of the bridal room; by this spring his wife's belly had not stirred; his mother took the wife to the Great Xingshan Temple and burned three sticks of incense before the Guanyin image, and when they came back, they came no more. The second was the grandson of an old medicine dealer of the West Market who had become infatuated with alchemy and gone into the Zhongnan Mountains after a Daoist; when he left he said: your Lord says that men who die will rise again; what he wants is not to die; not dying is quicker than rising again. The third and fourth were a pair of brothers in the Shence Army; the officers of their camp were Buddhist, and at the New Year they had to follow the officers to the temples to give alms; having given alms, they could not well go running to the hu temple. The fifth was He Jingzong. When Samaan had said it, he shut the account book, folded his hands in his sleeves, and said: Master, not one of these people has cursed the monastery, nor said the Lord is not good; they just don't come. Jingjing said he knew.

That spring, the character he wrote on his board was several times the same. What he read was the line in the Gospel that John handed down where the Lord says: In my Father's house are many mansions. He read it and wrote the character jia, house, home; and when it was written thought: these people have gone out of this house, and having gone out, gone into other houses, and in other houses there is incense, there are images, there are officials, there are elixirs, there are many things that this house of his has not. He sat under the lamp facing that character, thought till dawn, and could not think of words to call them back. He wiped the board, and the next day wrote home again.

In the fourth month He Jingzong came once more, this time not alone: with him came his father, Old He, Granny He's son, past sixty, in a new silk robe, with two servants behind carrying a small wooden chest. Old He bowed to Jingjing as soon as he came in, and spoke even more courteously than his son: his mother had lain more than twenty years in the monastery's burial ground outside the Kaiyuan Gate, and owed much to the monastery's care; now his son was an official, and the family had bought an ancestral burial ground south of the city, and besides had begged a place in the pagoda courtyard of the Jianfu Temple; he meant to move his mother's bones there to lie with his father; the monks of the Jianfu Temple had already chosen the day: the third of next month. He said his mother in her lifetime believed in yours; that he knew; but his mother was of the He family, and those of the He family should be buried in the He family's ground; as a son he could not let his mother lie alone in a hu burial ground, so that people would say the He family's county sheriff had a grandmother buried beside a hu temple.

Jingjing heard him out and said Granny He was baptized; before her death she had told Priest Yisi that when she died she wished to be buried at the monastery, together with the monastery's people; that day Priest Yisi was at her bedside, and Shiyiniang too, and Shiyiniang could bear witness; and he said Granny He's name was carved on the side of the stele in the front courtyard, in Syriac, written by Priest Yisi's own hand; if the body was moved, the name would still be on the stele. Old He said: if the name's on the stele, let it stay on the stele; he didn't concern himself with the stele; he concerned himself with his mother's bones. He Jingzong stood behind his father, red-faced, unable to put in a word. Samaan beside them said softly in Syriac: the burial ground is monastery property; it's written in the register; if we don't allow the moving, even the government can't force it. Jingjing knew this; he knew too that He Jingzong was a county sheriff, that Lantian was not far from Chang'an, that if the He family meant to move her, sooner or later they could. He looked at Old He; Old He's hair was white; he gripped a string of prayer beads in his hand, gripped it till his knuckles were white; Jingjing thought: what this man wants is the same thing his Pa wanted when he brought back Du De's bowl: a son wanting to bring his mother home.

He said: move her. He said before the third of next month the monastery would raise her itself, not let the Jianfu Temple's people come; the bones raised, the monastery would wrap in cloth and hand to the He family, and the He family could go and bury them together. Old He was taken aback, probably not having expected him to agree so quickly, bowed again, and left with his men. Samaan said: Master, give way this time, and from now on every family will come to move theirs. Jingjing said: whichever family comes to move theirs, the monastery will raise them for that family; the people are the monastery's people, and also people of their own houses.

On the day of the raising Jingjing took Lingbao outside the Kaiyuan Gate, and Shiyiniang went too, taking a piece of cloth: blue cloth left over from patching Yisi's robes in those days, washed white. Granny He's grave was at the east end of the first row, right beside Yisi. Jingjing and Lingbao took spades and dug spadeful by spadeful, dug half a day, and came to a layer of rotted wood, and beneath the wood were bones, very small, very light; Jingjing knelt in the pit and picked them up piece by piece and passed them to Shiyiniang; Shiyiniang took them and laid them on the blue cloth, set them in order, head above, feet below. At the very last, at the bottom of the pit, there was a small wooden cross with the character Mi carved on its back, the way of carving handed down from Yisi's father Milis's generation; Yisi had put it in. Jingjing held that cross, thought a moment, and laid it on the blue cloth too, at Granny He's breast, and told Shiyiniang: wrap it. Shiyiniang wrapped it and tied it tight and put it into the hands of the He family's servants. The He family's servants carried the bundle away; Jingjing stood beside the empty pit; Lingbao took the spade to fill in the earth, and Jingjing said: don't fill it; let it be empty; empty, and when the monastery's people walk past here they'll know someone lay here.

In that overgrown garden west of the Qujiang Pool in the south of the city, the people grew more year by year. The first year four households, the second nine, and by the tenth year of Zhenyuan, Samaan had counted, seventeen households, forty-odd souls. Baaluo had put up a straw shed in the garden, and in the middle of the shed stood a wooden cross he had whittled himself, thicker than the one in the monastery's hall, without lotus, without clouds: just two pieces of wood nailed together. Every day before light he poured cold water over himself at the well, and when he had poured it sang scripture in the straw shed, told of the visions he had seen in Lingnan, told of the Dharma King Baolu in iron chains, told how the monastery in Chang'an was unclean. Those who went to him were outsiders from other wards who could not get gruel outside the shed in the ward street, a few young monks of the monastery, two households of old people left from the old Persian monastery of the Liquan Ward, and three or four young Tang men who had heard that the people there poured cold water over themselves every day, ate no meat, never went in at an official's gate, and thought that true religious practice, and went of their own accord.

They came to the monastery too, did not come into the hall, but stood at the gate; on the Lord's Day, when the hall came to the Tang hymn, they sang in Syriac outside the gate, very loud, drowning the sound in the hall. Of those singing in the hall some stopped and looked round, and some sang louder. Sabrisho was so angry his beard shook; he wanted the guard brothers to go and drive them off; Jingjing would not allow it, saying they stand in the ward street, and the ward street isn't the monastery's; what they sing is the psalms, and the psalms aren't the monastery's either. Sabrisho said: and their cursing the Master? They say the Master is the Son of Heaven's monk; they say the Master's Tang scriptures speak through the mouths of Buddhist monks and Daoists; they say that stele is an idol. Jingjing said the first two he had said himself; the third he did not accept; but he would not stop them saying it. He said too: those in the hall hear it, and will think about it themselves, and when they've thought, decide themselves. Sabrisho said: and if they decide wrong? Jingjing said: if they decide wrong, he's still here, and the gate is open.

There was a young monk named An Yan, a Sogdian, a little over twenty, of the monastery's young men the one whose Tang was best; of the three men Jingjing chose each year who could preach in Tang, he had been chosen in the seventh year of Zhenyuan. He went to the south of the city three times, and on coming back the third time came to the scripture room to find Jingjing and said he wanted to go and live a while in the south of the city. Jingjing asked him why. An Yan said what Elder Baaluo said, he had heard, and half of it he thought was right: the monastery's people going to the Commissioner of Merit's office to make registers, the monastery treating the wounds of the Shence Army's soldiers, the Son of Heaven's names many on the stele and the Lord's few—these he had all seen; he wanted to go and live over there a while and see how the people there kept the rule. Jingjing said: go; when you've lived there enough, if you want to come back, the gate is open. An Yan knelt down, knocked his head on the floor to him once, and left. Samaan entered a line in the accounts: The monk An Yan goes to the south of the city; the Master permits it. When he had written it he looked at Jingjing and said: Master, go on permitting like this, and the monastery will empty. Jingjing said it would not empty; and if it emptied, it would be they who left of themselves; those he kept back by force would stay in the monastery with their hearts in the south of the city.

That summer Xu Jing died. He was past eighty, deaf, and still lived in that little room at the north end of the Yining Ward; every day he came on his crutch to sit a while at the monastery gate, sat beside the stone block watching people come and go, talked to everyone: of the rules of the road, of drinking ginger broth before crossing the Pamirs, of the beacon towers of Hexi, of those two fingers he lost to frost—his fingers went to the beacon fire. He died on the hottest day of summer; in the morning he was still sitting at the monastery gate; at noon Lingbao went out to call him to eat, and he was leaning against the stone block as if asleep, a water-skin gripped in his hand, and the water-skin was empty. Jingjing remembered the spring of the second year of Shangyuan, at the gate of the Kaiyuan Gate, Xu Jing, on the army rolls and unable to leave the realm, pushing a water-skin into his arms and reciting the rules one by one, saying: I'm only saying it for my own sake. Xu Jing was buried outside the Kaiyuan Gate beside Narsai; Jingjing wrote on the stake the two characters Xu Jing, and below them a line of small characters: crossbowman of Hedong.

In the autumn Ishaq died too. He had been fat all his life, and at the end grew thin; lying in bed, his belly was still round; to all who came to see him he said one thing: mo pa, mo pa—grind and crawl; forty years he'd said it, and to the end his Tang never came right. On the night he died Jingjing sat by his bed; Ishaq held his hand and said he'd like a piece of dry flatbread; Jingjing went to the stove and fetched one, broke off a small piece and put it in his mouth; he could not chew it, but held it in his mouth, and holding it fell asleep, and never woke.

These people went one by one, and every time Jingjing saw one off, he went back and stood a while at the door of those few empty rooms in the back courtyard. Of the seven who came east from Ctesiphon in those days, Yonan, Narsai and Ishaq were gone; there remained Babai, Sabrisho, Gabriel and Haddad, four men of seventy and eighty: Babai's eyes had dimmed, he could not copy scripture, and every day he sat at the scripture room door in the sun listening to Lingbao read; Gabriel's voice had gone, and he could not tell stories; Haddad's ears were still good, he heard everything and said nothing; Sabrisho still kept the rule every day, and keeping it, contended with Jingjing. Marya was older still, and lay in bed more than he was up. Samaan's hands shook, and the characters of his accounts grew bigger year by year.

In the winter of the twelfth year of Zhenyuan, Shiyiniang fell ill. She was past fifty that year, half her hair white, and had always been well; after winter set in she coughed, coughed a month and did not get better, had fever at night; the fever broke and rose again. Shopkeeper Wang's son came to see her, said it was phlegm in the lungs, wrote a prescription; the medicine went down, the cough eased a few days and grew worse again. She herself knew. In the twelfth month she had Lingbao fetch Jingjing; she sat up in bed, two pillows behind her, her face so thin there was only a pair of eyes left, and the eyes still as bright. She sent Lingbao out and told Jingjing she had three things.

The first: when she died she was to be buried outside the Kaiyuan Gate beside your Pa; she had told your Pa so, and your Pa said good. Jingjing said he knew. The second: her first husband Cheng Yuan died at Xiangzhou in the second year of Qianyuan, and only a scabbard came back; the scabbard was buried in the Cheng family grave south of the city; the Cheng mother-in-law afterwards died at the monastery, and Jingjing too had laid out her body and buried her south of the city. She said that all these years, every year at the Cold Food festival, she had gone once to the Cheng grave to burn paper; this she had never hidden from your Pa, and your Pa had never stopped her. She said when she died, no one of the Cheng family would go any more; she wanted Lingbao to go once every year at the Cold Food festival. When she had said it she looked at Jingjing and said she knew that Baaluo of the south of the city said the monastery's people burning paper was sacrificing to ghosts; she knew it put you in a hard place too; say one word: whether Lingbao goes, whether he burns, she'd abide by you. Jingjing said: let Lingbao go; whether to burn paper, let Lingbao decide himself; if he goes and recites a passage of scripture before the grave for the Cheng family's people, that's good too. Shiyiniang nodded and said: if your Pa were here, he'd probably say the same.

The third thing she spoke of was Lingbao. She said this child Lingbao was born in this monastery; the day of his third-day bath Elder Narsai said no, and it was you who tested the water; from four years old Elder Narsai taught him his letters; in his teens he followed you copying scripture; when his great-uncle Official Pei was alive, he told her several times that Lingbao wrote Tang characters well and would surely pass the Classics examination, and once he passed, the Pei family still had a few men in the Metropolitan Prefecture who could look after him. She said she had asked Lingbao, and Lingbao said he wouldn't sit for it; he'd follow his brother. She said that she, as mother, was glad in her heart, and afraid too: afraid that following you he'd suffer all his life; how the monastery was now, she could see. She said she didn't ask you to look after him; he's your brother; there's no need for her to ask; she asked of you only one thing: in future if you're tired, if you have something to say, say it to him; don't bear it alone; your Pa bore it alone all his life, bore it till he died, and only then told you that thing. Jingjing sat by the bed, and at this bowed his head. Shiyiniang reached out and stroked his head, as when he was small and she had patched his robe for him and, when it was patched, stroked his head once.

She died on the night of the twenty-third of the twelfth month. Before she died she had Lingbao read scripture to her, in Tang, the passage Jingjing had translated: "In the highest of the heavens, deep reverence and praise; on the great earth, all again think on peace and concord"; Lingbao read and read and could not go on, and Jingjing took it up and read it; when it was read, Shiyiniang had fallen asleep, and sleeping, her breath was gone.

She was buried outside the Kaiyuan Gate beside Yisi. On the day of the burial the people of the south of the city did not come; An Yan came, alone, and stood outside the burial ground; when the burial was done he came over, knelt before Shiyiniang's grave and knocked his head on the ground three times, rose, and told Jingjing: Master Yi's wife once patched his robe for him that year. He said nothing else, nor whether he would come back, and left.

The next year at the Cold Food festival, Lingbao went to the Cheng family grave south of the city. He took a stack of paper, a small jar of wine, and that Tang scripture book. He knelt before the grave and first read the scripture, and when it was read took out the stack of paper and held it in his hands, thinking of how his Ma had squatted here every year at the Cold Food festival; in the end he did not burn it, but pressed it under a stone on the mound, poured the jar of wine before the grave, knocked his head on the ground, and came back.

He came back and did not tell Jingjing whether he had burned it or not. But the people of the south of the city told. There was a household in Baaluo's garden whose family graves were in that stretch too; at the Cold Food festival they saw Lingbao kneeling before the Cheng grave, saw him holding paper, did not see whether he burned it, went back and told it. A few days later, the people of the south of the city stood at the monastery gate, and on the Lord's Day, when they had finished singing in Syriac, someone shouted: the Chang'an monastery's deacon goes to the graves to burn paper to the ghosts; the papash's own brother sacrifices to ghosts. Everyone in the hall heard. Lingbao, kneeling in the hall, went red to the ears and was about to stand; Jingjing at the front glanced at him, and he knelt again. When worship was over Lingbao went to the scripture room to find Jingjing and said he hadn't burned it; he'd go and tell them. Jingjing asked him: you didn't burn it, but you held the paper, and you poured wine before the grave—yes? Lingbao said yes. Jingjing said: then they say you sacrificed to ghosts; you say you didn't burn it; they say you poured wine; you say the wine wasn't a sacrifice; they say then what was it? And by the end, all you're talking about is yourself, and you never get to the Lord. Lingbao said: then let them say it. Jingjing said: let them say it; you know in your own heart what you were reciting, kneeling there; your Ma knows; the Lord knows; that's enough.

Lingbao stood there and said: Brother, all your life you've been like this; whatever people say, you let them say it; aren't you tired? Jingjing looked at him and remembered what Shiyiniang had said: in future if you're tired, if you have something to say, say it to him. He said: tired. He said every morning he got up, stood a while in the middle of the courtyard, told the knotted cord once, read a passage of scripture, wrote a character, and when it was written, he wasn't so tired; that character once written, he shouldered it through the day, shouldered it till dark, put it down, and the next day took up another. He said that from the day Ma fell ill, the character on his board had always been home; he had written it all winter, and to this day it was still this character. He said: if you're willing, tomorrow morning, get up with him.

The next morning, before the wood was struck, Lingbao got up and stood in the middle of the courtyard; Jingjing stood beside him; Jingjing took the knotted cord from inside his robe, told one knot, and handed it to Lingbao; Lingbao took it, told another, and handed it back. When the edge of the sky turned grey, the two of them went into the scripture room; Jingjing opened the psalms and turned to "I will lift up mine eyes unto the hills"—in the spring of the year Azarel died, the first time he stood in the middle of the courtyard, this was the psalm he turned to as well. He wiped the character home off the board, handed Lingbao a stick of charcoal, and said: today you write.
