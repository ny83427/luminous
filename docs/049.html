Volume Eight　The Daqin Monastery

Chapter 49　Guo Ziyi

In the summer of the second year of Jianzhong, Guo Ziyi fell ill.

He had not been well since spring. He had not come on the day the stele was raised, saying he was ill; then it was only a cough, and restless nights. By the fourth month he was eating less; he could not finish a bowl of millet gruel, and had the half left taken away. By the fifth month he hardly drank the gruel at all; every morning he took only a few mouthfuls of rice-water, and the dish of pickles sat on the table untouched.

That year he was eighty-five.

The Guo mansion in the Qinren Ward took up a good part of the ward. There were so many people in the household that Lord Guo himself did not know them all. He had eight sons and seven sons-in-law, and grandchildren by the dozen, who grew up one by one, married and were married off, and had children of their own. At New Year and the festivals the grandchildren came in in a line to kneel and knock their heads to him; one knelt and rose, and another knelt. Lord Guo sat above and looked them over one by one, and as he looked, he only nodded.

Everyone in the household knew the Lord Director could not tell all his grandchildren apart. The grandchildren knew it too. When they knelt, if someone announced a name, the Lord Director said "Mm"; if no one announced it, the Lord Director said "Mm" too.

The Lord Director's "Mm" was always the same.

At the beginning of the sixth month it turned hot.

The household called in physicians, one after another. The palace sent people too: the Son of Heaven sent a palace envoy to ask after him, bringing medicines and words, who stood a while by the bed, said a few things, and left. When he had left, the household called in Buddhist monks too, and Daoist priests; in the front courtyard sutras were chanted, in the back courtyard rites were held, from morning to night, incense smoke curling, the wooden fish, the bells, the chanting drifting wave after wave into the inner room.

Lord Guo lay in bed and listened.

One evening he woke and heard people talking in the outer room.

It was one of his sons, and a grandson. The two kept their voices down, thinking he was asleep.

The son said the palace envoy had come again today and asked whether the Lord Director would like a great master brought into the house—a great master of the palace, from the Son of Heaven's own side. The son said: I told him we'd already sent for some. The envoy nodded, looked at all those monks in the front courtyard, and asked again: in the Lord Director's early years, wasn't there a hu monk in his command?

The grandson asked: what did you answer, Father?

The son said: I said we'd long had nothing to do with him.

The outer room was silent a while. The grandson asked again: that hu monk—Grandfather hasn't sent for him these days either.

The son said: better that he doesn't. People from the palace are in and out of this house all day. Your uncles' offices, your own appointment next year—it all hangs on this.

The talk in the outer room stopped. Footsteps went out.

Lord Guo lay with his eyes open, looking up at the bed canopy.

After a long while he turned his head and looked at the little stand beside the bed.

On the little stand sat a bronze basin. Wide-mouthed, shallow, with a dent in its rim. Its outside rubbed bright by hands, bright as a small mirror. Half full of clear water, left from washing his face that morning.

When the maids washed his face, they used a silver basin. He would not have it; he used only this one. The maids said behind his back that the Lord Director was sentimental, and couldn't bear to throw away a thing from his campaigning days.

He looked at that basin, looked a long time.

"Someone," he said.

The one who came in was that grandson.

Lord Guo said: "Pour out the water. Wipe it clean."

The grandson did as he was told.

"Wrap it in a cloth."

The grandson did that too. When it was wrapped, he held it in his hands and stood by the bed, waiting.

Lord Guo looked at the bundle, looked a while more.

"Take it to the Yining Ward," he said. "To the Great Virtue Yi."

"Grandfather, is there any message to go with it?"

Lord Guo thought.

"Returned to its owner," he said.

The grandson waited for him to go on. He said no more. He closed his eyes.

The grandson reached the Yining Ward early the next morning.

He was seventeen or eighteen, in a brand-new robe, on horseback, with a servant. He stood at the monastery gate, looked at the characters over the gate, looked at the newly raised stele in the courtyard, and asked to see the Great Virtue Yi.

Yisi came out.

The grandson handed him the bundle and said: "Grandfather's orders—this is for the Great Virtue Yi. Grandfather says: returned to its owner."

Yisi took the bundle and opened one corner.

It was the bronze basin.

He looked at it. The dent in the rim faced him exactly.

"Grandfather also said," said the grandson, and paused to think, as if considering how to say it properly, "that in his illness he sees no visitors, and the Great Virtue Yi needn't come to see him. The house is crowded, it's all confusion; he's afraid the Great Virtue would be slighted."

Yisi said: "I understand."

The grandson looked at him, looked at the basin, and asked a little shyly: "Great Virtue Yi, this basin… does it have some story? Everyone at home says Grandfather used it all his life to wash his face, and would rather go without the silver basin than without it. None of us know why."

"It's an army thing," said Yisi. "On the march, for drawing water, washing your face, drinking. In those years in camp, I used it too."

The grandson said "Oh," seeming a little disappointed, and seeming a little relieved. He bowed with clasped hands, mounted, and went.

Yisi stood at the monastery gate and wrapped the bundle up again, one layer, and another.

He was not much surprised.

In these years he had seen a great deal of it.

In Suzong's years there had been several officials of the ministries who came to the monastery every seventh day for the offering, stood at the back, listened very respectfully, and on leaving wrote their names in the book of merit. Later, when the Son of Heaven believed in the Buddha and the chief minister Wang Jin was in power, the whole court kept the fasts along with him, and those officials stopped coming. One of them met him in the West Market and from a long way off raised his sleeve to hide half his face, and walked past him.

There was a general too who, before he went on campaign, came knocking at the monastery gate in the middle of the night wanting to be baptised, saying that once baptised, swords and spears would not come near him in battle. Yisi told him there was no such teaching. The general said: baptise me anyway. He was baptised, and went. He came back victorious, was promoted, built a Buddhist temple in the south of the city, invited dozens of monks, and gave the temple its bell.

And there were those who came to stand a while under the purple robe, came to look at the stele, came to beg a bowl of gruel, came to beg a future. Many came, and many went. Some, when they went, looked back once. Some did not even turn their heads.

Yisi did not blame them.

But Lord Guo was different.

He could not say how he was different. On the day Lord Guo was baptised, what he asked was whether his sons and grandsons could be kept safe. He had told Lord Guo they could not. Lord Guo had said: baptise me anyway.

That night he had known this basin would come back sooner or later. Only he had not thought it would come back like this.

That evening, he did not go to the Guo mansion.

He lit a small lamp in the hall, the wick turned down very small, the flame no bigger than a bean. He set the bronze basin beside the lamp, with no water in it.

He knelt and recited a passage.

He recited it in Syriac, very low, very slow, like water running through the cracks in a rock. It was the passage his father Milis had taught him, which his father had learned from his father. Twenty-four years before, in a tent lit by one small lamp, he had recited it once. Afterwards in Chang'an he had recited it for many people.

This time, only he heard it.

When he had finished, he stayed on his knees.

He thought of that night, Lord Guo kneeling on the felt, the water running down from his white hair, dripping drop by drop into his beard. Lord Guo had opened his eyes and said: it's less than I thought. He had said: there was never much.

He knelt before the lamp a while longer before he stood up and blew it out.

In the sixth month, Guo Ziyi died.

It was the fifth day after the grandson's visit. He died at night; in the room were his sons, the physicians, the maids. It was said he went very quietly: the day before he had taken a few mouthfuls of rice-water, lain down at night, and not woken again.

The Son of Heaven suspended court for five days.

On the day of the funeral, all Chang'an came out. From the Qinren Ward to the city gate, both sides of the street were lined with people, several deep—officials, soldiers, merchants, common folk, hu, Tang. The procession took a long time to pass: banners, streamers, carriages, horses, monks, Daoists, company after company going by. As the coffin passed, the people on both sides of the street knelt in swathes.

He was buried as an attendant at Suzong's tomb, the Jian Mausoleum. That was a long way off, northwest of Chang'an, several days' journey.

Yisi did not follow.

He stood at the side of the street inside the Jinguang Gate, among a crowd of old soldiers. They were all men of the Shuofang army of those days, their hair all white now; some were lame, some had lost an arm, some leaned on sticks. Who knew where they had heard it, but they had come early in the morning and stood together, saying nothing. Yisi stood among them and said nothing either. He wore that old grey robe, not tall, very broad in the shoulder, and standing with them you could not tell how he was any different.

A lame old soldier recognised him, edged over, and said under his breath: "Master Yi, before the Lord Director went, did he send for you?"

Yisi said: "No."

The old soldier said "Oh," and after a while said again: "Ill, he saw no one. Well."

As the coffin passed before them, the old soldiers did not kneel. They stood straight, cupped their fists, and bowed together from the waist.

Yisi cupped his fists too, and bowed.

Bowed, he looked at the ground. On the ground were the tracks of the cart wheels, rut after rut, very deep. He thought of Old Duan, of Hun Shizhi, of Li Guangbi, of Du De, of the men below the walls of Changshan whose names he could not call. Lord Guo had remembered every one of them; he had told Yisi so once. Now Lord Guo was gone too, and those who remembered were one fewer.

That day when he got back to the monastery, he took the bronze basin out of its bundle.

He drew a basin of well water and washed it inside and out. Inside it had gone black; outside it was rubbed bright by hands. He washed it very carefully, once, and again, and the dent in the rim he wiped with his finger several times over.

When he had washed it, he dried it with a dry cloth and wrapped it in cloth again, one layer, and another.

Then he opened the cupboard, put it at the very back, and shut the cupboard door.

Jingjing stood at the door a while watching him do all this, and did not ask.
