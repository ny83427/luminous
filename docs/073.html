Volume Eleven　Huichang

Chapter 73　A-Nu

In the seventh month of the fifth year of Huichang, the lamps of all the city's monasteries were lit later at night than in other years. In the fourth month two edicts—under forty, under fifty—and those returned to lay life came out of the monastery gates batch after batch, in newly bought futou caps, bundles on their backs, going back to their native places to be registered; in the fifth month came another: those over fifty without certificates from the Bureau of Sacrifices were to return to lay life too, and foreign monks without such certificates were also to return to lay life and be sent back to their own countries. People in the West Market said that at the Zisheng Monastery an old monk from Japan, who had lived several years in Chang'an, had been ordered back to lay life in the fifth month, and, in Tang clothes and a futou, carrying his few chests of scriptures, had been escorted east by men sent by the government, to take ship at Dengzhou for his own country. When the monastery's people heard it, each bowed his head over his own work; Joseph and Elijah were men of Merv, written on the register as come from the Patriarchate; those few nights the two of them lit a lamp in the west wing and talked in Syriac till midnight.

By the seventh month it was said in the city that this was not the end; the palace was debating a great one: the monasteries of the empire were to be torn down, till each street of the two capitals kept only two, and each of those kept thirty monks; all the rest torn down without exception; the bronze images melted to cast coin, the iron images melted to cast farm tools; the monasteries' lands confiscated, their slaves freed, monks and nuns returned to lay life, not one kept. It was said in the city that the Secretariat-Chancellery was debating this one, the chief ministers were debating it, had debated it over a month, and it was not yet settled. That year morning devotion numbered nineteen.

Lü Shao came at dusk on the fourteenth of the seventh month. He was thirty-five that year and had been a scribe in the Secretariat a dozen-odd years, copying documents, fair-copying drafts of edicts; he wrote a fine regular hand; he had never been promoted and never demoted; the office's people called him Scribe Lü, and had called him so a dozen-odd years. In the Sweet Dew year he had hidden seven days in the monastery; since then, every year at the winter solstice and the New Year he came to the monastery once, bringing a packet of pastries, stood a while before the stele looking at his great-uncle's name, said a few words with Jingfu, and left. He did not believe—he had said so several times—but when anything concerned the monastery and he knew of it, he always came to say. In the third year of Kaicheng, when the gruel shed borrowed rice, it was he who wrote Jingfu the petition; in the first year of Huichang, when the Mi officer's son would give no more and Jingfu went to wait for him at the gate of the Secretariat, he gave two strings from his own pay for the gruel shed.

That day when he came it was no festival. When he came in at the gate the sky was not yet dark and the ward gate not yet shut; he brought no pastries, and the sweat on his face was not yet dry; once inside he called for Jingfu and said there was a matter he must tell the leader and the Great Worthy Lingbao—must tell indoors, with the door shut.

Jingfu led him into the scripture room. Lingbao was at the monastery that day; when morning devotion broke up he hadn't gone back but sat at the scripture room door in the sun; He Siniang helped him in and he sat down. Lü Shao stood and would not sit, saying he'd go as soon as he'd said it; he said today in the office he had fair-copied the draft of a memorandum of articles, submitted by the Secretariat-Chancellery to the Son of Heaven, debating precisely the tearing down of monasteries; the draft had been revised several times; the earlier rounds he hadn't copied; this round he had copied, and when it was copied it was handed in, and tomorrow it would be presented. He said the memorandum ran a dozen-odd sheets and spoke all of Buddhist monasteries—how many kept in the two capitals, how many kept in the prefectures, how many monks kept in the monasteries kept, timber from the demolished monasteries to repair government offices, bronze images cast into coin, lands confiscated, slaves registered as households—article by article, very detailed. He said that at the very end there was just one sentence that spoke of you.

He stood there and recited that sentence. He said when he was copying and reached this sentence his hand stopped; he read the sentence again, read it till he knew it; he was afraid of remembering it wrong, and on the way had said it over in his head several times. He said the sentence was written thus: As for the Daqin, the Magi and such shrines: the teaching of Shakya having been reformed, heretical cults may not alone survive; their people are all to be returned to lay life and sent back to their native places as tax households; if they are foreigners, they are to be sent back to their own places to be taken in charge there.

Everyone in the scripture room listened. When Lü Shao had recited it, he said it again, more slowly. He said he didn't understand your teaching; he knew only characters; he said that taken at their face the characters meant: the Buddhist monasteries are all torn down; hu monasteries like yours can't be left alone either; the monastery's people—the Tang among them return to lay life and go back to their native places to be tax-paying registered households; the foreigners are sent back to their own countries and handed over to be taken in charge there. He said whether the Daqin Monastery was to be torn down this sentence did not say; but if the Buddhist monasteries are all torn down and the hu monasteries may not alone survive, that means torn down. He said the memorandum would be presented tomorrow; if the Son of Heaven approved it, it would be a decree, and the decree would be promulgated probably next month.

Jingfu asked him: these words—for saying them, what punishment do you face? Lü Shao said: for leaking palace matters that should be kept secret, the code says strangling; judged lightly, it's still penal servitude for a year and a half. He said he knew; he had thought about it on the way; he said he was a copyist, had copied a dozen-odd years, and nothing he had copied had ever had anything to do with himself; this time, as he copied, he remembered that winter when he took a sword cut on his arm and climbed over the wall and it was this monastery's people who carried him indoors. He said he didn't believe in your Lord, but that morning your leader opened the gate and said the man is inside; he remembered it, and thought he owed that sentence.

When he had said it, he clasped his hands, turned, and left. Jingfu saw him to the monastery gate; he stood a moment before the lamp at the gate and told Jingfu: Leader, he hasn't been here today. Jingfu said he knew; you haven't been here today; he won't tell anyone you came; but if someone asks him, he won't say you didn't come either. When Lü Shao heard it, the corners of his mouth moved, and he said: you people of this monastery, even at a time like this, you're still like this; and he left.

That night Lingbao did not go back to the south end of the ward. He sent He Siniang back first with Xiaoman, and rested in the monastery, in the scripture room. He lay down and could not sleep; lay till the second watch, sat up again, and sent someone to the west wing to call Joseph.

Joseph came and asked what he wanted. Lingbao said: read me the accounts. Joseph said: which book? Lingbao said: Elder Samaan's, from the start; read as far as you get. Joseph said: Great Worthy, in the middle of the night—why read accounts? Lingbao said he couldn't sleep; his eyes couldn't see; he wanted to hear how this monastery had come through in the past.

Joseph lit a lamp, took Samaan's nine account books from the scripture cabinet, and began reading from the oldest. Second year of Baoying: Elders Azarel, Marya and Samaan arrive in Chang'an; for the welcome feast, so many dou of flour, so many sheep; from the Guo mansion's kitchen a basket of steamed cakes. First year of Guangde: the Tibetans enter the city; one hundred and forty-odd old and weak of the ward taken in; two sacks of rice to ransom the gate. The Dali years: the translating of scriptures, the gruel shed, the register of baptisms; the stonemason father and sons' meals for forty days the year the stele was raised; "Master Lü would not accept"; "'what is sought' changed to 'what is thanked'." Joseph read slowly; the Syriac letters were small, and in Samaan's later books the hand shook; he leaned to the lamp and made them out letter by letter, and when he had made out a line read it, and said it in Tang. Lingbao sat leaning against the wall, listening; sometimes he said: this entry he remembered; sometimes: this entry he didn't know.

When he reached the fourth year of Zhenyuan, Joseph came to the gruel shed's page. That year the Commissioners of Merit made the registers; the monastery's people could not cope; the gruel shed in the West Market stopped and moved to the ward street, two pots; Samaan recorded several pages—the rice, the firewood, the numbers who came for gruel, "those who got none"—and after came a line, which Joseph read out: From outside the ward: A-Nu, grandson of a certain old woman of Guangde Ward, six years old; given from the brethren's own portions.

Lingbao said: stop a moment. He said he remembered this line. He said that winter the gruel in the ward street was not enough to go round; the first day, Brother poured his own bowl out for a child held by an old woman; the child was from outside the ward; by the rule, the ward street's gruel was given only to the ward's people, not to those from outside; Brother poured out his own bowl, and the next day he poured his out too; he was in his early twenties then, and having poured it out, went hungry to run errands in the West Market. He said what that child looked like he didn't remember; Elder Samaan asked the child's name and entered it in the accounts; only today did he learn the child was called A-Nu.

Joseph turned on a few pages, and read another line. It was from that twelfth month—the corpse accounts: Elder Narsai pushing the cart, the stakes outside the Kaiyuan Gate, Samaan recording them one by one. Joseph read: Twelfth month, nineteenth day: Granny Yang of Guangde Ward, frozen to death by the gruel shed in the ward street, about sixty; collected by Narsai; stake, second row. Her grandson A-Nu had earlier been taken by a palace eunuch as an adopted son.

Lingbao, leaning against the wall, did not let Joseph read on. He said: read it again. Joseph read it again. Lingbao said: taken as an adopted son—that is, taken by a palace man. He said in the Zhenyuan years the palace eunuchs took adopted sons, bought from Lingnan or Minzhong, and some carried off from poor families of the wards; carried off, they were castrated, took their adoptive father's surname; the adoptive father in the palace, the adopted son entered the palace too, as a little Yellow Gate, and grown up became a eunuch; in those days the poor families of the wards—some wept, some didn't, some sent their children themselves, saying that with a eunuch they'd have a mouthful to eat. He said: that old woman, A-Nu's granny—that whole winter she came every day with the child in her arms for gruel; later the child was carried off by a eunuch, and she still came alone, came a few times, and in the twelfth month froze to death by the gruel shed.

Joseph said: Great Worthy, this was more than fifty years ago. Lingbao said he knew. He asked Joseph: on that page, A-Nu was of Guangde Ward? Joseph said Guangde Ward. Lingbao said: Granny Yang? Joseph said Granny Yang. Lingbao sat there, his hand resting on his knee, his fingers tapping, tap, tap. He said: last year when the hermitages were torn down, the old eunuch who came to inspect the ward—Headman Liu said his surname was Yang; the palace people call him Old Yang; he's been decades in the Commissioners of Merit's inspectorate of the Right Street. He said Jingfu had told him the old eunuch sat on his donkey and asked how many years the gruel shed had been open, asked how many pots a day, and when he'd asked, looked at the pot, and left. He said: a eunuch inspecting hermitages—inspect his Buddha shrines and be done; why ask about the gruel shed? Joseph said: Great Worthy, Yang is a great surname; there are tens of thousands named Yang in Chang'an. Lingbao said he knew. He said tomorrow he'd have Jingfu go and ask Headman Liu where that Old Yang came from.

Jingfu went to ask early the next day. Headman Liu was not very willing to say, saying: palace eunuchs' affairs—what would he, a ward headman, know? Jingfu asked twice before he said that he too had heard it from the old people of the ward: that Old Yang as a child was of Guangde Ward, his family poor; in the Zhenyuan years a eunuch surnamed Yang carried him off as an adopted son, when he was only six; he grew up in the palace, later became a eunuch, and spent the second half of his life in the Commissioners of Merit's inspectorate of the Right Street, rising to judge; his name was Yang Shousu; the palace people called him Old Yang; the ward's old people still remembered his child-name—what it was, Headman Liu said he couldn't recall.

Jingfu came back and told Lingbao. Lingbao said: then it's him. That night Jingfu and Lingbao debated in the scripture room, with Joseph there, and Elijah, and the Shi brothers at the door. Jingfu said: Uncle Lingbao, you want to go and find him. Lingbao said yes. Jingfu said: find him to do what—beg him? He said Old Yang was a judge of the Commissioners of Merit's inspectorate of the Right Street; the memorandum was debated by the Secretariat-Chancellery; the decree is sent down by the Son of Heaven; what could Old Yang, an inspectorate eunuch, have any say in? He said: even if he had a little say, if he said one word for a hu monastery and the palace people knew, Zhao Guizhen's band knew, then he, an old eunuch past sixty, would lose forty or fifty years' career, and very likely his life. He said: our monastery's rule is, we don't beg the government, we don't use connections; Master was so; Great Worthy Yi was so; at Lord Guo's, Great Worthy Yi never once in his life begged.

Lingbao said he would not beg him. He said he'd only go, spread that page of accounts open, and let him look. Jingfu said: how is that different from begging? Lingbao said it was different. He said: begging is asking him to do something; spreading out the accounts is letting him know something; once he knows, whether he does anything is his business. He said: that bowl of gruel—Brother poured it, he poured it; when they poured it they didn't know who that child would be, and the child didn't know who they were; Elder Samaan kept the accounts, kept the truth, and when he entered it he didn't know that one day someone would come and turn up this page. He said this page of accounts, fifty-odd years, has lain all along in the scripture cabinet; taking it out today, he isn't going to collect a debt for that bowl of gruel—gruel poured is poured; he is going to tell that man that as a child he ate a bowl of gruel in Yining Ward, and who poured that bowl, and that the monastery that poured it is to be torn down next month.

Jingfu said: then he'll go. He said: he's the leader; the monastery's affairs, he goes. Lingbao said: you won't go; he'll go. He said: that bowl of gruel—Brother poured it first, and the next day he poured his too; you weren't yet born; he said: you're the leader; if you go, it's the leader begging a judge of the inspectorate, the monastery begging; if he goes, it's a blind old man going to show another old man a page of accounts. He said he was seventy-eight; if he went and Old Yang had him thrown out, he'd have no grievance; if you went and were thrown out, the monastery would have no leader after.

Joseph beside them said: Great Worthy, what if Old Yang takes this for a trap—thinks the monastery has dug into his past and is using a thing of fifty years ago to threaten him? Lingbao said: then let him think so. He said he wasn't sure either; he was just a man who couldn't see, going once with an account book.

The Commissioners of Merit's inspectorate of the Right Street was in an office west of the Imperial City, with two clerks keeping its gate. Lingbao went on the seventeenth of the seventh month, He Siniang holding him up, the account book in his robe, wrapped in a piece of blue cloth. The clerks at the gate asked whom he sought; he said Judge Yang; the clerks asked who he was; he said: of the Daqin Monastery in Yining Ward, Yi Lingbao, formerly the monastery's priest, now a registered householder. The clerks said Judge Yang saw no outsiders and told him to go home. Lingbao said he wouldn't go; he said he'd wait at the gate; whenever Judge Yang came out, then he'd speak; if Judge Yang didn't come out, he'd wait till the ward gates were about to close and go back, and come again tomorrow.

He waited at the gate two watches. He Siniang found him a stone to sit on; he sat hugging that blue cloth packet. The sun moved from the east to overhead and slanted on toward the west; people went in and out of the gate, some in green robes, some in blue; all looked at him once, and no one paid him any heed. Past noon a minor clerk came out and said Judge Yang bade him come in.

Yang Shousu received him in a small room. In the room was a table, and on the table registers in stack after stack; against the wall a cabinet, and on the cabinet registers too; the window was small, the room dim; a lamp burned on the table. Yang Shousu sat behind the table, past sixty, thin, a little hunchbacked, in that green robe, his face beardless—a eunuch's face, pale, wrinkled; his eyes very small, the lids drooping. He Siniang helped Lingbao in; he told He Siniang to go out and wait outside the door; He Siniang looked at Lingbao; Lingbao nodded; she went out and drew the door shut behind her.

Only the two old men were left in the room. Yang Shousu said: Great Worthy, please sit; his voice was a eunuch's voice, thin and husky. Lingbao sat on the stool before the table, set the blue cloth packet on the table, untied it, took out the account book, and opened it. He could not see; last night he had had Joseph put a fine hemp cord in that page; he felt for the cord, turned to that page, turned the account book round, and pushed it to the far end of the table, to before Yang Shousu. He said: Judge Yang, this page—look at it. He said: this page is in Syriac letters; you can't read them; he'll read it to you.

He read it. He recited that line, first in Syriac, then said it in Tang: From outside the ward: A-Nu, grandson of a certain old woman of Guangde Ward, six years old; given from the brethren's own portions. He said: this was in the winter of the fourth year of Zhenyuan; that year the Commissioners of Merit made the registers; the monastery's people couldn't cope; the gruel shed in the West Market stopped and moved to the ward street of Yining Ward; the gruel wasn't enough to go round; the ward street's gruel was given only to the ward's people, not to those from outside; an old woman of Guangde Ward came with a child in her arms for gruel, couldn't get any, and stood by the pot; his brother Jingjing—the one who wrote the stele—poured his own bowl out for that child, and the next day he poured his out too; Elder Samaan asked the child's name and entered it in the accounts—this line. He said he had come today only to let Judge Yang look at this line.

When he had said it, he reached again for the account book, turned on a few pages, to the place marked by another hemp cord, and pushed it over again. He said: this page is from that year's twelfth month. He read: Twelfth month, nineteenth day: Granny Yang of Guangde Ward, frozen to death by the gruel shed in the ward street, about sixty; collected by Narsai; stake, second row. Her grandson A-Nu had earlier been taken by a palace eunuch as an adopted son. He said: Elder Narsai was the one who pushed the monastery's corpse cart; that winter he pushed dozens out of the Kaiyuan Gate; this old woman he pushed out, and buried outside the Kaiyuan Gate, stake in the second row; on the stake was written Granny Yang of Guangde Ward; that stake, fifty-odd years, was still there some years ago; whether it's still there this year, he can't see any more; he doesn't know.

When he had said it, he put both hands back on his knees. The flame of the lamp in the room jumped. Yang Shousu sat behind the table, head bowed, looking at those two pages of accounts. Not one Syriac letter could he read; he looked at those letters, at those winding strokes, small and close, line upon line, looked at the two places Lingbao's finger had just pressed. He put out his hand—a eunuch's hand, pale, thin, the nails trimmed very even—and with one finger pressed on that line, pressed on the line's beginning, then moved to the line's end and pressed. He closed the account book, closed it very gently, wrapped it in the blue cloth, and pushed it back to this end of the table, to Lingbao's hand.

Lingbao felt the blue cloth packet and took it back into his arms. Yang Shousu, behind the table, asked one thing; what he asked was about that line in the accounts—stake, second row—on which side outside the Kaiyuan Gate? Lingbao said: to the north, against the earth slope; the second row, counting from the east end, the tenth-something stake; he couldn't remember exactly; Elder Narsai's hand; on the stake was written Granny Yang of Guangde Ward; decades now; the characters have likely been worn smooth by wind and rain. Yang Shousu heard it and asked nothing more. Yang Shousu said: Great Worthy, the matter of the decree is the Secretariat-Chancellery's affair, the Son of Heaven's affair; the inspectorate has no say in it. He said he'd been in the palace over fifty years, the second half of his life in the inspectorate; he knew what the inspectorate had a say in and what it hadn't. He said: the Great Worthy came today; he heard; what the Great Worthy said, he'll remember. He said: Great Worthy, please go home; the sun is strong outside; have that wife of yours hold you up and walk slowly.

Lingbao stood up, clasped his hands, turned, and went toward the door; he could not see, and walked slowly; at the door he reached out and felt the doorframe, found it, and stopped. He turned back and said to the man in the room: Judge Yang, that bowl of gruel—poured is poured; he didn't come today to collect on it. Yang Shousu, behind the table, said he knew.

He Siniang was waiting outside the door and held him up. They walked out of the office into the street; the sun was already slanting west. Lingbao walked a few steps and said to He Siniang: he didn't agree. He Siniang said: he didn't agree, and he didn't have you thrown out either. Lingbao said yes. He said: when he was small, Pa took him to the Guo mansion; Pa said: when Lord Guo doesn't send for him, he never goes; today he went of himself; no one sent for him.

That evening Yang Shousu sat in that small room till after the ward gates had shut; the lamp on the table was refilled twice. A minor clerk of the inspectorate came to tidy up and saw him sitting behind the table, a sheet of paper spread before him with nothing written on it, the brush in his hand, the ink dry on its tip. The clerk asked whether the judge needed anything more; he said no, and sent the clerk home, and blew out the lamp himself.
