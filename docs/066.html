Volume Ten　Morning Praises

Chapter 66　Siyuan

In the seventh month of the ninth year of Dahe another dispatch came from the Commissioners of Merit's inspectorate.

This time it was not only the registers being counted; there was to be a purge. The dispatch said that by imperial order: among the monks and nuns of the realm the false and the improper were many, eating away at the living; the senior officials of each locality, together with the Commissioners of Merit's office, were to go to the monasteries and examine them; monks and nuns must recite a number of sheets of their own scriptures, and those who failed were to be ordered back to lay life; the old and the sick were to be granted some leniency. People in the West Market said it was Li Xun's idea. Li Xun had newly come into favour, not old, loud of speech, and had told the Son of Heaven that half the monks and nuns of the Buddhist monasteries were dodging labour service and the other half dodging taxes, and the Son of Heaven, hearing it, issued an edict.

When the monastery's people heard, the first thing they thought of was that business at Sizhou, and they all said that this time it was deserved: those who had bought certificates at Sizhou ought to be purged. When they had said it, they thought of themselves: the twenty on the register were monks too.

That year twenty-seven stood at the morning. In these few years the Kangs' old uncle by marriage, the carpenter who whittled crosses, had died; the Luo Twelfth had moved to Luoyang with his mother; and this summer the heat had killed one more old believer, the widow who sold needles and thread at the west end of the ward, past seventy, who died beside her own stall, a hank of thread still gripped in her hand.

Lingbao, off the register, lived at the south end of the ward, came in every day before light along the ward street, broke the bread at the offering once in seven as before, and kept the accounts as before. He was sixty-eight. The hands that broke the bread shook worse than they had a few years ago, and sometimes Jingfu stood beside him and held the glass bowl steady for him. He Siniang came with him every day and stood on the women's side; in winter she carried a little hand-warmer, and when the morning broke up she pushed it into Lingbao's hands.

On the day the inspectorate came to examine them, the one who came was an administrative assistant, with two clerks and an aide-de-camp, and he set a table in the gallery before the hall. The assistant was forty or so, narrow-faced, unsmiling.

"The monks of your monastery—what scriptures do they recite?"

"The scriptures of our own religion," said Jingfu. "Some in Syriac, some in Tang."

"Which are in Tang?"

Jingfu had Qushen bring the list of scriptures. Qushen held it out; the assistant read it through, and when he reached the last line, not yet translated, put his finger on it. "What does this mean?"

"Those whose translation isn't finished."

"Not finished—do they count as scriptures?"

"They count. At the Patriarchate they're scriptures; in Chang'an they're scriptures not yet finished being translated."

The assistant asked no more, and had the clerks call them by the register one by one. Whoever was called stood before the table and recited a passage of scripture; those who could recite it were marked with a circle, those who couldn't with a cross.

The first was Joseph. He stood before the table and recited a psalm in Syriac, fast and loud. When he had finished, the assistant looked at the clerk and the clerk looked at the assistant; neither could understand.

"What did you recite?"

"A psalm. The twenty-third. The Lord is my shepherd."

"Did you get it wrong?"

"No."

"How do I know you didn't get it wrong?"

Joseph stood there and thought. "Then let Your Honour call Elijah. Elijah will recite it once more, and if the two of us recite the same, it wasn't wrong."

The assistant raised his eyes to him. "Good. Call Elijah."

Elijah came and recited it once, a little slower. The assistant listened and couldn't understand, but the two recitations were about the same length and much the same in tone, and he had the clerk draw two circles.

Qushen recited the Sutra of Profound Peace and Joy, a long passage, without one wrong word. When he reached Like a mirror reflecting things: the thing goes, the image is gone, the assistant raised his head. "This is your scripture?"

"It is."

"That line sounds like a Daoist saying."

"The scripture was translated by Master Jingjing," said Qushen. "The Master said there were only these words in the speech of the Tang, so he borrowed them for use. Where he borrowed, he put a dot beside it, so that when someone found words of their own, they could change them."

"Where are the dots?"

"On the scrolls in the scripture room. When you recite, you can't recite the dots."

The corner of the assistant's mouth moved, and he had the clerk draw a circle.

When Siyuan was called, he shuffled to the table on his stick. He was sixty-five that year and had pushed the cart for the dead more than forty years; his back was bent, his knees ruined; in winter he couldn't get out of bed, and in summer could only shuffle slowly. He steadied himself and recited the passage he had recited all his life, the one Elder Narsai taught him in his day, from the books of the Law:

"Ye shall not afflict any widow, or fatherless child, or the stranger. If thou afflict them in any wise, and they cry at all unto me, I will surely hear their cry…"

He recited a few lines and stopped; the lines after would not come back to him. He stood there on his stick, his lips moving. Jingfu stood by a gallery pillar and did not prompt him. The assistant waited, the clerk waited with his brush, everyone in the gallery waited. Siyuan shifted his feet once and still could not remember, and recited it again from the beginning, and came to the same place, and stopped again.

"I'm old; I can't remember. Your Honour, mark the cross."

The assistant looked at him. "What do you do in this monastery?"

"Push the cart."

"What cart?"

"The cart for the dead. Those in the city who freeze or starve by the roadside, whom no one sees to—I push them outside the Kaiyuan Gate and bury them and set up a stake. Those with names, I write the name; those without, I write which ward they died in, and which day."

"How many years have you pushed it?"

"I took over the cart in the seventh year of Zhenyuan. After Elder Narsai died I took it over and pushed it, and I've pushed it till now: forty-four years. When I couldn't push it any more, young fellows from the ward helped push, and I walked holding the shafts."

"How many have you buried?"

"I've never counted. It's in the monastery's accounts. Lingbao knows."

The assistant did not go to ask Lingbao. He looked at Siyuan a while, and had the clerk draw a circle. "The old and the sick, to be granted some leniency. That's what the dispatch says."

When the calling was done that day—twenty men, nineteen circles, one cross—the cross was drawn under the name of the younger Shi brother. As a child he had learned few characters; he couldn't recite the Syriac scriptures, nor the whole of the Tang ones either. The assistant said he would take the register back, and how the Commissioner's office disposed of it, they must wait for a dispatch. The clerk put away the register, the aide-de-camp yawned behind them, and they left.

The younger Shi brother did not sleep that night. He sat on the well curb, and his elder brother sat with him until dawn.

Rijin came on the third night. In the hermitage in the south of the city, people from Wannian County had come to examine them too. What he held was a Sizhou certificate, and the county commandant took one look and called it false and improper and made him recite a Buddhist scripture; he couldn't. The old monk, hard of hearing, pleaded for him; the commandant said that certificates from that platform at Sizhou, every one found in the capital counted; under his name too a cross was drawn.

He stood outside the monastery gate beside the lamp and talked with Jingfu.

"Director, if I'm returned to lay life, I'll go home. Going home means being a registered householder, and householders answer labour service. Pa's dead, Ma's old; returned to lay life, I'll be just in time to go back and look after Ma," he said. "I shaved my head in my day to dodge the service. Now they've found me out; I'm not wronged."

"Once you're home, will you still come to the morning?"

Rijin rubbed his hands. "I don't know. I don't know yet whether a shaved head like mine can come in at this gate."

The monastery's dispatch never came, and nothing more was heard of Rijin's cross either. That winter something great happened in the city, and after that no one spoke of the purge again.

What happened in the city of Chang'an on the twenty-first of the eleventh month, the monastery's people learned only at dusk.

That morning the morning was done as usual and the gruel shed opened as usual. In the afternoon, of a sudden, people were running along the ward street, coming from the north, running past the gruel shed; one ran so hard that he knocked over a stack of bowls by the gruel pot, and the bowls smashed all over the ground, and he did not stop. Jingfu caught hold of one he knew. "What's happened?"

"They're killing in the palace! The Shence Army has come killing out of the palace, killing everyone they see. The people of the Secretariat and the Chancellery have been killed, the chief ministers have fled. The Shence Army is searching the city—searching for the chief ministers and the chief ministers' men—and anyone they see in an official's robe they cut down. The ward gates are all going to be shut. Get your pots in and go back, quick!" The man tore his hand free and ran.

Jingfu had the gruel pots brought in and the monastery gate shut. Lingbao was at the monastery that day going over the accounts. Jingfu wouldn't let him go back to the south end of the ward, but had him stay the night at the monastery, and sent someone to bring He Siniang too. Once the ward gates were shut, nothing in the city streets could be heard; only far off someone shouting, a shout, a pause, another shout, sometimes hooves, sometimes weeping. After dark, fires rose at several places to the north, and the firelight showed red on the clouds.

Salang had been carrying loads at the Uighur warehouse in the West Market that day, and at dusk came back over the ward wall.

"The West Market's been looted. Men of the Shence Army looting, and the ward's young toughs looting along with them. Several hu merchants' shops have been smashed. The He shop's all right; He Qi braced the gate and stood inside with a gate bar right till dark." He crouched by the stove. "On the way back I saw people lying in the streets over by the Imperial City, in official's robes, in clerk's clothes. Some had heads and some didn't. The Jinwu Guard doesn't do anything, and the Shence Army men are riding back and forth with blood on their swords."

He held a bowl of gruel and drank a mouthful, couldn't drink any more, and put it down.

That night at the second watch, a man fell off the top of the back-courtyard wall.

Siyuan heard it first. He lived in the room against the wall of the back courtyard, near where the cart for the dead was kept, and slept little at night. He heard a noise on the wall top and thought it was a wild cat, came out on his stick to look, and saw a man lying face down at the foot of the wall, not moving. He called out, and there was no answer. He went over slowly; he couldn't crouch, so he touched the man's shoulder with his stick. The man groaned and raised his head.

It was a young man in his twenties in a black clerk's robe, its front slashed open by something, one arm all blood, blood on his face too, running down from his forehead into his eyes. He blinked at Siyuan. "Where is this?"

"The Daqin Monastery, Yining Ward."

The young man put his head down again. "I know. It's here I meant to come."

Siyuan called out. Jingfu, Lingbao and Joseph came, and Salang too. They carried the young man into Siyuan's room, laid him on the bed, and lit the lamp. Lingbao looked at his arm. It was a sword cut on the forearm; it had not cut through the bone, but the wound was deep, and blood kept seeping out. The one on his forehead was from a fall; he had struck it on the tiles of the wall top coming over. Lingbao sent Salang to heat water and Joseph to fetch medicine from the medicine chest, washed his own hands, and sat by the bed washing the wound; at each wipe the young man clenched his teeth. When it was washed, medicine put on and bound with cloth, the young man could speak.

"My name is Lü, Lü Shao. I'm a copyist in the Secretariat. This morning early I was copying documents in the Secretariat, and when the trouble in the palace started I knew nothing of it. All at once the Secretariat's door was burst open and a crowd of Shence Army men came in, cutting down everyone they saw. Some copyists and I jumped out of a back window and ran south with the crowd, squeezed out of the Daming Palace round about the Jianfu Gate. There were Shence Army men in the streets too. We split up and ran. I ran into a crooked lane and hid in someone's woodpile till dark, and went over several ward walls, and came over this one."

He panted a few breaths.

"I know this monastery. My grandfather was Lü the Fourth of the Changle Ward, and my grandfather's third brother was Lü Xiuyan, who wrote out the stele for this monastery. When I was small my grandfather brought me once to see the stele, pointed at that line at its foot, and said: this your great-uncle wrote, and your great-uncle wouldn't take one coin. Today, running west, there was nothing in my head at all; I only thought of running here. I don't know why."

"Rest now. Don't talk any more," said Lingbao.

"One more thing," said Lü Shao. "About the chief ministers I know nothing at all. But the Shence Army men don't care about that; when they see a clerk's robe they cut it down. If I'm found here, the monastery will suffer for it too. I'll rest till daylight and go by myself."

"Once it's light, where will you go? The men in the streets are still searching."

Lü Shao turned his head to the wall.

The Shence Army came just before dawn. First there was a stir at the ward gate; the ward guard opened it; a troop of horsemen came into the ward, hooves striking the ward street, beat by beat, pounding on every door. When a door was opened they went in and searched, came out, and pounded on the next. Jingfu stood inside the monastery gate listening to the hooves come closer, house by house. Lingbao stood beside him; Joseph, Elijah, Qushen, the Shi brothers and Salang were standing in the courtyard. Siyuan was still in the room in the back courtyard, keeping watch over Lü Shao.

"Uncle Lingbao, do we hide him or not?"

"You're the Director."

"I know I'm the Director. I'm asking what you think, Uncle Lingbao."

"The man's in the back courtyard, hidden in Siyuan's room; they come in and search, and they find him. Hide him behind the scripture cabinet, hide him in the hall—they find him all the same; the monastery is only so big," said Lingbao. "And even if he could be hidden—when they ask you whether there's an outsider in the monastery, do you answer yes, or no?"

"The monastery's rule: no lies."

"There you are. That rule is older than my Pa's generation. My Pa never lied all his life—not before Lord Guo the Director, not before the Son of Heaven."

"Then hand him over?"

"I didn't say hand him over," said Lingbao. "The man came over the wall, our people carried him into the room, our people bound his wound. Hand him over, and it's our people sending him under the sword. That I won't do either."

"Not hide him, and not hand him over—then what?"

"I don't know," said Lingbao. "Director, go and open the gate."

The pounding reached the monastery gate. Jingfu drew back the bar and opened the gate.

Outside stood a dozen-odd men, all mounted, in the dress of the Shence Army, carrying torches; the torchlight made even the lamp at the gate look dim. At their head was a squad leader of forty or so, not tall, with an old scar on his face running from under his left ear down to his jaw.

Jingfu knew him at a glance.

That scar he knew. When he came into the monastery at five, there was a boy in the back courtyard two years older, called Gou'er, Doggy; Doggy took him climbing the jujube tree and stealing sesame cakes from the stove. The year Doggy was twelve he fought with a dog of the ward and the dog bit his face, and it was Jingfu who ran crying to fetch Uncle Lingbao, and while Uncle Lingbao sewed it up, he held Doggy's hands down. Later, at fifteen, Doggy went into the Shence Army, and on the day he left knocked his head at the monastery gate to Uncle Siyuan, and after that never came back.

Jingfu stood inside the threshold looking at the scar. His mouth opened, and he did not call out. He thought: if he called Doggy once, this troop of horsemen would go. What he called would be a name, not a lie; but to call it would be to trade an old fondness for a man's life. When he opened this gate today, it was to tell them the truth. He swallowed the word back.

The squad leader, on his horse, looked in through the gate and saw a crowd of men in black robes standing in the courtyard.

"What monastery are you?"

"The Daqin Monastery."

"A hu temple. Did any outsider come in last night? Several clerks ran out of the Secretariat; someone saw them run west and go over ward walls."

The torchlight shone on Jingfu's face.

"Yes," he said. "Last night at the second watch a man fell off the top of the back-courtyard wall, with a sword cut on his arm. The monastery's people carried him into a room and bound his wound. He's inside."

The squad leader stood stock still. The soldiers behind him stood still too; one had already jumped down from his horse and was walking toward the gate. He took two steps and looked back at the squad leader.

"What did you say?"

"He's inside," said Jingfu. "The monastery's rule is no lies. Your Honour asks, so I say: he's inside."

"Then hand him over."

"I won't. The monastery's people carried him in; the monastery's people bound his wound. Hand him over, and it's the monastery's people sending him under the sword."

"You won't hand him over—I'll go in and take him myself."

"If Your Honour goes in, I won't stop you. The gate is open."

The squad leader got down from his horse and walked toward the gate. As he reached the threshold, from over by the back courtyard someone came out on a stick.

It was Siyuan. He came out of the back courtyard, along the gallery, into the middle of the courtyard, walking very slowly, the stick striking the ground beat by beat. He reached Jingfu's side, stopped, raised his head, and looked at the squad leader. His eyes were no longer good. In the torchlight he squinted a good while, and when he saw the scar, saw the jaw below the scar, his mouth opened.

"Doggy."

The squad leader stopped.

"Doggy—you're Doggy," said Siyuan. "In the winter of the fourteenth year of Zhenyuan I picked up a child by the moat outside the Kaiyuan Gate, three or four years old, frozen to his last breath, his mother dead beside him, her ragged padded jacket over him. I put the child on the cart for the dead and pushed him back and raised him in the back courtyard till he was twelve. The child fought with a dog of the ward and the dog bit his face, from under the ear right down to the jaw, a bloody mess, and Lingbao sewed it up. Eleven stitches. While it was being sewn the child didn't cry; he bit on a piece of cloth."

Leaning on his stick, he shuffled half a step forward.

"The year the child was fifteen, I got the Mi family to send him into the Shence Army. When the child left he said, Uncle, when I've made something of myself I'll come back and see you. He never came back once," he said. "Doggy, that scar on your face—Lingbao sewed it. Do you remember?"

The torches roared in the wind, and everyone in the courtyard stood still. The squad leader stood at the threshold with his hand on his sword hilt, kept it there, and did not draw. He looked at Siyuan, and then at the old man behind Siyuan. Lingbao was looking at him too, leaning his head forward, squinting, searching for something.

The squad leader's hand came off the sword hilt. He turned and said to the soldiers behind him, "This place has been searched. No one. Move on."

The soldier who had already dismounted said, "Squad leader, he said the man's inside."

"What he said is nonsense. An old monk in a hu temple, gone muddled—you'd listen to him?"

The squad leader swung up onto his horse, turned it, and went off along the ward street. His soldiers followed him, a dozen-odd horses, the torches bobbing away, to pound on the next door. At the corner of the ward street the squad leader reined in, turned his head back toward the monastery gate, and the torchlight shone on his face; the scar was white. He turned his head away and went on.

Jingfu stood at the gate; the gate was still open. Behind him, Siyuan could no longer stand on his stick; his knees gave way, and Lingbao and Joseph caught him one on each side.

Lü Shao stayed seven days at the monastery. On the third day the city stopped searching; on the fifth the Secretariat posted a notice telling the scattered clerks to report back to the Secretariat, with no charges. On the seventh, the wound on his arm had scabbed and he could move it, and he said he would go back to the Secretariat.

The night before he left, he went alone with a lamp to look at the stele in the front courtyard. He stood before the stele and looked from its head to its foot, and when he reached the lowest line—Written by Lü Xiuyan, Gentleman for Court Discussion, formerly Administrator of Granaries of Taizhou—he raised the lamp a little and reached out to touch the characters.

That night Jingfu, having lit the lamp at the gate, saw a light by the stele and went over. Lü Shao heard his steps and turned.

"Director, that morning, why didn't you say no? Say one no, and the Shence Army men would have gone. You said yes; if Elder Siyuan hadn't come out, I'd have been dragged off, and the monastery would have suffered for it too."

"The monastery's rule is no lies."

"That's your rule. You believe; I don't."

"I know you don't. Your great-uncle didn't believe either, in his day; when he wrote out the stele he said, you believe, I don't," said Jingfu. "A rule is kept by those who believe; whether you believe has nothing to do with it. That morning when I opened the gate, I didn't know who was outside, and I didn't know Uncle Siyuan would come out. I knew only two things: no lies, no handing him over. A third thing I couldn't think of, so I opened the gate. When I'd opened it I recognized Doggy, and I didn't call out. I was afraid that if I called, the gate wouldn't have been opened clean."

"And if Elder Siyuan hadn't come out?"

"Then I don't know," said Jingfu. "To this day I haven't worked out what I should have done if Uncle Siyuan hadn't come out."

Lü Shao left the next day. He stood a moment at the monastery gate. "Director, I don't believe in your Lord. But I'll remember this monastery. From now on, if the monastery has any business where a copyist of the Secretariat could be of use, send someone to the Secretariat to find me."

"Good."

When Lü Shao reached the corner of the ward street he turned back and made a salute with crossed hands.

Siyuan died that winter. From that morning on he could not get out of bed; his knees swelled like two steamed buns, and at night they hurt so that he couldn't sleep. Lingbao put medicine on them all winter, and they did not improve. He lay in that room in the back courtyard and said that at night he could hear the axle of the cart creaking in the wind.

In the twelfth month he called the younger Shi brother. "The cart—from now on, you push it."

"Uncle, I won't push it well."

"Pushing a cart doesn't need pushing well; it needs pushing at all," said Siyuan. "You couldn't recite the scripture, and the assistant marked you with a cross. Not being able to recite scripture doesn't matter. If you can push the cart—those in the city who freeze to death, you push them out, bury them, set up a stake, write their names; if you can't write a name, write which ward they died in, and which day. That is scripture."

The younger Shi brother knelt before the bed and wept. "Uncle, I'll push it."

Siyuan died in the night of the twentieth of the twelfth month, with Lingbao by his bed and He Siniang at the door boiling a pot of water. Before he died he called out once for Elder Narsai, and once for Lord Mi. Lord Mi was his Pa; all his life he had called his Pa Lord Mi, as the monastery's people did.

The funeral was three days later. Lingbao presided and broke the bread, very slowly, his hands trembling all the while. The Mi family came; the aide-de-camp brought his household, and Qiniang came too. It was the first time in five years since she married into the Zheng family that she had come in at the monastery gate. She wore plain mourning white and stood on the women's side beside He Siniang, a red cord round her neck with something tied to it, tucked inside her collar, out of sight.

Siyuan's coffin was laid on the cart for the dead. The younger Shi brother pushed, his elder brother steadying it behind, out of the monastery gate, into the ward street, toward the Kaiyuan Gate.

At the ward gate stood a man in the dress of the Shence Army, not mounted, standing alone, with a scar on his face running from under his left ear down to his jaw. When the cart reached him, he stepped forward.

"Brother, let me."

The younger Shi brother looked at him and gave way. He took the shafts and pushed the cart out of the Kaiyuan Gate.
