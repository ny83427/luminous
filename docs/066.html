Volume Ten　Morning Praises

Chapter 66　Siyuan

In the seventh month of the ninth year of Dahe, another dispatch came from the Commissioners of Merit's inspectorate. This time it was not only the register to be counted; there was to be a purge. The dispatch said that by edict, of the monks and nuns of the empire many were false and spurious, consuming and harming the living; the chief officials of each place and the Commissioners of Merit's office were to examine them monastery by monastery; monks and nuns must recite so many sheets of the scripture of their own calling, and those who failed were to be returned to lay life; the old and the sick to be granted some leniency. The people of the West Market said this was Li Xun's idea—Li Xun, newly in favour, not old, with a loud voice—who told the Son of Heaven that half the Buddhist monks and nuns were dodging service and half were stealing taxes; the Son of Heaven heard, and sent down an edict. When the monastery's people heard, the first thing they thought of was that business at Sizhou, and they all said this time it was deserved: those who'd bought certificates at Sizhou ought to be purged. When they had said it, they thought of themselves: the twenty on the register were monks too.

That year morning devotion numbered twenty-seven; in these years the Kangs' old uncle, the carpenter who whittled crosses, had died; the Luo Twelfth had moved to Luoyang with his mother; and this summer another old believer had died of the heat, the widow at the west end of the ward who sold needles and thread, past seventy, who died beside her own stall still clutching a hank of thread. Lingbao had left the register and lived at the south end of the ward; every day before light he walked in from the ward street; at the offering once in seven he broke the bread as before and kept the accounts as before; he was sixty-eight; the hands that broke the bread shook worse than a few years before; Jingfu sometimes stood beside him and steadied that glass bowl for him. He Siniang came with him every day and stood on the women's side; in winter she carried a little hand-warmer, and when morning devotion broke up she pressed the hand-warmer into Lingbao's hands.

The day the inspectorate came to examine, it was a judge who came, with two scribes and an officer; a table was set under the colonnade before the hall; the judge sat, the scribes held the roll, the officer stood behind. The judge was forty-odd, his face narrow, unsmiling; he asked first what scripture the monks of this monastery recited; Jingfu said the scriptures of their own teaching, some in Syriac, some in Tang. The judge asked which ones were in Tang; Jingfu had Qushen bring the list of scriptures; Qushen held it out to him; the judge read it through, reached that last line, "not yet translated," and asked what it meant; Jingfu said those were not yet finished; the judge said: not finished—do they count as scriptures? Jingfu said they did: at the Patriarchate they were scriptures; in Chang'an they were scriptures not yet translated. The judge asked nothing more and had the scribes call them one by one by the roll; whoever was called stood before the table and recited a passage of scripture; whoever could recite it, the scribe drew a circle against his name; whoever could not, a cross.

The first called was Joseph. Joseph stood before the table and recited a psalm in Syriac, fast and loud; when he had finished the judge looked at the scribe and the scribe looked at the judge; neither understood; the judge said: what was that? Joseph said in Tang that it was a psalm, the twenty-third, the Lord is my shepherd. The judge asked: did you get it wrong? Joseph said no. The judge said: how would he know you didn't get it wrong? Joseph stood there, thought, and said: then Your Honour can call Elijah; let Elijah recite it once more; if the two of them recite the same, it isn't wrong. The judge looked at him once and said: good—call Elijah. Elijah came and recited it once, a little slower; the judge listened, could not understand, but the two recitations were about the same length and about the same tune, and he had the scribe draw two circles.

Qushen recited the Sutra of Profound Peace and Joy, a long passage, not one character wrong; when he reached the line "as a mirror reflects a thing: the thing gone, the image vanishes," the judge raised his head and asked: is this your scripture? Qushen said it was. The judge said: this line sounds like the Daoists. Qushen said the scripture was translated by the Great Worthy Jingjing; the Great Worthy said there were only these characters in the language of the Tang, and borrowed them; when he borrowed, he put a dot beside them; later, when someone found characters of their own, they'd change them. The judge said: where are the dots? Qushen said: the dots are on the rolls in the scripture room; reciting, you can't recite the dots. The corner of the judge's mouth moved, and he had the scribe draw a circle.

When Siyuan was called, he came on his stick to stand before the table. He was sixty-five that year, had pushed the corpse cart forty-odd years; his back was bent and his knees ruined; in winter he could not get out of bed, and in summer could only shuffle slowly; he shuffled to the table, steadied himself, and recited the passage he had recited all his life, which Elder Narsai had taught him long ago, from the books of the Law: the stranger, the fatherless and the widow, thou shalt not afflict them; if thou afflict them, and they cry unto me, I will surely hear their cry. He recited a few lines and stopped; the next lines he could not remember; he stood there on his stick, his lips moving. Jingfu stood by a pillar of the colonnade and did not prompt him; the judge waited, the scribe waited with his brush, everyone under the colonnade waited. Siyuan, on his stick, shifted his feet once, still could not remember, began again from the start, reached the same place, and stopped again. He said he was old and couldn't remember; let Your Honour draw a cross.

The judge looked at him and asked: what do you do in this monastery? Siyuan said: push a cart. The judge asked: what cart? Siyuan said: the cart for the dead; those in the city who froze to death by the roadside, starved to death, whom no one would see to—he pushed them outside the Kaiyuan Gate and buried them and set up a stake; for those with names he wrote the name; for those without he wrote which ward they died in and which day. The judge asked how many years he had pushed it; Siyuan said he took over the cart in the seventh year of Zhenyuan, after Elder Narsai died, and pushed it till now, forty-four years; he couldn't push it any more; lately young men of the ward helped push and he walked holding the shaft. The judge asked how many he had buried; Siyuan said he'd never counted; the monastery's accounts had it; Lingbao knew. The judge did not go to ask Lingbao; he looked at Siyuan, looked a while, had the scribe draw a circle, and said: the old and the sick to be granted some leniency—that's what the dispatch says.

When they had finished calling that day: twenty men, nineteen circles, one cross; the cross was drawn against the name of the Shi Second, who as a child had learned few characters and could not recite the Syriac scriptures, nor all of the Tang. The judge said he would take the roll back; how the commissioners' office would dispose of it, they must wait for the dispatch. The scribes put away the roll; the officer behind yawned; they left. That night the Shi Second did not sleep; he sat on the well curb, and his brother sat with him till light. Rijin came on the third night. In the hermitage south of the city, the people of Wannian county had come to examine too; he held a certificate from Sizhou, and the county sheriff, seeing it, said at once it was spurious and had him recite a Buddhist scripture; he couldn't; the old monk, hard of hearing, pleaded for him; the sheriff said: certificates from that Sizhou platform—every one found in the capital counts; a cross was drawn under his name too, waiting for the dispatch to come down ordering him back to lay life. That night he stood beside the lamp outside the monastery gate and told Jingfu: if he went back to lay life he'd go home; at home he'd be a registered householder, and householders must do service; his Pa was dead, his Ma was old; if he went back to lay life, it was just right to go back and look after his Ma. He said he had shaved his head back then to dodge service; now he'd been found out, and he had no grievance. Jingfu asked whether, gone home, he would come to morning devotion. Rijin stood by the lamp rubbing his hands and said he didn't know; he said he still didn't know whether a head that had been shaved could come in at this gate. The monastery's dispatch never came, and Rijin's cross never came to anything either. That autumn great things happened in the city, and the purge was never spoken of again.

What happened in Chang'an on the twenty-first of the eleventh month, the monastery's people learned only at dusk. That morning morning devotion was held as usual, the gruel shed opened as usual, and in the afternoon people suddenly came running along the ward street, running from the north, running past the gruel shed; one ran so hard he knocked over a stack of bowls by the gruel pot, the bowls smashed all over the ground, and he did not stop but ran on. Jingfu caught hold of one he knew and asked what had happened, and the man said: there's killing in the palace; the Shence Army has come killing its way out of the palace, killing everyone it sees; the people of the Secretariat and the Chancellery have all been killed; the chief ministers have fled; the Shence Army is searching the city, searching for the chief ministers and the chief ministers' men, cutting down anyone in an official's robe; the ward gates are about to shut; pack up your pot quick and get back. When he had said it he wrenched free and ran.

Jingfu had the gruel pot packed up and the monastery gate shut. Lingbao was in the monastery that day checking accounts; Jingfu told him not to go back to the south end of the ward but to rest the night in the monastery, and sent to fetch He Siniang as well. Once the ward gate shut, nothing could be heard of the streets of the city; only people shouting in the distance, a shout, a pause, another shout; sometimes horses' hooves, sometimes weeping; till dark; after dark several fires rose in the north, and the firelight showed red on the clouds.

That day Salang was carrying goods in the Uighurs' warehouse in the West Market; at dusk he came back over the ward wall and said the West Market had been looted—looted by the Shence men, and the young toughs of the wards joining in; several hu merchants' shops had been smashed; the He shop was all right; He Qi had braced the door and stood inside it holding a bar till dark. He said on the way back he'd seen people lying in the streets over by the Imperial City, in officials' robes, in clerks' robes, some with heads and some without; the Jinwu guards did nothing; the Shence men galloped back and forth with swords, and blood on the swords. When he had told it, he squatted by the stove with a bowl of gruel in his hands, drank a mouthful, could not drink more, and put it down.

That night, in the second watch, a man fell from the top of the back courtyard wall. Siyuan heard it first. Siyuan lived in the room by the wall in the back courtyard, near where the corpse cart stood; he slept little at night; he heard something on the wall top and thought it a wild cat; he came out on his stick to look, and saw a man lying face down at the foot of the wall, not moving. He called once; there was no answer; he went slowly over; he could not squat, so he touched the man's shoulder with his stick; the man groaned and raised his head. It was a young man, twenty-odd, in a black clerk's robe, its front slashed open by something; one arm was all blood, and there was blood on his face too, running down from his forehead into his eyes; he blinked at Siyuan and asked where this was. Siyuan said: the Daqin Monastery, Yining Ward. When the young man heard it he laid his head down again and said he knew; this was where he meant to come.

Siyuan called for people. Jingfu, Lingbao and Joseph came, and Salang too; they carried the young man into Siyuan's room, laid him on the bed, and lit a lamp. Lingbao looked at his arm: a sword cut, across the forearm, not through the bone, but the gash was deep and the blood kept seeping; the one on his forehead was from the fall, knocked on the tiles of the wall top as he climbed over. Lingbao sent Salang to boil water and Joseph to fetch medicine from the medicine chest; he washed his own hands, sat by the bed, and washed the young man's wounds; at each wash the young man clenched his teeth. When they were washed, the medicine applied, and cloth wound round, the young man could speak.

He said his surname was Lü, his name Lü Shao, a scribe of the Secretariat; this morning early he was copying documents in the office; when the business in the palace began he knew nothing; suddenly the office door was burst open and a crowd of Shence men came in cutting down everyone they saw; he and a few scribes jumped out of a back window, ran south with the crowd, squeezed out of the Daming Palace somewhere about the Jianfu Gate, ran into the street; there were Shence men in the street too; their band split up and ran; he ran into a crooked lane and hid in someone's woodpile till dark, climbed over several ward walls, and climbed over here. He said he knew this monastery; his grandfather was Lü the Fourth of Changle Ward; his grandfather's third elder brother, Lü Xiuyan, was the man who wrote the stele for this monastery; when he was small his grandfather brought him once to see the stele, pointed to the line at its foot, and said: that's your great-uncle's writing; your great-uncle didn't take a single coin. He said today, running west, there was nothing in his head; he only thought of running here, and he didn't know why.

Lingbao said: rest now; don't talk any more. Lü Shao said he had one more thing to say; he said in the office he was only a copyist; of the chief ministers' business he knew nothing; but the Shence men didn't care about that; they cut down anyone in a clerk's robe; if he were found, the monastery would suffer for it; he'd rest till light and go by himself. Lingbao said: once it's light, where will you go? The men are still searching in the streets. Lü Shao turned his head toward the wall.

The Shence men came when the sky was nearly light. First there was a stir at the ward gate; the watchman opened it; a troop of horsemen entered the ward, hooves striking the ward street one after another; they pounded on gate after gate, and when a gate opened they went in and searched, and when they had searched came out and pounded on the next. Jingfu stood inside the monastery gate listening to the hooves come nearer house by house. Lingbao stood beside him; Joseph, Elijah, Qushen, the Shi brothers and Salang all stood in the courtyard; Siyuan was still in the room in the back courtyard, keeping watch over Lü Shao.

Jingfu asked Lingbao: do we hide him or not? Lingbao said: you are the leader. Jingfu said he knew he was the leader; what he was asking was what Uncle Lingbao thought. Lingbao said what he thought was: the man is in the back courtyard, hidden in Siyuan's room; the Shence men come in and search and find him; hide him behind the scripture cabinet, hide him in the hall—they'll find him too; the monastery is only so big. He said: what he thought was, even if he could be hidden, when they ask you whether there's an outsider in the monastery, will you answer yes or no? Jingfu said: the monastery's rule is, we don't lie. Lingbao said: there you are; the rule was set in your grandfather's generation, earlier than your grandfather's generation; the Great Worthy Yisi didn't lie all his life, not before Lord Guo, not before the Son of Heaven. Jingfu said: then hand him over? Lingbao said he hadn't said hand him over. He said: the man came over the wall; our people carried him into the room; our people bound his wounds; hand him over, and it's our people sending him under the blade; that he won't do either. Jingfu said: don't hide him, don't hand him over—then what? Lingbao said he didn't know; he said: Leader, go and open the gate.

The pounding reached the monastery gate. Jingfu drew back the bar and opened the gate. Outside stood a dozen-odd men, all mounted, in Shence Army dress, holding torches; the torchlight made the lamp at the gate look dim. The leader was a squad leader of about forty, not tall, with an old scar across his face from below his left ear all the way to his chin. Jingfu knew him at once. That scar he knew: when he entered the monastery at five, there was a child in the back courtyard two years older, called Gou'er, Doggy; Doggy took him climbing the jujube tree, took him stealing sesame cakes from the stove; the year Doggy was twelve he fought with a dog of the ward and the dog bit his face, and it was Jingfu who ran crying to fetch Uncle Lingbao, and while Uncle Lingbao sewed it he held Doggy's hands down; later, at fifteen, Doggy went into the Shence Army, and the day he left knocked his head to Uncle Siyuan at the monastery gate, and never came back after. Jingfu stood inside the threshold looking at that scar; his mouth opened, and he did not call out. He thought: if he called Doggy, this troop of horsemen would go; what he'd call was a name, not a lie; but calling it, he'd be trading old affection for a man's life; he was opening this gate today to tell them the truth; he swallowed that call back. The squad leader, on horseback, looked in at the gate, saw a crowd of men in black robes standing in the courtyard, and said: what monastery are you? Jingfu said: the Daqin Monastery. The squad leader said: Daqin Monastery—a hu monastery; did any outsider come in last night? Several clerks ran out of the Secretariat; someone saw them run west, over the ward walls.

Jingfu stood inside the threshold, the torchlight on his face. He said: yes. He said: last night in the second watch a man fell from the top of the back courtyard wall with a sword cut on his arm; the monastery's people carried him into a room and bound his wound; the man is inside.

The squad leader was taken aback. The soldiers behind him were taken aback too; one had already jumped from his horse and was walking toward the gate; he took two steps and looked back at the squad leader. The squad leader sat his horse looking at Jingfu and asked: what did you say? Jingfu said: the man is inside; he said the monastery's rule is not to lie; Your Honour asks, so he tells: the man is inside. The squad leader said: then hand him out. Jingfu said he would not hand him out; the man was carried in by the monastery's people, his wound bound by the monastery's people; hand him out, and it's the monastery's people sending him under the blade. The squad leader said: you won't hand him out—I'll go in and take him myself. Jingfu said: if Your Honour goes in, he won't stop you; the gate is open.

The squad leader got down from his horse and walked toward the gate; as he reached the threshold, someone came out from the back courtyard on a stick. It was Siyuan. He came out of the back courtyard, along the colonnade, to the middle of the courtyard, walking very slowly, the stick tapping the ground step by step, walked to Jingfu's side, stopped, raised his head, and looked at the squad leader. He was sixty-five that year, his eyes no longer good; in the torchlight he squinted and looked several times, looked at that scar, at the chin beneath the scar; his mouth opened, and he called: Doggy.

The squad leader stopped. Siyuan said: Doggy—you're Doggy. He said: in the winter of the fourteenth year of Zhenyuan, he found a child by the moat outside the Kaiyuan Gate, three or four years old, frozen to his last breath, his mother dead beside him, covered with his mother's ragged jacket; he put the child on the corpse cart and pushed him back; Elder Narsai was no longer living then; orphans were his business; he raised the child in the back courtyard till he was twelve; the child fought with a dog of the ward and the dog bit his face, bit him from below the ear all the way to the chin, a mess of blood; Lingbao sewed it for him, eleven stitches; while he sewed the child didn't cry, but bit on a piece of cloth. He said the year the child was fifteen he asked the Mi family to put him in the Shence Army; when the child left he said: Uncle, when I've made something of myself I'll come back to see you; and he never came back, not once. He said: Doggy, that scar on your face—Lingbao sewed it; do you remember?

The torches roared in the wind; everyone in the courtyard stood still. The squad leader stood at the threshold, his hand on his sword-hilt, holding it, not drawing; he looked at Siyuan, and then at the old man standing behind Siyuan, Lingbao; Lingbao was looking at him too, his head leaning forward, squinting, as if searching for something. The squad leader's hand came off the sword-hilt. He turned round and said to the soldiers behind him: this house has been searched; no one; go.

The soldier who had already dismounted and was walking toward the gate said: Squad leader, he said the man's inside. The squad leader said: what he said was hu nonsense; an old monk in a hu monastery, gone senile—you'd listen to him? He swung up onto his horse, turned his horse's head, and went off along the ward street, and his soldiers followed him, a dozen-odd horses, the torches swaying away, to pound on the next gate. At the corner of the ward street the squad leader reined in his horse and looked back once at the monastery gate; the torchlight lit his face, and the scar was white; he looked once, turned his head, and went. Jingfu stood at the gate; the gate was still open. Behind him Siyuan could no longer stand on his stick; his knees gave way, and Lingbao and Joseph, one on each side, held him up.

Lü Shao stayed seven days in the monastery. On the third day the searches in the city stopped; on the fifth the Secretariat posted a notice calling the scattered clerks to report back to the office, without charge. On the seventh day the wound on his arm had scabbed and he could move it; he took leave of Lingbao and Jingfu, and said he must go back to the office.

The night before he left, he went alone to the front courtyard to look at the stele. He held a lamp and stood before the stele, looked from the head of the stele to its foot, reached the lowest line—written by Lü Xiuyan, Gentleman for Court Discussion, formerly Administrator for Public Works of Taizhou—raised the lamp a little higher, looked, and put out his hand to touch those characters. That night, when Jingfu had lit the lamp at the gate, he saw a light before the stele and went over; Lü Shao heard his steps and turned his head, and asked Jingfu: that morning, why didn't the leader say no? He said: if the leader had said one no, the Shence men would have gone; the leader said yes; if Elder Siyuan hadn't come out, he'd have been dragged out, and the monastery would have suffered too.

Jingfu said: the monastery's rule is not to lie. Lü Shao said: that's your rule; you believe; he doesn't. Jingfu said he knew you don't believe; your great-uncle didn't believe either; when he wrote the stele he said: you believe, I don't. He said the rule is kept by those who believe; it has nothing to do with whether you believe; that morning, when he opened the gate, he didn't know who was outside, and didn't know Uncle Siyuan would come out; he knew only two things—don't lie, don't hand the man over—and he couldn't think of a third, so he opened the gate; when he opened it he recognized Doggy, and didn't call him; he was afraid that if he called, this gate wouldn't have been opened clean. Lü Shao said: and if Elder Siyuan hadn't come out? Jingfu said: then he doesn't know either; he said that to this day he hasn't worked out what he should have done if Uncle Siyuan hadn't come out.

Lü Shao left the next day. As he left he stood a moment at the monastery gate and told Jingfu he didn't believe in your Lord, but he'd remember this monastery; in future, if the monastery had any need of a copyist in the Secretariat, send someone to the office to find him. Jingfu said: good. Lü Shao went, and at the corner of the ward street turned back and clasped his hands.

Siyuan died that winter. From that morning on he could not get out of bed; his knees swelled like two steamed buns; at night the pain kept him from sleeping; Lingbao put medicine on them all winter, and they did not mend. He lay in that room in the back courtyard, near the corpse cart; he said at night he could hear the cart's axle creaking in the wind. In the twelfth month he called the Shi Second and said: the cart—from now on, you push it. The Shi Second said he couldn't push it well. Siyuan said: pushing the cart needn't be done well; if you can push it, that's enough; he said: you can't recite scripture, the judge drew you a cross—not reciting scripture doesn't matter; if you can push the cart—those in the city who froze to death, push them out, bury them, set up a stake, write the name; if you can't write the name, write which ward they died in and which day; that is scripture. The Shi Second knelt before the bed and wept, and said: Uncle, I'll push it.

Siyuan died on the twentieth of the twelfth month, at night; when he died Lingbao was by his bed and He Siniang was at the door boiling a pot of water. Before he died he called out once for Elder Narsai, and once for Lord Mi; Lord Mi was his Pa; all his life he had called his Pa Lord Mi, as the monastery's people did.

The funeral was three days later; Lingbao presided and broke the bread, broke it very slowly, his hands shaking all the while. Some people came from the ward; people came from the Mi family—the officer with his household—and Qiniang came too; it was the first time in her five years married into the Zheng family that she had come in at the monastery gate; she wore plain white and stood on the women's side beside He Siniang; at her neck hung a red cord, and something was tied to the red cord, tucked inside her collar where it could not be seen. Siyuan's coffin was laid on the corpse cart; the Shi Second pushed the cart and his brother steadied it from behind; they pushed it out of the monastery gate into the ward street, toward the Kaiyuan Gate.

At the ward gate stood a man in Shence Army dress, not on horseback, standing alone, a scar across his face from below his left ear all the way to his chin. When the cart reached him, he stepped up and said to the Shi Second: brother, let me. The Shi Second looked at him, and let him. He took the shafts of the cart and pushed it out through the Kaiyuan Gate.
