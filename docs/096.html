Appendix 8　Patriarchs of the Church of the East

The book often speaks of "the Patriarchate" and "the Patriarch." The Patriarchate means the headquarters of the Church of the East, at first in Seleucia-Ctesiphon, the capital of the Persian Empire, and from about 775 in Baghdad, the new city of the Abbasids. The patriarch was the head of this church; his formal title in Syriac was "Catholicos-Patriarch of the East," and the Syriac on the stele calls him "Father of fathers."

The patriarch was elected by the bishops, and in the Islamic period also had to be confirmed by the caliph. So the succession often involved the court, the Christian physicians and secretaries of the palace, and the interests of the great metropolitan sees, and was bitterly fought over. Shapur in this book loses an arm and later dies at an assassin's hand against exactly this background of struggle for the seat.

This appendix first lists the patriarchs of the seventh to ninth centuries relevant to the book, then introduces in turn the few who appear or are mentioned in it.

Two more are connected with the beginning and end of the book: Yohannan V (r. c. 1000–1011), patriarch when the Kerait were baptised in the Epilogue; and Yahballaha III (r. 1281–1317), a patriarch born in northern China, discussed in section 7 below.

2. Ishoyahb II (r. 628–645)

When Alopen came to Chang'an, the patriarch was Ishoyahb II.

He was an able diplomat. Soon after his election, just as the decades-long war between Persia and Eastern Rome had come to an end, the Persian queen Boran sent him on an embassy to Constantinople to make peace with the emperor Heraclius. He set out the faith of the Church of the East before Heraclius, and it is said that the emperor, having heard it, judged it no different from his own and received communion with him.

In the years of his reign the Church of the East took great strides in its mission eastward. Later histories of the Church of the East attribute to him the sending of missionaries to "Sin" (China). The patriarch's seal that Alopen carried to Chang'an (Chapter 46) was made in his time.

3. Jacob II (r. 753–773) and Surin

Around 750 the Islamic world changed dynasties: the Abbasids replaced the Umayyads. The patriarch of the Church of the East, Aba II, died in 751, and for the next two years the seat stood empty while several factions fought for it.

According to Mari, the historian of the Church of the East, a man named Surin, from a noble Persian family, bribed the governor of al-Mada'in (that is, Ctesiphon), who sent his men to stand with drawn swords at the door of the sanctuary and force the bishops to consecrate him. The bishops did it, weeping. Afterwards the elders of the church complained to the caliph al-Saffah, who had the governor flogged and told them to hold a new election. Surin was deposed and Jacob II elected.

But Surin and his kinsmen would not let it rest, and attacked the new patriarch without pause. Later the caliph al-Mansur even threw Jacob II into prison. When Jacob came out, the faithful begged him to send Surin off to Basra as metropolitan, and only then was the matter closed.

In the book, Yisi's elder brother Shapur stands on Jacob II's side. He is attacked in the struggle of 753 and loses his right arm (Chapters 3 and 10); nine years later, when work begins on the new city of Baghdad, the faction that lost that time returns, and at last he dies at an assassin's hand (Chapter 31). Shapur is invented; the turmoil over the succession is real. When the Afterword says "that sword-blow in the book comes from sources like these," this is the affair it means.

4. Hnanisho II: the book's Hnanisho (r. 773–780)

At the end of the Chinese text of the Luminous stele there is a line: "At that time the Lord of the Law, the monk Ningshu, oversaw the Luminous congregations of the East." The Syriac at the foot of the stele also records that when it was raised, the Father of fathers Hnanisho was universal patriarch. Ningshu is the Chinese rendering of Hnanisho.

The histories have left very little about this patriarch. It is known in outline that he was elected around 773; that in his time the patriarch's seat moved from Ctesiphon to Baghdad; and that he died between 778 and 780. News of his death had still not reached Chang'an when the stele was raised in the first month of 781, so what is carved on it is the name of a patriarch no longer living.

The book's Hnanisho is mostly invented:

  • In Chapter 40 he writes back to Yonan, allowing him to be released from his vow of celibacy; his letter says "God has regard to human weakness," sends greetings to A-Lian, and thanks Yonan for keeping the Eastern church alive through the war.
  • Chapter 44 describes his old age: past seventy, after the move to the new city of Baghdad, he still often loses his way by the river.

These are a novelist's inventions.

In Chapter 66, the old patriarch in Baghdad reads that line of Syriac on the side of the stele and says: "When the stele was raised, Hnanisho was already dead." That line is written from the facts on the stele.

5. Timothy I (r. 780–823)

Timothy I was one of the most famous patriarchs in the history of the Church of the East, and reigned for more than forty years.

His election was not creditable. According to the records, he used bribery and deceit, and some of his rivals died in unexplained ways. Joseph, metropolitan of Merv, led a group of bishops in a synod that excommunicated him; he excommunicated Joseph in turn. Joseph appealed to the caliph without success and later converted to Islam. With each side excommunicating the other, things went so far that the Christians of Baghdad rioted in the streets, and only the mediation of the caliph's Christian physician finally calmed it. When Chapter 44 says he "was elected by political skill, and his opponents would not accept it," this is the episode it means.

Yet once he was patriarch, he was an outstanding leader.

  • Debate. Around 782, summoned by the caliph al-Mahdi, he debated the differences between Christianity and Islam with the caliph at court over two days. His answers, gentle and firm, were held up as a model by later generations. Near the end of the debate he used a comparison: truth is like a pearl dropped in a dark house; everyone in the house thinks he has picked it up, and only when day comes do they see who has it in his hand.
  • Mission. He set great store by the mission to the East. He consecrated metropolitans for the Turks of Central Asia and for China, and spoke of consecrating one for Tibet. Fifty-nine of his letters survive, and they often mention monks sent to distant lands who set out with nothing but a staff and a scrip.
  • Learning. He took part in translating the Greek works of Aristotle and others into Arabic, and left writings on law, liturgy and theology.

He lived past ninety, died in 823, and was buried in Baghdad.

Some of the book's Timothy is invented:

  • In Chapter 44 he reads the life of Nestorius and concludes that "one must choose the man who can keep the church alive";
  • In Chapter 53 he writes to Jingjing about the arrangements for the province of China, and Jingjing thinks of making one more journey west, and in the end does not go.

That he consecrated a metropolitan for China is documented; the letter in the book is imagined on that basis.

6. Abraham II (r. 837–850)

When the Luminous Religion was banned in Chang'an in the fifth year of Huichang (845), the patriarch in Baghdad was Abraham II. Here too the sources say little: only that he reigned thirteen years and died in 850, after which the seat was vacant for three years.

In Chapter 66, Salang, Elijah and their party reach Baghdad in the spring of the fourth year of Dazhong, that is, 850, and meet a patriarch old and ill. They report to him the end in Chang'an and hand over their copy of the Syriac names from the side of the stele. That summer the old patriarch dies. The book does not give his name; reckoning by the year, it is Abraham II.

7. Two after the Epilogue

The Epilogue concerns events around 1007. According to the Syriac historian Bar Hebraeus, in that year the metropolitan of Merv wrote to the patriarch Yohannan V in Baghdad, reporting that the king of a people on the steppe had lost his way in the snow and, after being rescued, wished to have his people—two hundred thousand of them—baptised; and asking what they should eat in Lent, since there was no wheat or vegetables on the steppe. The patriarch wrote back that they might drink milk. That passage of the Epilogue follows this record.

More than two hundred years later, the Church of the East had a patriarch who came from the East. His name was Mark; he was an Önggüt, born in northern China, and as a young man he set out from Khanbaliq, the Yuan capital, with his teacher Rabban Sauma, meaning to make the pilgrimage to Jerusalem. When the two reached Baghdad, war kept them from the Holy Land and they stayed. In 1281, when the old patriarch died, Mark was elected patriarch as Yahballaha III. His teacher Sauma was later sent by the Mongol Ilkhan as envoy to Europe, and met the king of France and the Pope of Rome.

From Alopen's coming east to Yahballaha III's going west, six hundred years and more had passed.

The patriarchal line of the Church of the East has continued since, through divisions, to the present day. The patriarch of the Assyrian Church of the East today is Mar Awa III, enthroned in 2021.
