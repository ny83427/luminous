Volume Three　The Pamir Road

Chapter 13　The Saucer Lamp

In the sixth month, Xizhou grew hot.

By day the sun baked the mud walls of the city until they burned; put your hand on one and it was like putting it on a stove. Under the grape trellises people sat fanning themselves, not moving. The people of the church all kept inside the sanctuary, where it was cool and smelled of earth and old wood. At night the wind came off the gravel desert and it was cool again at once, cool enough for a lined robe.

Old Duan had been gone more than a month.

By day the seven elders studied characters with Jingjing, sitting in a row, each with a sand tray before him. Jingjing wrote one character on the board, they traced it in the sand, smoothed it out, traced it again. The grass in the courtyard was pulled clean, the walls were mended, the hired woman brought a pot of broth every noon, and every time Ishaq first said "wan" and then corrected himself to "fan."

One evening Ishaq came to find Jingjing and said that in the daytime there were too many people and he could not take it in; seeing the others write so fast made him anxious, and the more anxious he was the worse he wrote. Could he come by himself in the evening, and learn just a few characters?

Jingjing said yes.

The next evening Babai came to ask as well. On the third, Sabrisho came too. Later the seven arranged an order among themselves, one a night, taking turns.

The side room where Jingjing lived was very small, with only one low table. After dark he lit an oil saucer on the table—a little sesame oil poured into a dish, a wick twisted from rush pith, its flame no bigger than a bean. When an elder came he sat across the table, and by that little light learned a few characters.

Which characters, they chose for themselves.

Ishaq chose medicine, sick and peace.

At the Patriarchate he had charge of the sickroom, and the people he had treated outnumbered the scriptures he had seen. When he wrote 藥, medicine, and came to the 樂 at its bottom, he stopped and asked Jingjing whether this 樂 was the le of happy.

Jingjing said it was.

Ishaq laughed, his belly shaking, and said the Tang people were funny: take medicine and you're happy.

He said that when he was young he had travelled with a caravan to Merv. That year there was sickness in the city of Merv, and a priest wrote to the Patriarchate begging for medicine, and the letter took two months and more. The Patriarchate made up the medicine and sent someone with it, and that took two months and more too. When the medicine arrived, the priest who had written was already buried.

"And the medicine?" Jingjing asked.

"Given to the men who buried him," said Ishaq. "They were sick too."

He bent his head and wrote peace again. It came out crooked; the roof on top, 宀, was too big, and hid the 女 beneath it from sight.

Babai wanted to learn only one character: scripture.

He said he had learned the other characters by day; only this one he wanted to write better than anyone. He had looked after scriptures all his life, had copied scriptures twenty years in the library of the Patriarchate.

He wrote all evening, dozens of scripture. Jingjing was so sleepy he kept nodding off, and Babai was still writing.

As he left, he said that every day he went to look at the three rolls in the cupboard of the church in the north of the city. One the elder Ahad had bought back, two the old bookseller had returned. Every day he went and counted them, as if afraid they would grow legs and run off.

Sabrisho learned time, hour.

He said that at the Patriarchate the praises were said seven times a day, the first before light and the last at night, and before each the wood was struck as a signal, not one stroke amiss. He had kept this at the Patriarchate for more than ten years: when to rise, when to pray, when to sleep, he decided all of it.

Jingjing said that at the monastery in Lingzhou, counting the elder Ahad, there were only three people. Often they could not make up the seven praises. Sometimes the elder Ahad prayed them alone.

Sabrisho heard it and for a long time said nothing. That thick beard covered all the expression on his face, and Jingjing could not tell whether he was angry or not.

After a long while he said: "If they can't make them up, they must say them anyway."

Having said that, he bent his head and wrote, and said nothing else all evening.

On Haddad's night he brought no sand tray, but a roll of paper and a brush. He wanted to learn letter, road and money. He said these three were what travelling men could not do without.

He wrote very fast, and talked as he wrote—how many times he had run between the two rivers and Persia, which city's taxes were heavy, in which city the foreign merchants liked to cheat.

Jingjing asked him how the monastery in Chang'an exchanged letters with the Patriarchate.

"By caravan," said Haddad. "The post horses only run to the edge of the Tang, and beyond the edge you have to give it to merchants. A merchant gets to a city, rests a few days, changes camels, goes on. If all goes well, a letter from Chang'an to the Patriarchate takes the better part of a year. If there's war and the caravans don't go, a letter can sit in some city a year—that happens all the time."

"And the answer?"

"The answer the same. There and back, a year and more at least." Haddad smiled a little. "Your pa wrote that the people of the monastery in Chang'an had scattered; the letter reached the Patriarchate last autumn. By the time we get to Chang'an it'll be another year. A year and more of scattering; who knows how many are left."

Jingjing thought and said: "My uncle said the elder Yonan was in Chang'an before."

"He was," said Haddad. "Bishop of Chang'an. In those days the monastery in Chang'an was bigger than now, more people too. The Patriarchate sent him to look after Chang'an and the other churches in the east. Later the Patriarchate called him back."

"When?"

Haddad thought a moment. "Ten years or so ago. Not long after you were born."

He finished the last stroke of money, blew on the ink, and said: "Your pa was in Chang'an then too. He must have known him."

That night, after Haddad had gone, Jingjing sat before the oil saucer thinking a long time.

Pa had talked about the many people at the monastery in Chang'an. He had talked about the Great Virtue Jilie, about an old deacon who swept the courtyard, about the man who sold flatbread at the monastery gate. Pa had never talked about Yonan.

He thought: Pa must have forgotten. Pa knows too many people.

That evening Gabriel learned light.

He said the fire in his house when he was little had broken out at night, and when he woke the whole room was full of light, bright as day. He ran out with his sister in his arms, and when he reached the door he looked back once—and with that one look the left side of his face was burned.

"So this character," he said, touching the wrinkled half of his face and smiling, "I want to learn."

Narsai was the sixth to come.

He came exactly on time; Jingjing had only just lit the oil saucer when he knocked. He sat down with his back very straight and his two hands on his knees, as if listening to the scriptures in the sanctuary of the Patriarchate.

"I'll learn one character," he said.

"Which one?"

Narsai thought, and said a word in Syriac. Jingjing understood it: it meant a word a man speaks before the Lord and cannot take back.

Jingjing thought a long time and wrote on the board the character 誓, vow.

Narsai looked at it a long time.

"On top is 折, break, as in breaking something off," said Jingjing, pointing. "Underneath is 言, word, as in speaking. When Pa taught me this character he said: a word once spoken is broken off, and can't be taken back."

When he had said it he felt he had not said it quite right, and added: "That's what Pa said. The dictionaries don't necessarily say so."

Narsai did not smile. He said the character over once. Shi. And again. Shi. He said it very heavily, the sound jumping out, the way he read the law.

Then he began to write.

He wrote even more slowly than by day. The first stroke of break, the falling stroke, he wrote several times, and every time it came out like a spear. Jingjing remembered the man he had written by day, like two men fighting, and did not dare say anything, only watched beside him.

By the seventh or eighth he had it written properly at last.

Narsai put down the brush and looked at the character in the sand tray.

"I made my vow at the Patriarchate when I was twenty," he said. "My mother stood outside the sanctuary and wept. She wanted grandchildren."

Jingjing did not know what to say. He knew that the clergy of the Luminous Teaching could marry and have children, not like Buddhist monks; Pa had married Ma. But the vow the elder Narsai had made seemed to be different from other people's.

"Once you've vowed, you must keep it," said Narsai. "A man who can't keep it shouldn't vow."

As he said it his eyes were on the sand tray, not on Jingjing. The flame in the oil saucer jumped, and those two high ridges of his brow threw two deep blocks of shadow on the wall.

He sat a while, then rose, bowed to Jingjing as one bows to a grown man, turned and left.

Jingjing left the vow in the sand tray all night, and did not smooth it out.

The last to come was Yonan.

He came latest. Jingjing thought he was not coming and was about to blow out the lamp when there were two soft knocks at the door.

Yonan came in and sat down across the table. He looked at the oil saucer, then at Jingjing, and smiled a little.

"I'll learn four characters," he said.

"Which four?"

"Lotus cross."

Jingjing was a little surprised. He had been able to say that word since he was three. On the lintel of the monastery in Chang'an, on the gate of the monastery in Lingzhou, under the wooden cross that hung round his own neck—there was always a lotus holding it up. The people of the monastery said it every day, lotus cross, lotus cross, said it like eat rice. In Tang writing it was four characters: 蓮花十字.

"The elder can't write it?"

"No," said Yonan. "There's one carved on the lintel of the monastery in Chang'an. I went in and out under it for years, saw it every day. But I never could write it."

So Jingjing taught him.

十, ten, the cross shape, one stroke across and one down, Yonan learned at once. 字 was not hard either: a roof on top, 子 underneath. For 花, the grass radical with 化 below, he wrote it three times and had it.

The hardest was 蓮, lotus.

Under the grass radical came 連, and the walking radical of 連—a dot, then a turn, then a long trailing sweep to finish—Yonan wrote crooked however he tried. Either the trail was too short or the sweep too long; once it trailed off a long way, like a broken rope.

"The walking radical goes like this," said Jingjing, and took his hand and guided it once through the sand. "Dot first, then turn, then draw it across. When you draw it, don't move your wrist."

Yonan's hand was very thin, the tendons standing out on its back one by one. Holding it, Jingjing felt how cold it was.

Yonan wrote it again himself. He wrote one and smoothed it out; wrote another, smoothed it out. The three characters before it together he had written fewer than ten times; this one lotus he wrote more than twenty.

Jingjing yawned and said: "That's a hard one. It took me a long time to learn it too."

Yonan did not look up. "It is hard," he said.

At last there was one that looked right. Yonan looked at it a long time, and did not smooth it out.

The oil in the saucer was nearly gone, and the flame had shrunk smaller still.

Yonan put down the brush and asked Jingjing a few things. What was the monastery in Chang'an like now? Was the great locust tree at the gate still there? How big was the monastery in Lingzhou? Were Yisi's legs sound, did he cough at night?

Jingjing answered each one.

Yonan listened, nodding, and suddenly said: "I saw you once."

"When?"

"When you were this big." Yonan held up his two hands, a little less than a foot apart. "In the monastery in Chang'an. You were asleep."

Jingjing thought it was funny. He could not imagine what he had looked like so small.

"Did I cry?"

"No," said Yonan. "You slept the whole time."

"Pa never told me," said Jingjing.

Yonan paused.

"He was busy then," he said.

He rose and brushed a little sand off his clothes. Jingjing took down the four characters he had written on the board—they were on a sheet of paper—and gave them to him to take back and practise from.

Yonan took it, glanced at it, folded it, and put it inside his robe.

"Thank you," he said. He said it in Tang speech, very slowly; but not one sound went crooked.

That night Jingjing lay down and could not sleep.

He put his hand inside his coat and took out the knotted cord the elder Ahad had given him under the mulberry tree in Balkh. Twisted wool, black and shining.

He turned one knot.

"Lord, have mercy on Pa."

He turned another.

"Lord, have mercy on the elder Ahad."

"Lord, have mercy on Uncle Duan. Have mercy on Uncle Xu."

He went down them one by one. The elder Ishaq, the elder Babai, the elder Sabrisho, the elder Haddad, the elder Gabriel, the elder Narsai.

At the last one he paused.

"Lord, have mercy on the elder Yonan."

He thought: the elder Yonan has seen me asleep, and Pa never told me. He thought about it a while and could not work it out, and then thought: when we get back to Chang'an, I'll ask Pa, and then I'll know.

By the time he had turned the last knot, the sky in the east showed a little greyish white.

East was Fengxiang. Pa was at Fengxiang.

The next morning Jingjing went into the sanctuary and passed by Yonan's sand tray.

In the sand were written four characters: 蓮花十字, lotus cross.

The sand under the lotus was shallower than elsewhere, as if it had been smoothed out many times. In the topmost lotus, the walking stroke trailed neither too short nor too long, just right.

Jingjing looked at it and felt rather pleased with himself. He thought: that character, I taught him.
