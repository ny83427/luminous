Volume Eleven　Huichang

Chapter 71　Qushen

In the spring of the fourth year of Huichang a prophecy went round the city.

It was said to be what the Daoists in the palace had told the Son of Heaven: "The eighteen sons of Li—before their glorious fortune is spent, a Son of Heaven in black robes will rule the state."

The eighteen sons of Li, put together, make the character Li—it meant the Son of Heaven's house. In black robes meant people who wore black. Buddhist monks wore black. The Daoists meant that one day a man in black robes would come and sit in the Son of Heaven's seat.

People in the West Market said that when the Son of Heaven heard this he couldn't sleep at night, and the next day issued a decree: monks were not to walk in the streets; a monk leaving his monastery must first report to the monastery's Three Officers, the Three Officers to the inspectorate, and only with the inspectorate's leave might he go out; after the bell, no monk was to spend the night in another monastery.

When this reached the monastery, the monastery's people all looked down at the robes they wore.

The monastery's robes were black. Since Elder Alopen's time they had been black.

"The monks at the Patriarchate all wear black," said Joseph. "Black doesn't show dirt, it wears well. It's nothing to do with the Tang's Son of Heaven."

"That prophecy speaks of Buddhist monks," said Lingbao. "But when the Tang look at us, it's the same black as when they look at Buddhist monks."

That year twenty stood at the morning. At the beginning of spring An Yan died.

He died in the first month. In the last days before he died he still had himself helped to stand the morning; on the last day he did not come. When the morning broke up Jingfu went to see him. He was lying on his sleeping place in the west wing with his eyes open, covered with an old outer robe whose collar was worn through. It was Jingjing's robe.

"The snow was heavy that year," said An Yan. "Master Jingjing tramped through the snow to the south of the city, stood below the well curb, and said his piece. When he'd said it he turned and went, and didn't tell anyone to come back with him."

Jingfu sat down at the edge of the bed.

"I was standing in the snow stripped to the waist, watching his back go toward the garden gate. I ran a few steps and caught up. He turned and saw me, took off this robe, and put it round me." An Yan's hand felt the robe. "All my life I've been coming back, following this robe."

After a long while he said, "I'm cold."

Jingfu put another quilt on him.

"Not that kind of cold."

He closed his eyes. Jingfu laid the old outer robe over him, and it was buried with him.

With An Yan dead, a place in the quota came empty. Of the ten who had left the monastery, all who could come back onto the register had come; of the few left, some had married, some had made homes outside, some had died, and there was no one who could come back to fill it. Jingfu reported to the inspectorate, and the aide-de-camp made a stroke in the register and did not ask who would fill it.

That summer the Son of Heaven issued another decree: in all the realm, mountain cells, hermitages, common Buddhist chapels, charitable wells and village fasting halls without an imperial name-board and under two hundred bays were to be torn down without exception, and the monks and nuns in them ordered back to lay life and entered on the registers as households.

The day the decree came down, people in the city said the little hermitages in the Southern Mountains, several hundred of them, were all to be torn down.

The inspectorate's men came into the city to inspect in the seventh month. Most of the Buddhist monasteries in the city had name-boards, and those with name-boards were not torn down; what was torn down were the little Buddhist chapels people of the wards had built themselves, the little niches at lane mouths with a stone Buddha in them, the charitable wells with a little room put up beside them for reciting scripture. The inspectorate sent several parties, and counted ward by ward.

On the day they reached the Yining Ward, the one who came was an old palace official, past sixty, thin, a little stooped, in a green robe, riding a donkey, with two clerks and an aide-de-camp behind him. He looked as he went, and wherever he saw a Buddha niche at a door, he had the clerks note it.

When he reached the monastery gate the gruel shed's pot was open. Jingfu stood by the pot ladling gruel, and the queue stretched from the pot to the corner of the ward street.

The old official halted his donkey and looked at the pot, and at the people in the queue.

"This gruel shed—how many years has it been open?"

"It opened in the Dali years. First in the West Market; in the fourth year of Zhenyuan it moved to the ward street. Seventy-odd years."

"How many pots a day?"

"Two, before. Since the year before last, one."

The old official said nothing, and raised his head to look at the name-board over the monastery gate. The wood was old, the gilt of the three characters Daqin Monastery peeled. The old people of the ward said that in the Tianbao years the Son of Heaven had granted a name-board; which board it was, and whether the one hanging now was that one, no one could say any more.

"Does this monastery have a name-board?"

"It does. The name was changed in the Tianbao years, by decree."

"With a name-board, it isn't torn down."

He turned his donkey and went off toward the east end of the ward.

Jingfu asked Headman Liu afterward who that old official was.

"From the Right Street Commissioners of Merit's inspectorate, surnamed Yang. He's been at the inspectorate decades. Doesn't talk much; handles the inspections. The palace people, when they see him, call him Old Yang."

The news from Zhouzhi came in the eighth month.

The one who brought it was a young man who had run from a mountain valley south of Zhouzhi County. He had run two days; the straw sandals on his feet were worn to shreds, and as soon as he reached the monastery gate he knelt before Jingfu.

"Director, the house is gone."

In that mountain valley lived the seven households Elder Ishaq had baptized in the Dali years, grown since with births to ten-odd households. In the valley they had built three earthen rooms, with a blue brick set in the lintel and a cross carved on the brick, and at the offering once in seven they worshipped in the middle room. Every year the monastery in Chang'an sent someone once, with bread and scriptures. In Jingjing's time it sent Lingbao; later it sent Jingfu.

"The inspectorate's men came to Zhouzhi to inspect, came to our valley, and asked what the three rooms were. We said they were rooms for worship. They said rooms for worship were fasting halls, and without an imperial name-board—tear it down," said the young man. "When they'd torn it down they prised the brick out of the lintel and threw it into the gully."

"And the brick?"

"We went down into the gully at night to look for it. We looked all night and found it. A corner broke off in the fall; the cross is still there," said the young man. "We've set the brick up under the old oak tree at the head of the village and wedged it steady with stones. At the offering once in seven the ten-odd households kneel under the tree."

He raised his head.

"Director, from now on, when we worship under the tree, does it count as worship?"

"It counts," said Jingfu. "Aluohe doesn't live in a house. The house is torn down; Aluohe is still there. When you kneel under the tree, you're worshipping. I'll come at the end of the month."

The young man stood up and asked again, "From now on, will there be Chang'an monasteries torn down too?"

"The Chang'an monasteries have name-boards. Those with name-boards aren't torn down."

"I heard the inspectorate's men say that even with name-boards, not necessarily."

Before Jingfu went to Zhouzhi, he talked over the matter of the bread with Lingbao in the small room in the back courtyard.

"Before, once a year Zhouzhi had an offering once in seven with bread—the time the monastery sent someone," said Jingfu. "The one sent was a priest—you, Uncle Lingbao—and the bread was consecrated in their room and broken in their room. Now you can't walk a hundred-odd li of mountain road, and if I go, I can't consecrate. If I go, all I can do is lead them in reciting the scripture, and when we reach the offering bread, stop, empty-handed."

Lingbao sat in the corner and said nothing.

"I want to take bread," said Jingfu. "Consecrated in the hall in Chang'an, and I'll take it."

"The offering bread doesn't leave the hall door," said Lingbao. "That rule Brother set in the sixth year of Zhenyuan, after the Kang child died. Fifty-odd years, and it's never been broken once."

"I know."

"Brother set that rule for fear people would carry the bread home and use it as medicine, as a charm, soak it in well water for a child to drink. You'd be taking bread to Zhouzhi, to the place where they worship. Their house is torn down; the oak tree is their hall. I can see that," said Lingbao. "But what I can see isn't written in the rule Brother set. I can't change this rule on Brother's behalf."

Mother Kang was sitting at the door of the small room that day, waiting to go back to the south end of the ward with He Siniang. She heard, and stood up on her bamboo stick.

"Your Reverence, let me put in a word."

The two of them turned their heads.

"That rule was set because of me," said Mother Kang. "I carried the bread home and soaked it in well water and the child died, and that's when Master Jing set it. These fifty-odd years, every offering once in seven when I've taken the bread, I've eaten it on the spot; not once have I carried it out."

She stood at the door on her bamboo stick.

"You ask me, and I say take it. In my day I carried the bread home wanting my child to live. Those people in Zhouzhi want the bread so they'll know for themselves they're still of Aluohe's household. The two aren't the same. If Master Jing were here, he'd see too that the two aren't the same."

Lingbao reached out for the glass bowl on the table, felt its rim, felt it all round.

"Then take it," he said. "In the accounts I'll make an entry: Eighth month of the fourth year of Huichang: bread leaves the hall door, to Zhouzhi, carried by the Director Jingfu; the old woman Mistress Kang judged it right; the priest Lingbao concurred. After this, if anyone says this rule's been broken, let them turn up the accounts and they'll know how it was broken, why it was broken, and who said yes."

Jingfu left the city on the twenty-sixth of the eighth month.

He carried a bundle on his back. In it was a small earthenware jar, its mouth sealed with wax, and in it the bread Lingbao had consecrated at the offering once in seven the day before, broken into twenty-odd pieces; and a small flask of wine and a few rolls of scripture. He went by the road he had walked a dozen times: out by the Yanping Gate, south-west, across the Feng, through Hu County, to the county town of Zhouzhi, then south into the mountains. The mountain road was narrow, slope on one side and gully on the other. He walked two days and rubbed two blisters on his soles.

In other years when he came, he could see the roofs of the three earthen rooms from far off. This time, when he reached the valley mouth, what he saw was an empty patch of ground, grass growing on the heaps of earth from the fallen mud walls, and a few half-burned rafters thrown down beside them.

The old oak at the head of the village was still there. At its roots stood the blue brick with its broken corner, and in front of the brick a few earthenware bowls with wild flowers stuck in them.

The ten-odd households had all come—old and young, the grandsons and great-grandsons of those first seven households. Some spoke Tang thickly and knew not one sentence of Syriac; they could only say Lord, have mercy, said as four words of Tang.

Jingfu stood under the oak and they knelt before the blue brick. He led them in reciting the scripture, the Tang scripture the Master had translated. When they came to the offering bread he opened the earthenware jar, took out the bread piece by piece, and put it in an earthenware bowl.

"This bread was consecrated in the hall in Chang'an, consecrated by the Great Worthy Lingbao," he said. "Hands haven't been laid on me; I can't consecrate. I've only brought it. I've brought it so that you'll know that at the offering once in seven in the hall in Chang'an, there's a share for you."

He handed it out one by one.

When he reached an old woman, she took the bread and held it in her palm and did not eat it, and looked up at him.

"Is the hall in Chang'an still there?"

"It's still there."

The old woman put the bread in her mouth, chewed, and swallowed it.

"Then it's well," she said. "Our hall is gone. If the hall in Chang'an is still there, it's well."

That day he stayed a night in the valley, sleeping in a family's kitchen. In the night the wind rose, and the oak leaves rustled all night. The next day he started back, and at the valley mouth looked back. The wild flowers in those bowls had been blown over by the wind, and a little child ran over and stood them up again stem by stem.

Qushen left that autumn.

He was forty-three that year; after he came back onto the register he had lived at the monastery fourteen years more. Jingjing had given him the name from put away the extreme, put away the extravagant, put away the excessive, to rein in his temper; he had remembered it all his life, and his temper was still quick, and when he was roused his words came out like beans poured from a sack. These years he had read the accounts in the scripture room for Lingbao and copied scripture for the monastery. The Tang scriptures Jingjing had translated in his day—the Sutra of Profound Peace and Joy, the Scripture on Proclaiming the Origin, the Hymn of the Three Majesties, and a few more—all their copies were in his hand. The rolls in the scripture room were too old; he made a new copy, put the old one away, and brought out the new one for use. He wrote an upright hand, and the little dot beside every borrowed word he put in just as it was, not missing one.

Lu Shouchong had come to the monastery several times that year. The first time he came with a rope to measure the ground; when he came after that, it was to find Qushen.

"I heard at the temple that the Daqin Monastery has a copyist with a fine hand who's read the Laozi," said Lu Shouchong, standing at the scripture-room door. "The temple has a scripture it would like you to come and copy."

Qushen went, copied half a month, and on the evening he came back went to find Jingfu in the back courtyard.

"Daoist Lu's register from measuring the ground—I saw it at the temple," he said. "It was lying open on the table beside the scripture room; I could see it whenever I raised my head from copying. There was a plan of the Yining Ward on it, and our monastery's piece circled in red, and beside it written: halls so many bays, courtyards so many mu, one well. I didn't turn the pages."

Jingfu listened and said nothing.

"And another thing," said Qushen. "Of the scriptures at the Tongzhen Temple, I can understand half. The half I understand—many of the words are the same as in the scriptures Master Jingjing translated. Non-action, no desire, clarity and stillness, mystery and wonder. As I copied, I felt as if I were copying our own scripture."

"Those words are borrowed. When the Master borrowed them, he put dots beside them."

"I know," said Qushen. "But in the temple's scriptures there are no dots beside them. Those words live in their own house there, and live easy. Their scriptures speak in their own words."

That year his speech grew shorter every year. Before, reading the accounts, he would argue with Lingbao as he read—this entry's wrong, that one's too fussy; now he read them through, closed the ledger, and went. On the day An Yan died he stood at the door of the west wing and did not go in. On the day the decree tearing down the hermitages came, he sat alone all night in the scripture room.

Jingfu asked him once.

"I'm thinking of Uncle An Yan," said Qushen. "He kept faith all his life, and died on his bed in the west wing. Those on the monastery's register are one fewer every year. In the city of Chang'an the Buddhist chapels have been torn down, and the house at Zhouzhi too, and the day will come when even those with name-boards aren't safe—how many will be left in this monastery then? And those left—where will they go?"

He rubbed the ink on his hands.

"I'm forty-three. I don't know where I'll go."

One night in the tenth month, Qushen left.

Salang, keeping watch inside the gate, saw him come out with a bundle on his back.

"Where to?"

"The Tongzhen Temple. Daoist Lu's place is short of a copyist. Board and lodging."

"Will you come back?"

Qushen stood beside the lamp at the gate.

"I don't know."

Salang did not stop him. Qushen went out of the gate and off toward the far end of the ward street.

The next day, when Jingfu asked, Salang said, "My Pa used to say, keeping the gate is keeping out those outside who'd come in. When those inside want to go out, the gate can't stop them."

After that, at every offering once in seven, Salang often saw a man standing at the foot of the Jincheng Ward's wall across the ward street, in a grey cloth Daoist robe, hands tucked in his sleeves, looking over toward the monastery gate. When the singing in the hall was done and people dispersed, he left.

Salang told Jingfu.

"I know who it is," said Jingfu. "If one day that man crosses the street, don't stop him, and don't call him either. Let him walk up to the lamp by himself."

On the first morning after Qushen left, Jingfu went to the scripture room, and the scripture cabinet was open, and the top shelf empty.

On that shelf had been the Tang scriptures Qushen had copied—the Sutra of Profound Peace and Joy, the Scripture on Proclaiming the Origin, the Hymn of the Three Majesties, and a few more of Jingjing's translations—all newly copied, eleven rolls in all. The old rolls were still on the shelf below, wrapped in cloth: Jingjing's own hand from his day. Qushen had not touched them.

Weighted on the cabinet door was a sheet of paper in Qushen's upright regular hand:

The old rolls stay with the monastery; the new rolls go with me. The scriptures are the monastery's; the copying is my hand. I take them, and will not sell them, burn them, or give them to anyone. If one day the monastery needs them, send someone to fetch them.

At the end was written Qushen, and when he had written it he had put a dot beside it.

Lingbao came on He Siniang's arm. Jingfu read him the paper. Lingbao stood before the scripture cabinet, reached out to feel the top shelf, felt the empty board, felt it once, and again.

"Eleven rolls."

"Eleven rolls."

"Qushen, copying scripture, never left out one dot," said Lingbao. "His going is his affair. The scriptures he copied—the old rolls are still here; not one scripture in the scripture room is missing; only one set of copies. I'll enter it in the accounts."

"How?"

"As it is. Tenth month of the fourth year of Huichang: Qushen left the monastery, for the Tongzhen Temple, taking eleven newly copied rolls of the Tang scriptures; the old rolls remain with the monastery." Lingbao's hand still rested on the empty board. "One more place on the register empty."

Joseph came in at the door and heard the tail of it.

"So Qushen has gone over to the Daoists."

"He said he went to copy books," said Lingbao.

"Copying books all the way into a Daoist temple, living in a Daoist temple—if that isn't going over, what is?"

"Whether he's gone over, I can't see," said Lingbao. "I only know that when he left, what he took was our scriptures; of the Daoists' scriptures he didn't take one roll. And on the paper he left, beside the last character, he put a dot."

The next day Jingfu went himself to the inspectorate to report that Qushen had left the monastery.

"Gone where?" asked the aide-de-camp.

"The Jincheng Ward. The Tongzhen Temple."

The aide-de-camp raised his head.

"So one of your monastery's people has gone to a Daoist temple."

"Yes."

The aide-de-camp made a stroke in the register and asked no more.

That evening Jingfu was alone in the scripture room. He wiped the empty top shelf clean, wiped it several times until it shone. When he had wiped it he did not move the old rolls up from below, nor put anything else in, but closed the cabinet door, and did not lock it.
