Volume Ten　Morning Praises

Chapter 62　Guangzheng

In the winter of the second year of Changqing, Elder Hanan died.

He had been blind nine years. In the last months before he died he still had someone help him out into the courtyard every morning to stand among those at the morning. He could not see, so he listened: listened to Jingfu's footsteps stop in the middle of the courtyard, listened to the people behind settle into place one by one, listened until it was light, and then had himself helped back. He died one night early in the twelfth month. Old Cao was dozing in his room, woke, felt his hand, and it was cold.

Siyuan pushed the cart and buried him outside the Kaiyuan Gate. The Tang characters on the stake Jingfu wrote: Hanan, of the Patriarchate. Lingbao carved a line of Syriac beside them. Since the winter began, two old believers at the east end of the ward had gone, one dead of illness, one off to Luoyang with his son; with Hanan gone, those at the morning dropped from forty-one to thirty-eight.

Lingbao went to the inspectorate to report the death, came back, and said to Jingfu, "Nineteen left on the register. The place that's come empty—let's have the elder Shi brother back. He's the oldest, and this year out of the monastery he's slept on sacks in the hu merchant's storeroom and coughed all winter."

"And the one kept for one who is to come?"

"That one isn't touched," said Lingbao. "One of the old goes, one of the ten who went out comes back. That one stays empty, always."

The elder Shi brother came back the next day with his little bundle on his back. His brother saw him to the monastery gate and did not come in.

A few days after Hanan's burial, Lingbao fell ill.

First he was cold at night, shivering under two quilts; then the fever came, five or six days of it, and he talked in his delirium, in the dialect of Balkh, which no one at the monastery could understand. Joseph sat by the bed listening all night, and the next day said to Jingfu, "I've heard this kind of speech in Merv. There's one word in it that means Pa. He said it several times."

Jingfu sent to the West Market for Shopkeeper Wang's grandson, who wrote a prescription; they brewed the medicine and poured it down him. On the seventh day the fever broke. He had grown a size thinner and could not get out of bed.

That month there were two offerings once in seven, and neither had the offering bread.

The first time, the hall sang as always and recited as always, and when they reached the place where the bread was to be broken, An Yan stood before the table. On the table the glass bowl stood empty, a clean cloth laid by its rim, and under the cloth nothing. An Yan held the edge of the table with both hands, head bowed, waited for the hall to finish singing that passage, and recited on. Those in the back rows did not notice at first; when it came time to file forward, those in front did not move, and so those behind did not move either. An old woman come back from the south of the city had already stood up; slowly she knelt down again, her knees cracking.

When it broke up, several old believers from the ward surrounded Jingfu at the gate.

"Why was there no bread today?"

"Uncle Lingbao is ill."

"Then can't you break it? You're the Director."

"No," said Jingfu. "The Director manages affairs. The bread must be blessed by someone hands have been laid on. Hands haven't been laid on me."

"Then don't bless it. Break it, and everyone eat a mouthful."

"That would be eating bread, not receiving it."

The old believer looked at him, a long while. "When your Master was here, there was never one offering once in seven that people went home from empty-handed."

The second time, there was one row fewer in the hall, and no one asked again. When it broke up a few old people went to the table, reached out and touched the cloth, and when they had touched it, left.

That evening Lingbao had himself helped into the little room in the back courtyard, and sat wrapped in a quilt in the corner, his face yellow. Jingfu, An Yan, Joseph and Elijah were there, and Guangzheng too, sitting by the door. Since he left the monastery he didn't sleep there at night, but he still often came to deliberations, and no one kept things from him.

"I've been thinking for several days," said Lingbao. "The bread must be broken by a priest, and of priests in Chang'an there's only me left. Before, there were the elders, there were those Elder David laid hands on, there were those Brother laid hands on in his eight years as bishop; when one died there was still another. With Brother gone, each one who dies is one fewer, and none can be made up. To make them up needs the laying on of hands, the laying on of hands needs a bishop, and Chang'an has no bishop any more."

He coughed a while, got his breath back, and went on. "Hands haven't been laid on Jingfu. An Yan is a deacon; he can pass the bowl, not break the bread. Joseph and Elijah left home at Merv, and hands weren't laid on them either. I'm fifty-five. This time I've recovered; next time? When I die, this monastery will be a monastery without bread. A monastery without bread—the offering once in seven is just a crowd of people standing reciting scripture, and after a few years of that, people won't come."

"Then what's to be done?" asked Jingfu.

"Go to the Patriarchate and ask for a bishop to come. At the least, ask for someone who can lay on hands."

No one in the room spoke. After a while Jingfu asked, "To the Patriarchate—how would one go?"

That night no one could answer. Joseph said he would go and ask about the road.

The road to the Patriarchate could not be got through in the west. In the first year of Changqing the Tang and the Tibetans swore a covenant in the western suburbs of the capital, and the next year swore it again at Lhasa, and when the hu merchants of the West Market spoke of it they all said the road would open. But beyond the Long Mountains westward, the whole of Hexi was Tibetan country, and the caravans were searched hard; anyone found carrying scriptures had them burned on the spot. The road north through the Uighurs went too far round, and in the khagan's royal tent sat Manichaean teachers; a monk of the Luminous Religion who went there could scarcely hope to come out alive.

All that was left was the sea.

Joseph asked in the West Market for three days, came back, sat in the little room, and spoke very slowly.

"Elder David came by sea in his day. Elijah and I came with him; we were in our early twenties that year. We took ship at Basra, forty-odd people on board—Persians, Arabs, Indians, Sogdians—and by the time we reached Guangzhou, eleven had died. Some died of sickness, some were swept off by the waves outside the Lion Kingdom, and once swept off they were gone; you couldn't even hear them call."

Elijah sat beside him, telling the string of knots in his hand.

"Past the Lion Kingdom there's a stretch of sea where for forty days you see no land," said Joseph. "Water has to be saved—a ladle a day, and the last few days half a ladle. People lay on the deck planks and licked dry the rainwater that seeped into the cracks between them. Elder David was ill half a year at Guangzhou, and the two of us took turns watching over him. Over the Dayu Ridge, through Hongzhou, through Xiangzhou, and when we reached Chang'an it was already the twelfth month."

Jingfu heard him out, and it was a long while before he said, "A road like that—whom would we send?"

"I'll go," said Guangzheng by the door.

Jingfu did not agree.

"And your ma? Your ma's eyes are bad. If you go, who'll thread her needles?" he said. "Three years ago you ten left the monastery to give your places to the old. The places given up, but you yourself still in Chang'an, coming to the morning every day—that's one thing. If you board a ship, that's another. Brother Joseph has told us: of forty-odd, eleven died. Whether you'd be among the eleven, no one knows. Guangzheng, this I can't agree to. If I agree and you die at sea, what do I tell your ma?"

Lingbao waited until he had finished, then spoke. "I want Guangzheng to go."

Jingfu turned his head to look at him.

"I'm afraid too," said Lingbao. "I watched Guangzheng grow up. He came in at fourteen, learned his Syriac letters from Brother, learned them better than I did. I can't bear to let him go. But if Chang'an has no one to lay on hands, in another ten or twenty years the monastery will be empty. That's a thing people die of too—only slowly, and you don't see it."

"If there's one person in the monastery who can go, it can only be Guangzheng. His Syriac the Patriarchate's people can understand, and how Chang'an has got through these years, he knows. And one more thing—" he coughed twice, "—Guangzheng isn't on the monk register now."

They all looked at him.

"Those on the monk register leaving the capital first need a certificate from the inspectorate. The inspectorate asks where you're going, what for, whether you'll come back—documents layer on layer, until the questions reach the Commissioner of Merit. The Commissioner is a palace man, and when palace men hear the words 'west of the sea,' that's the end of the matter. For Joseph and Elijah to go would be harder still. They're hu monks; the register says they came from the Patriarchate; once out of the capital going south, they'd be questioned at every pass."

"Three years ago Guangzheng went himself to the inspectorate and returned to lay life; now he's a registered householder of the Buzheng Ward. A householder going on a long journey asks for a travel permit, writes that he's going to Guangzhou with such-and-such a hu merchant's goods, and he can go. At Guangzhou, whether he boards a ship or not, the permit doesn't say. A Tang man going to sea without leave is, by the code, crossing a border unlawfully, and if it's found out he'll be punished. If a man on the monk register does it, the guilt reaches the monastery; if a householder does it, he bears it alone."

When he got to this his voice sank. "Three years ago, when Guangzheng gave up that place, all I thought of was that the old would have somewhere to live. I didn't think of today."

Guangzheng sat by the door with his head bowed, both hands gripping his knees.

Jingfu said, "I'll think about it more."

The money came within reach after spring had come.

In the first month Lingbao could get out of bed; the second month passed, and Jingfu still had not given his word. One offering once in seven in the third month, when it broke up, the daughter of the He family of the West Market stayed in the hall, waited until everyone had gone, and came to find Jingfu.

The He family were Sogdians who had kept a silk shop in the West Market three generations and believed three generations. Old He was past seventy with bad legs; these two years his daughter had helped him to the monastery. She was the fourth of her generation, and the ward called her He Siniang. She was in her early thirties and had been married once, to a hu merchant who sailed the sea, surnamed Kang. Three years before he had taken ship at Guangzhou for Basra, and once the ship was past the Lion Kingdom there was no more word of it. A year later someone who had sailed on it and come back sent word that the ship had met a storm at sea and the men were lost. She did not marry again, but moved back to her parents' house, and at every offering once in seven helped her Pa come, and stood on the women's side, and did not say much.

"Director, I hear the monastery is going to send someone to the Patriarchate, by sea."

"Who told you that?"

"Elder Joseph asked two hu merchants in the West Market—asked the price of passage, asked which house's ships in Guangzhou go to Basra," said He Siniang. "The West Market is only so big. Ask one house, and ten houses know."

Jingfu did not answer.

"My husband in his day was in partnership with a Persian family in Guangzhou. Their surname is Li; they've lived three generations in the foreign quarter of Guangzhou, and the ship was theirs. After my husband died the Li family sent back the money for his share, thirty-odd strings of cash. I've never touched it; it's at the bottom of a chest in my parents' house. My Pa told me to buy land with it, and I didn't," she said. "This money I'll give to the monastery. The price of passage, the travelling money on the road—it should be about enough. And I'll write a letter to the Li family in Guangzhou. The Li family know my husband's hand, and they know mine too. When the letter arrives, the Li family will find that young man a berth on the ship, and find an old hand on the ship to look after him."

"Mistress He, this is money your husband paid for with his life."

"I know," said He Siniang. "The road he went, he didn't come back by. If a young man of the monastery goes that road and does come back, I'll count it as my husband coming back once too."

She took a cloth packet from her sleeve and laid it on the table. "The money is at home; tomorrow I'll have someone from my Pa's shop bring it. This is the letter. I wrote it last night, in Sogdian letters, and the cover says the Li family of the foreign quarter in Guangzhou."

Jingfu sent for Lingbao. Lingbao was not yet fully recovered; he came out of the little room holding on to the wall, sat at the table, opened the cloth packet, looked at the cover of the letter, and wrapped it up again.

"Mistress He, this money—how should the monastery record it? As whose gift?"

He Siniang thought. "Record it in my husband's name."

"What was he called?"

"Surname Kang, called Kang Hunu. Later he thought the name too lowly and changed it: Kang Shouxin."

Lingbao took from his breast a small book, the one he kept accounts in and carried with him, opened it, dipped the brush, and wrote stroke by stroke, very slowly, and when he had written it read it to her: "Third month of the third year of Changqing: Siniang of the He family of the West Market, with the sea-ship money of her late husband Kang Shouxin, gives to the monastery, for Guangzheng's expenses to the Patriarchate." He raised his head. "Written so—is it right?"

"It's right," said He Siniang. "Your Reverence is ill; you'd better not be up and walking about."

She bowed and went to the hall door to help her Pa. Lingbao sat at the table, waited until the ink of that line in the book was dry, and only then closed it.

That night Jingfu went to the Buzheng Ward.

Guangzheng's house was in a crooked lane in the north of the ward: two rooms, with a crook-necked scholar tree by the door. When he arrived the lamp was lit inside, and through a crack in the window he could see Guangzheng sitting under the lamp with his mother across from him, mother and son leaning close over one lamp, Guangzheng with a needle pinched in his fingers, threading it. Jingfu stood outside the door and waited until he had finished threading that one before he knocked.

Guangzheng came out, saw who it was, and drew him under the scholar tree. The two of them stood and talked and did not go in.

"I'll ask you one thing only," said Jingfu. "If you reach the Patriarchate and the Patriarchate gives no one, and won't lay hands on you either—will you come back?"

"I'll come back," said Guangzheng. "If they won't give, I'll still come back. Come back and tell the monastery the Patriarchate won't give. Once the monastery knows, it won't wait any longer; how to go on after that, the monastery will think for itself."

"If you die at sea, the monastery will never know all its life whether the Patriarchate would give or not."

"Then the monastery will wait. Waiting all its life is still waiting."

Jingfu stood under the scholar tree listening to his mother inside putting away her thread board, sticking the needles in one by one; each one she stuck in made a little sound.

"Go, then," he said. "Tomorrow I'll have Brother Joseph teach you about the sea—teach you to know the speech of the Lion Kingdom, teach you how to keep water on a ship. Before you go, at the offering once in seven, come and stand at the front, beside Uncle Lingbao, and watch how he breaks the bread. Watch it clearly, so that when you reach the Patriarchate and people ask how the bread is broken in Chang'an, you can tell them."

"Good."

Jingfu walked out of the crooked lane, and the ward gate was already shut. He waited under the ward gate until the clapper, and only then went back to the Yining Ward. At the morning the next day he stood in the middle of the courtyard, and when the sky greyed he looked back and counted: thirty-eight, not one fewer. He went into the scripture room and wrote on the board the character qu, to go.

Guangzheng's mother learned of it only ten days before he left.

Guangzheng hadn't hidden it from her, but he hadn't been able to say it either. Those nights he sat under the lamp threading needles for her, threaded one and then another, threaded a whole row and stuck them in her thread board, enough to last her several months. She felt the row of needles on the board and asked him, "Where are you going?"

"To Guangzhou. And on west of the sea, to the Patriarchate, to ask for someone who can lay on hands to come back for the monastery."

"How long?"

"I don't know. When Elder David came, from Basra to Chang'an took two years."

"A place so far—once you've gone, will you come back?"

"I'll come back."

She did not cry and did not stop him. The next day she went to the monastery. Her eyes were bad; from the Buzheng Ward to the Yining Ward took her half an hour. She stood at the monastery gate and asked Old Cao, who was sweeping, to go in and call someone. Jingfu came out.

"Director, I haven't come to stop it," she said. "He came in at this gate at fourteen, and once he came in at this gate he wasn't mine alone. I only ask one thing: this Patriarchate—where in the west is it? Farther than Xizhou?"

"Farther than Xizhou. Much farther," said Jingfu. "By sea-ship, past the Lion Kingdom, across the great sea, ashore at Basra, then north, across two great rivers."

"Then I know."

Three days before Guangzheng left, she sewed him a small pouch of oiled cloth, sewn very close, the stitches crooked in several places; she couldn't see, and sewed it by feel. She gave him the pouch. "That letter, and the monastery's letter to the Patriarchate—put them in here and carry them next to your skin. It's damp at sea; don't let the letters get wet."

The monastery's letter to the Patriarchate Lingbao wrote, in Syriac letters on a sheet of parchment, over three evenings. He wrote it to the patriarch Timothy. Whether Timothy was still living he did not know; he knew only that while Jingjing lived, Timothy had sent David once, and David had laid on Jingjing the hands of a bishop.

He wrote of Chang'an these years: Elder David died in the twelfth month of the fifth year of Yuanhe; Jingjing was bishop eight years and died too in the twelfth month of the thirteenth year of Yuanhe; Chang'an has no bishop any more, and the laying on of hands is broken; of priests only he is left, fifty-six years old; the directing of the monastery is given to Jingfu, on whom hands have not been laid; the monastery's register holds twenty, the quota twenty-one, one remaining. He wrote that in Chang'an every day before light thirty or forty people stand in the courtyard; he wrote of the gruel shed in the ward street, of those rows of stakes outside the Kaiyuan Gate, of the stele. At the end he wrote: We beg the patriarch to send one who can lay on hands; if he cannot send one, we beg the patriarch to lay hands on the bearer of this letter, Guangzheng, and let him return to Chang'an. Chang'an cannot be without bread.

When he had written it he had Jingfu sign at the end. Jingfu could not write Syriac letters, so Lingbao held his hand and led it stroke by stroke, and the letters that came out were crooked, as if a child had written them.

Jingfu wrote a sheet of his own in Tang characters. He knew the patriarch could not read Tang characters, and wrote it all the same: Jingfu, Director of the Daqin Monastery of Chang'an, bows his head to the ground. Below he left it blank, not knowing what else to write, folded it, and put it into the oiled-cloth pouch with the rest.

The travel permit An Yan went to apply for: first fetching a dispatch from the Metropolitan Prefecture, then taking the dispatch to the Office of Gates in the Department of State to have it issued, two trips. The clerk of the Office of Gates asked him, "Where to?"

"To Guangzhou. With a letter from the He family of the West Market, to the Li family of the foreign quarter in Guangzhou."

"What for?"

"To go to Guangzhou, and then by ship west of the sea, to our religion's Patriarchate."

The clerk raised his head and looked at him, looked a while, lowered his head, and wrote on the permit the words to Guangzhou. The second half of the sentence he did not write. A Tang man going west of the sea was, by the code, crossing a border unlawfully; if he didn't write it, he hadn't heard it.

On the last night, Lingbao turned the small book to the page after He Siniang's and added a line: Ninth month of the third year of Changqing: Guangzheng goes to the Patriarchate, to ask for a bishop. Below this line he left a line empty, with nothing written.

Guangzheng left in the ninth month of the third year of Changqing, with a caravan bound for Xiangzhou, out of the city by the Chunming Gate, by Lantian, through the Wu Pass, through Xiangzhou, through Hongzhou, over the Dayu Ridge—the very road Elder David had come by, walked the other way.

That day, before it was light, the morning was done as usual, with Guangzheng standing in the last row. When it broke up he shouldered his bundle, and Jingfu saw him to the monastery gate. The lamp at the gate Jingfu had lit the night before. Guangzheng crouched and filled the lamp dish to the brim with oil and did not light it. He stood up and brushed the dust off his knees.

"The evening one—the Director still does that."
