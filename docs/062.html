Volume Ten　Morning Praises

Chapter 62　Guangzheng

In the winter of the second year of Changqing, Elder Hanan died. He had been blind nine years, and in the last months before he died he still had himself helped out into the courtyard every morning to stand among those at morning devotion; he could not see, so he listened: listened to Jingfu's footsteps stop in the middle of the courtyard, listened to those behind settle into place one by one, listened till light, and then had himself helped back. He died one night at the beginning of the twelfth month; when he died Old Cao was dozing in his room, and woke and felt his hand, and it was cold. He had come to Chang'an from Xizhou with a Sogdian caravan in the second year of Baoying, nineteen that year, and lived in the monastery sixty years; his Syriac was better than his Tang, and to his death his Tang kept the Patriarchate's accent, saying wan for fan, rice. Siyuan pushed the cart and buried him outside the Kaiyuan Gate; on the stake was cut Hanan, of the Patriarchate, the Tang characters written by Jingfu, and beside them Lingbao cut a line of Syriac. Since the start of winter, of two old believers at the east end of the ward, one had died of sickness and one had gone with his son to Luoyang; with Hanan gone, that winter the number at morning devotion fell from forty-one to thirty-eight. Hanan's name came off the monk register, leaving nineteen on it; Lingbao went to the inspectorate to report the death, and came back and told Jingfu: for this empty place, let the Shi family's eldest come back; he's the oldest, and this year out of the monastery sleeping on hemp sacks in the hu merchant's storeroom, he's coughed all winter. Jingfu said: and the one kept for one who is to come? Lingbao said that one stays untouched; one of the old goes, one of the ten who went out comes back; that one stays empty, always. The Shi eldest came back the next day with his little bundle on his back; his younger brother saw him to the gate and did not come in.

A few days after Elder Hanan was buried, Lingbao fell ill. First he took chills at night, shivering under two quilts; then the fever came, and burned five or six days; he raved, in the country speech of Balkh, which no one in the monastery understood; Joseph said he had heard such speech in Merv, and could tell that one word in it was a call for Pa, called several times. Lingbao's Pa was a man of Balkh, dead nearly forty years; Lingbao had never in his life been to Balkh; those few words of country speech he had heard as a child at his Pa's knee. Jingfu sent to the West Market for the grandson of Proprietor Wang, who wrote a prescription; the medicine was boiled and poured down him, and on the seventh day the fever broke; he was a size thinner and could not get out of bed. That month there were two offerings once in seven, and at neither was there offering bread. The first time, the hall sang as ever, recited as ever, and when they came to the place where the bread should be broken, An Yan stood before the table; on the table the glass bowl stood empty, a clean cloth laid by the bowl's rim, and nothing under the cloth; An Yan rested both hands on the table's edge, head bowed, waited for the hall to finish singing that passage, and recited on. At first the back rows did not notice; when it came time to line up and go forward, those in front did not move, so those behind did not move either; an old woman come back from the south of the city had already stood up, and slowly knelt back down, her knees cracking as she knelt. When it broke up, the old believers from the ward surrounded Jingfu at the gate and asked why there was no bread today; Jingfu said Uncle Lingbao was ill; one said: can't you break it? you're the leader; Jingfu said he couldn't; the leader is for managing affairs; the bread must be blessed by one who has had hands laid on him, and he had not. The man said: then don't bless it; break it and let everyone eat a mouthful; Jingfu said: that would be eating bread, not receiving bread. The old believer looked at him and said: when your Master was here, there was never one offering once in seven when we went back empty-handed. The second time, there was a row fewer in the hall, and no one asked; when it broke up, a few old people went up to the table, put out a hand and touched that cloth, and only then left.

What could not be done, everyone in the monastery knew; before, it was not much spoken of; with Lingbao's illness, it was spoken of. The bread must be broken by a priest, and of priests in Chang'an only Lingbao was left. Before, there were the elders—Azarel, Marya, Sabrisho—there were those David had laid hands on, those laid hands on in the eight years Master was bishop; when one died there was still another; after Master passed away the priests died one by one, each death one fewer, and could not be replaced, because replacing needs the laying on of hands, and laying on of hands needs a bishop, and Chang'an had no bishop now. Jingfu had been leader three years, and had not even had hands laid on him as deacon; by standing he was no more than a brother; An Yan was a deacon, ordained by Master, and a deacon could pass the bowl but not break the bread; Joseph and Elijah were brothers, who had left home in Merv, and had had no hands laid on them either. Lingbao was fifty-five; he had recovered this time; and next time?

That evening several of them sat in the small room; Jingfu called no council; they only sat. Lingbao, propped in bed, had himself helped into the small room, and sat wrapped in a quilt in the corner, his face yellow. He said he had been thinking for days: Chang'an must not be without someone to lay on hands; when he died, this monastery would be a monastery without bread; and in a monastery without bread the offering once in seven was a crowd of people standing reciting scripture; recite a few years, and people would stop coming. He said they must go to the Patriarchate and ask for a bishop, or at least ask for someone who could lay on hands. Jingfu asked how one went to the Patriarchate, and that night no one could answer; Joseph said he would go and ask about the road.

The road to the Patriarchate by the west could no longer be travelled. In the first year of Changqing the Tang and the Tibetans had sworn a covenant in the western suburbs of the capital, and the next year sworn it again at Lhasa; the hu merchants in the West Market, speaking of it, all said the road would open; but past the Long mountains going west, the whole of Hexi was Tibetan land; at the gates of Shazhou and Guazhou stood Tibetan soldiers; Tang monks could not get through; only caravans could, and caravans were searched strictly; find one carrying scripture rolls and the rolls were burned on the spot. Going north by the Uighurs' road, the way round was too far; the Uighurs believed in Mani, and in the khagan's tent sat Manichaean teachers; a monk of the Luminous Teaching going there—whether he'd come out alive was hard to say. What was left was only the sea. Elder David had come by sea long ago; he took ship at Basra, drifted nine months at sea, was ill half a year in Guangzhou, crossed the Dayu range, passed Hongzhou, passed Xiangzhou, and by the time he walked into Chang'an it was the twelfth month. Joseph and Elijah had been with him that time, a little over twenty, and it was the two of them who watched over him by turns in Guangzhou.

Joseph spoke of that road very slowly. He said there were forty-odd people on their ship—Persians, Tazi, Indians, Sogdians—and by the time they reached Guangzhou eleven had died; some died of sickness, some were swept off by the waves outside the Lion Kingdom, and swept off, they were gone; you couldn't even hear them cry out. He said past the Lion Kingdom there was a stretch of sea where for forty days you saw no land; water had to be saved, a ladle a day, and the last few days half a ladle a day; there were men who licked clean the rainwater that seeped through the cracks of the ship's planks. Elijah sat listening, telling the knotted cord in his hand. When Jingfu had heard it, he said: a road like this—whom would we send? Guangzheng was in the small room that day too, sitting by the door; since leaving the monastery he did not sleep there at night, but he still often came to councils, and no one kept things from him. He said he would go.

Jingfu would not agree. He said: and your Ma? Your Ma's eyes are bad; if you go, who'll thread her needles? He said: three years ago the ten of you left the monastery to give up your places to the old men; the places were given up, but you were still in Chang'an and came to morning devotion every day; that's one thing; if you go on board a ship, that's another; Joseph has told us—of forty-odd, eleven died; if you go, whether you'll be among the eleven, no one knows. He said he could not agree to this; he was the leader; if he agreed and you died at sea, what would he say to your Ma?

Lingbao waited for Jingfu to finish, and said he wanted to send Guangzheng. He said he was afraid too; he had watched Guangzheng grow up; he had entered the monastery at fourteen, learned his Syriac letters from Brother, learned them better than he himself had; he couldn't bear it. But if Chang'an had no one to lay on hands, in ten years, twenty years, the monastery would be empty; this too was a thing that killed people, only slowly, where you couldn't see it. He said if anyone in the monastery could go it could only be Guangzheng: Guangzheng's Syriac the Patriarchate's people could understand; Guangzheng knew how Chang'an had come through these years; Guangzheng—he paused, and said: Guangzheng is not on the monk register now.

They all looked at him. Lingbao said: for a man on the monk register to leave the capital, the inspectorate must first issue a pass; the inspectorate asks where to, what for, whether he's coming back; document upon document, the questions go up to the Commissioner of Merit, and the Commissioner of Merit is a palace man, and once a palace man hears the words "west of the sea," that's the end of it; for Joseph and Elijah to go would be harder still; they are hu monks, written on the register as come from the Patriarchate; leaving the capital and going south, they'd be questioned at every pass. Guangzheng had gone to the inspectorate himself three years ago and returned to lay life; he was now a registered household of Buzheng Ward; a registered householder going a long way asks for a travel permit, writes on it bound for Guangzhou, trading goods with such-and-such hu merchant family, and he can go; once at Guangzhou, whether he boards a ship the permit doesn't say. He said that for a Tang man to go to sea on his own was, under the code, crossing a border illicitly, and if found out would be punished; if a man on the monk register committed it, the guilt reached the monastery; if a registered householder did, he bore it alone. He said that place Guangzheng gave up three years ago—back then he had thought only that the old men would have somewhere to live; he had not thought of today. Guangzheng sat by the door, head bowed, both hands gripping his knees; Jingfu said he would think again.

The money came to hand after the start of spring. In the first month Lingbao could get out of bed; the second month passed and Jingfu still had not given way; in the third month, when one offering once in seven had broken up, the daughter of the He family of the West Market stayed in the hall, waited for everyone to leave, and came to find Jingfu. The He family were Sogdians who had kept a silk shop in the West Market three generations and believed three generations; Old He was past seventy and his legs were bad; these two years his daughter had helped him come. The daughter was the fourth child; the ward called her He Siniang, Fourth Daughter He; past thirty, married once; her husband was a seafaring hu merchant surnamed Kang, who three years before had taken ship from Guangzhou for Basra, and once the ship was past the Lion Kingdom there was no news; after a year, men who came back on the same ship sent word that the ship had met a wind at sea and the people were lost. She had not married again, and moved back to her parents' house; at every offering once in seven she helped her Pa come, and stood on the women's side, and said little.

She said she had heard the monastery meant to send someone to the Patriarchate, and by sea. Jingfu asked who told her; she said Elder Joseph had asked two hu merchants in the West Market about the price of a passage, and which ship at Guangzhou went to Basra; the West Market was only so big; ask one shop and ten knew. She said her husband had been in partnership with a Persian family in Guangzhou; that family was surnamed Li and had lived in the foreigners' quarter at Guangzhou three generations; the ship was theirs; after her husband died, the Li family sent back her husband's share, thirty-odd strings of cash all told; she had never touched it; it was at the bottom of a chest at her parents' house; her Pa told her to buy land with it, and she had not. She said this money she would give to the monastery; the passage and the journey's expenses—it would probably be enough; and she would write a letter to the Li family at Guangzhou; the Li family knew her husband's hand and knew hers too; when the letter arrived, the Li family would find the young man a berth on a ship, and find an old hand on board to look after him.

Jingfu said: this is money your husband's life bought. He Siniang said she knew. She said that road her husband had travelled, he did not travel back; if a young man of the monastery travelled this road and came back, she would count it that her husband too had come back once.

When she had said this, she took a cloth packet from her sleeve and laid it on the table, and said: the money is at home; tomorrow she'd have someone from her Pa's shop bring it; this was the letter; she wrote it last night, in Sogdian letters; on the cover was written the Li family, foreigners' quarter, Guangzhou.

Jingfu called Lingbao. Lingbao was not yet fully recovered; he came out of the small room holding the wall, sat by the table, opened the cloth packet, looked at the cover of the letter, wrapped it again, and asked her how the monastery should enter this money—as whose gift? He Siniang thought, and said: enter it in her husband's name. Lingbao asked what her husband was called. She said: surnamed Kang, called Kang Hunu; later he thought the name low and changed it, and was called Kang Shouxin, Kang Keeps-Faith. Lingbao felt in his robe and took out a little book he carried to keep accounts in, opened it, dipped the brush in ink, and wrote stroke by stroke, very slowly, and when he had finished read it to her: "Third month of the third year of Changqing: He Siniang of the West Market gives to the monastery the sea-trade capital of her late husband Kang Shouxin, toward the cost of Guangzheng's going to the Patriarchate." When he had read it, he looked up at her and asked whether that was right. He Siniang said it was right, and said: the Great Worthy is ill; better not to get up and walk about much. When she had said it, she bowed and went to the hall door to help her Pa. Lingbao sat by the table till the ink of that line in the book was dry before he closed it.

That night Jingfu went to Buzheng Ward. Guangzheng's house was in a crooked lane in the north of the ward, two rooms, a crook-necked locust tree by the door; when he arrived a lamp was lit inside, and through a crack in the window he could see Guangzheng sitting under the lamp and his mother sitting opposite him, mother and son leaning together over one lamp, Guangzheng with a needle in his fingers, threading it. Jingfu stood outside the door and waited till Guangzheng had finished threading that one before he knocked. Guangzheng came out, saw who it was, and drew him under the locust tree; the two of them stood and talked and did not go in. Jingfu asked him only one thing. He said: if you get to the Patriarchate and the Patriarchate won't give, won't send anyone, won't lay hands on you either—will you come back? Guangzheng said he would. He said: if they won't give, he'll still come back, and come back and tell the monastery the Patriarchate won't give, and then the monastery will know, and stop waiting; what to do after, the monastery will think out for itself. Jingfu said: if you die at sea, the monastery will never know whether the Patriarchate would give. Guangzheng said: then the monastery waits; waiting a lifetime is still waiting. Jingfu stood under the locust tree listening to his mother inside putting away the thread-board, sticking the needles in one by one—one needle, one small sound. He said: go. He said: tomorrow he'd have Joseph teach you the things of the sea, teach you the speech of the Lion Kingdom, teach you how to keep water on board; before you go, at the offering once in seven, come and stand at the front, beside Uncle Lingbao, and watch how he breaks the bread; watch it clearly, so that at the Patriarchate, when they ask how the bread is broken in Chang'an, you can tell them. Guangzheng said: good. Jingfu came out of the crooked lane; the ward gate was already shut; he waited under the ward gate till the clapper before going back to Yining Ward; at morning devotion the next day he stood in the middle of the courtyard, and when the sky greyed looked back and counted: thirty-eight, not one fewer; he went into the scripture room and wrote on the board the character qu, to go.

Guangzheng's mother learned of it only ten days before he left. Guangzheng had not hidden it from her, but neither had he ever brought himself to say it; those few nights he sat under the lamp threading needles for her: one threaded, then another, threaded a row, and stuck them in her thread-board, enough to last her several months. She felt that row of needles on the thread-board and asked him where he was going. Guangzheng said: to Guangzhou, and on west of the sea, to the Patriarchate, to ask for someone who can lay on hands and bring him back for the monastery. She asked how long; Guangzheng said he didn't know; when Elder David came, from Basra to Chang'an took two years. She asked again: a place so far—once you've gone, will you come back? Guangzheng said he would.

She did not cry, and did not stop him. The next day she went to the monastery to find Jingfu; her eyes were bad; walking from Buzheng Ward to Yining Ward took her half a watch; she stood at the monastery gate and asked Old Cao, sweeping at the gate, to go in and call someone. Jingfu came out, and she said she hadn't come to stop it; her son had come in at this gate at fourteen, and having come in at this gate, he was no longer hers alone. She asked only one thing: that Patriarchate—where in the west was it? Farther than Xizhou? Jingfu said farther than Xizhou, much farther than Xizhou: by sea-ship, past the Lion Kingdom, across the great sea, ashore at Basra, then north, across two great rivers. She nodded and said: then now she knew.

Three days before Guangzheng left, she sewed him a little bag of oilcloth, sewed very close, the stitches crooked in several places; she couldn't see clearly and sewed it by feel. She gave him the bag and said: that letter, and the monastery's letter to the Patriarchate, put them in here, carry them next to your skin; the sea is damp; don't let the letters get wet.

The monastery's letter to the Patriarchate was written by Lingbao, in Syriac, on a sheet of parchment, written over three evenings. He wrote to the Patriarch Timothy; he did not know whether Timothy was still living; he knew only that while Jingjing lived, Timothy had once sent David, that David had laid hands on Jingjing as bishop, and died in Chang'an in the twelfth month of the next year. He wrote of Chang'an these years: Elder David passed away in the twelfth month of the fifth year of Yuanhe; Jingjing was bishop eight years, and passed away likewise in the twelfth month of the thirteenth year of Yuanhe; Chang'an has no bishop any more; the laying on of hands is broken; of priests now only he is left, fifty-six years old; the leadership of the monastery is handed to Jingfu, and Jingfu has not had hands laid on him; the monastery's register has twenty, the quota twenty-one, one remaining. He wrote that every day before light thirty or forty people stood in the courtyard in Chang'an; wrote of the gruel shed in the ward street, of those rows of stakes outside the Kaiyuan Gate, of the stele. At the end he wrote: we beg the Patriarch to send one who can lay on hands; if he cannot send one, we beg the Patriarch to lay hands on the bearer of this letter, Guangzheng, and let him return to Chang'an; Chang'an must not be without bread. When he had finished, he had Jingfu sign at the end; Jingfu could not write Syriac letters; Lingbao held his hand and guided it stroke by stroke, and the letters came out crooked, like a child's.

Jingfu wrote a sheet of his own in Tang characters to the Patriarch; he knew the Patriarch could not read Tang characters; he wrote it all the same. What he wrote was Jingfu, leader of the Daqin Monastery at Chang'an, knocks his head; below that it was blank; he did not know what else he should write; he folded it and put it in the oilcloth bag with the rest.

The travel permit An Yan went to obtain: first a dispatch from the Jingzhao prefecture, then with the dispatch to the Office of Gates of the Department of State, and he ran there twice. The clerk at the Office of Gates asked where Guangzheng was going; An Yan said to Guangzhou, carrying letters of the He family of the West Market to the Li family of the foreigners' quarter at Guangzhou; the clerk asked what for; An Yan said: to Guangzhou, and then on board ship west of the sea, to the Patriarchate of their teaching. The clerk looked up at him once, and wrote on the permit bound for Guangzhou; the latter half he did not write; for a Tang man to go west of the sea was, under the code, crossing a border illicitly; if he did not write it, he had not heard it.

The night before he left, Lingbao turned his little book to the page after the one that recorded He Siniang, and added a line: "Ninth month of the third year of Changqing: Guangzheng goes to the Patriarchate to ask for a bishop." Below this line he left a line empty, and wrote nothing in it.

Guangzheng left in the ninth month of the third year of Changqing, with a caravan bound for Xiangzhou, out of the city by the Chunming Gate, by Lantian, through the Wu Pass, past Xiangzhou, past Hongzhou, over the Dayu range—the very road Elder David had come by, travelled the other way. That day it was not yet light; morning devotion was held as usual, Guangzheng standing in the last row; when it broke up he shouldered his bundle, and Jingfu saw him to the monastery gate. The lamp at the gate, lit by Jingfu the night before, was still burning. Guangzheng squatted, lifted the lamp, tipped out the dregs, wiped it clean with the cloth, added new oil, trimmed the wick even, and set it back on the stone block; he did not light it; it was nearly light; there was no need. He stood up and said: the evening one—the leader still does that.
