Volume Nine　The Translation Hall

Chapter 56　Mi Jifen

Mi Jifen was an old soldier. How old he really was, no one at the monastery could say for sure, nor could he; ask him and he said he was born in the Kaiyuan years, which year of Kaiyuan he couldn't remember, but anyway older than anyone here. He was a man of Mi. The kingdom of Mi lay beside Kang, so small that many people in Chang'an had never heard of it. In his father's generation the king of Mi had sent his father to Chang'an as a hostage, held before the Son of Heaven, held all his life, and never gone back. He was born in Chang'an and grew up in Chang'an, and when he was grown he joined the army, and served until his hair was white, served until he was a general in the Shence Army. His Tang had a little hu in it, which you had to listen closely to hear. His youngest son Siyuan was a monk at the monastery and, after Narsai died, had taken over the cart for the dead.

At every offering once in seven Mi Jifen came. When he was younger he came on horseback; later he came by carriage; later still he couldn't sit steady in the carriage either, and had his servants hold him up one on each side. When he came he didn't sit up front but always in the last row by the door. When the others knelt he couldn't kneel, so he laid his stick across his knees and bowed his head. When the preaching went long he would doze, wake halfway, look around, see that no one was looking at him, and doze on. When Jingjing preached he never woke him.

One day in the spring of the sixteenth year of Zhenyuan, when worship was done, he did not leave, but had his servants help him shuffle over to Jingjing, stood, panted a few breaths, and said, "Your Reverence, the year Luo Haoxin came to find you—it was I who told him to come."

Jingjing thought, and only then remembered which year it was. Fourteen years ago.

"Luo Haoxin asked me whose Tang writing in the city was good, and I said Jingjing of the Daqin monastery. I never said you understood the Buddha," said Mi Jifen. "Afterwards, when that sheet of abuse came down, I heard of it, and felt bad, and meant to come and say so to you—and meant to for fourteen years. This year that Yuanzhao of the Ximing Monastery compiled a catalogue of the Buddhist scriptures by imperial order and presented it. A general in my camp who believes in the Buddha borrowed it to look at, came across you, and copied a few lines out for me. When I read them I felt worse."

He took a sheet of paper from his sleeve. Jingjing took it. On it were the very lines Luo Haoxin had copied out that year, not a character different, except that in front was added the Persian monk Jingjing of the Daqin monastery.

"This time it's been carved into a book," said Mi Jifen. "For a thousand years to come, everyone who reads the Buddhist scriptures will read these lines."

Jingjing folded the paper and said, "There's no need for you to feel bad, Lord Mi. Among those lines, one isn't abuse."

"Which one isn't abuse?"

"Jingjing should propagate the teaching of Mishihe."

Mi Jifen was taken aback, and laughed. When he laughed there was no sound; only his shoulders shook and shook, until his stick was jabbing the floor, and his servants hurried to steady him.

That night Lingbao was copying scripture for Jingjing in the scripture room, and Jingjing showed him the paper. Lingbao was past thirty. His office as deacon was held acting; Samaan's ledger recorded that the laying on of hands had not been done. He had copied scripture for more than a decade, and his Tang characters were more upright than anyone's at the monastery. When he reached the line though it was called a translation, not half a pearl had been obtained, he asked, "Brother, what does half a pearl mean?"

"Just half a pearl."

"And not half a pearl obtained?"

"Not even half."

Lingbao held his laughter in until his face went red, and Jingjing was the first to laugh, and then Lingbao laughed too, the two brothers laughing under the lamp. When they had laughed, Jingjing tucked the paper under the inkstone, together with the slip on which he had written Jingjing should propagate the teaching of Mishihe.

That year many came from the Shence Army asking to be washed. Wu Shaocheng of Huaixi had rebelled again, and the court sent troops against him, and part of the Shence Army was assigned too. A good many of the hu and Tang soldiers in the camp knew that Mi Jifen believed in the Daqin monastery, and before they marched, some came to him. "Lord Mi, if we're washed once at that monastery of yours, will blades and spears not touch us in battle?" Mi Jifen said there was no such thing, and they didn't believe him. "Lord Mi, you're nearly ninety, you've fought all your life, and there isn't a hole in you anywhere. You've been washed!" Mi Jifen could do nothing with them, and brought a dozen-odd to the monastery. They stood in the courtyard, every one of them young, sturdy, bright-eyed.

Jingjing came out and asked each of them in turn. The dozen-odd said much the same: some said they wanted blades and spears not to touch them; some said they wanted to come back alive and marry; some said a fellow from their district in the camp had been washed and fought three years without dying.

"The monastery has a rule for washing people," said Jingjing. "First you hear the scriptures, for a year. If after a year you still want to be washed, the monastery will wash you."

The soldiers looked at one another, and the one at their head said, "Master, we march next month. A year? In a year we'll long since be dead in Huaixi."

"I know," said Jingjing. "I can't deceive you. Washed, blades still cut into you, arrows still go into you. My Pa was washed, and at the Xiangji Monastery took three arrows. If you die in Huaixi unwashed, the Lord knows you all the same. My Pa had a friend called Old Duan the Seventh who was never washed all his life and died at Xiangzhou, and my Pa prayed for him and said, Lord, you know him. Before you march, the monastery will give each of you a piece of bread and a bowl of hot broth, and pray for you. When we've prayed, you go; and if you come back alive and want to hear the scriptures, the monastery's gate is open."

The soldiers ate the bread, drank the broth, and when the prayers were done went to the gate. One turned back and asked whether he could have one of those crosses to hang round his neck. Lingbao looked at Jingjing, and Jingjing said, "Give them. Whittle them some. Tell them it's a sign, not a charm; they wear it to remember that someone in Chang'an is praying for them." Lingbao whittled a dozen-odd through the night, not well made, and the next day gave them out one by one. Samaan made a line in the ledger: Fourteen soldiers of the Shence Army came asking to be washed; not washed; given bread and broth; prayed for; each given one wooden cross.

Three days later an aide-de-camp came from the Shence Army's camp with four soldiers, dismounted at the monastery gate, and came in asking which was Master Jing.

"I come by General Luo's order. The General is leading troops to Huaixi this time, and he hears a dozen-odd soldiers of the camp came to the monastery asking to be washed, and the monastery wouldn't wash them. The General isn't pleased. When the Son of Heaven's troops go to war they must pray for blessing. Every monastery is holding rites: the Ximing Monastery has held them, the Zhangjing Monastery has held them, even the Daoist temple in the south of the city has held them; and only you hu temple won't. The General says that before they march, the monastery is to send people to the camp and baptize the whole camp. A camp is eight hundred men, and not one is to be left out. When they've been washed, the monastery will be rewarded with a hundred bolts of silk." When he had said it he set his hand on his sword hilt and waited for Jingjing's answer.

"The monastery can go to the camp and pray for the whole camp," said Jingjing. "To pray for blessing on the Son of Heaven's troops, the monastery is willing. Baptism it cannot do. To be washed a man must believe for himself, ask for himself, and have heard the scriptures himself. Of eight hundred men, few believe. Wash them, and we'd have deceived the seven hundred and more, and deceived the General too."

The aide-de-camp's face darkened. "Master Jing, think again about the General's words. Your monastery's affairs, on the Commissioners of Merit's side, are in the hands of the General's superior." With that he left.

Samaan said, "There's no dodging it this time."

That night Jingjing went to Luo Haoxin's house. Luo Haoxin slapped his thigh. "This General Luo I know. He's the Commander's adopted son. In the camp his word is final. I'm only a commander; I have no say." Jingjing went to Mi Jifen's house too. Mi Jifen was lying in bed. When he had heard it out he lay with his eyes closed, and Jingjing thought he had fallen asleep; then he had his son help him up and said, "Tomorrow morning, early, I'll go to the camp."

"Lord Mi, at your age, if you go, General Luo won't necessarily give you face."

"I've served seventy years; in the Tianbao years I was already in the camp. Half the officers in the camp now I watched grow up. General Luo, when he was a boy, led horses under me," said Mi Jifen. "If I go, whether General Luo gives me face is General Luo's business. Whether I go is mine."

The next day Mi Jifen, held up by two servants, rode in his carriage in at the gate of the Shence Army's camp and got down before General Luo's tent. He could not stand. When the servants let go, he went down on his knees, knelt on the sand before the tent.

"General, the Daqin Monastery won't wash them; that's the monastery's rule, not disrespect for the General. The monastery's people are willing to come to the camp and pray for the whole camp. I'm an old soldier; I've come on the monastery's behalf to beg the General's pardon."

General Luo came out of the tent, saw him kneeling, and could not keep his countenance; he hurried to have him helped up. "Lord Mi, what are you doing! If the monastery won't wash them, then don't. If it's prayer, then prayer. I don't want the silk either."

Mi Jifen was helped to his feet, two patches of sand on his knees, and said, "Thank you, General."

As he left, General Luo said behind him, "Lord Mi, that Lord you believe in won't even wash soldiers. When there's fighting, does he look after you?"

Mi Jifen did not turn round. "I've fought seventy years. Whether he looked after me, I know myself."

The day before the march, Jingjing went into the camp with Lingbao and Siyuan. On the drill ground eight hundred soldiers stood in several ranks. He stood in front and read a prayer in Tang, The Lord shall preserve thy going out and thy coming in from this time forth, and even for evermore, and when he had read it, read it again in Syriac. The soldiers stood, some with heads bowed, some gazing about, some laughing. The fourteen who had been to the monastery stood together, wooden crosses round their necks, standing straightest of all. Among the fourteen was a Sogdian soldier surnamed An whom the camp called Sesame Cake. Before leaving he stood a moment at the monastery gate and said to Siyuan, "I've a child at home, five years old. If I don't come back, look out for him."

That autumn the war in Huaixi was lost. Many of the Shence Army died, and fewer than half came back. Of the fourteen, two came to the monastery, one missing an ear, one lame in the leg, still wearing those wooden crosses, the red cords worn white. They said they wanted to hear the scriptures. Jingjing said good: "From today, at the offering once in seven, you sit beside Lord Mi." The others did not come back. At the offering once in seven Lingbao read their names out once, as Samaan's ledger recorded them, the names of those who had names and, for those without, what the camp called them: Zhao the Eldest, Little Wang the Fifth, Sesame Cake.

That year two more were added to the names Jingjing said every morning as he told his knots. Ishaq died in the spring. He had been fat all his life, and at the end was down to a handful of bones, though his belly was still round. In his last days whoever came to see him, he took their hand and said mo pa, mo pa, as he had for forty years; to his death he never got the Tang right. Haddad died at the beginning of autumn. Of the seven in those days he had been the one who dealt with the outside, who knew several tongues; Yonan's secret he had known since Xizhou, and never spoke of it all his life. Before he died he sent for Jingjing and said only one thing: "All my life what I was best at was talking, and what I did most was not talking." When he had said it he laughed first himself. From then on, when Jingjing said the names in the middle of the courtyard before light, when he reached Ishaq, reached Haddad, he paused, and went on.

In the winter of the sixteenth year of Zhenyuan, Mi Jifen came again. It was not a day of worship, just an ordinary day, the hour of wei, overcast, about to snow. The carriage stopped at the monastery gate; the servants helped him down. He turned back to lift a child out of the carriage and couldn't, and a servant took the child and set him on the ground. The child was five or six, in an old padded jacket that didn't fit, the cuffs worn shiny, his hair a little curly, black with some yellow in it, his eyes set a little deep, like a hu. He had half a sesame cake gripped in his hand and was gnawing it as he stood, and as Jingjing came out he had just finished the last bite and was licking his fingers clean too.

Mi Jifen panted for several breaths before he could speak. "This child is Sesame Cake's. His father was a Sogdian surnamed An, baptized at the monastery a few years back; Siyuan knew him. His mother was Tang, daughter of a small household outside the city of Chang'an; she died of an illness the year before last. The child's been knocking about the camp with some old soldiers, fed by everyone, for the better part of a year. Now the camp's marching again and the old soldiers can't see to him, and they came to me and said, Lord Mi, you believe in that; see what's to be done with this child. I'm old, and my household can't keep him…"

Jingjing crouched down to the child's height. The child wasn't afraid of him. He looked at his face, looked at the bit of leather cord showing at his neck, and at once reached out to touch it. Jingjing tucked the cord back into his collar; the child's hand grabbed at nothing, but he wasn't put out, and drew it back and wiped it on his jacket.

"What's he called?"

"He's no name. His father called him A-Fu, and the camp called him Sesame Cake too, the same as his father—he eats like anything; however much you give him, he eats it, and when it's gone holds out his hand again."

Jingjing looked at the child, and the child looked at him, his eyes bright, not shrinking at all. He remembered something Ma had told him while she lived: in the fifth year of Tianbao she had carried him, five days born, to the Yining Ward and knocked at the door of a hu deacon, and the deacon asked nothing, only one question: can this child be kept alive?

"Lord Mi," said Jingjing, "can this child be kept alive?"

"He can. He eats."

"Then give him to me."

When he had said it he himself was taken aback for a moment, and Siyuan behind him was taken aback too. Siyuan had looked after the orphans seven years, and Jingjing had signed the register of orphans for seven years, and never once said he would take one as his own. Mi Jifen was not taken aback. He nodded, as if he had known all along that Jingjing would say this, and had a servant bring down a small bundle from the carriage. In the bundle were two garments of the child's, a pair of shoes, and a little horse whittled from wood, roughly whittled, one leg long and one short. "His father whittled it," said Mi Jifen.

The child saw the little horse and snatched it and hugged it to him. Jingjing held out his hand. The child looked at the hand, shifted the little horse under his other arm, freed a hand, and let himself be led. Jingjing's hand was cold; he had sat half the day in the scripture room, the brazier long since out, and his sleeve smelled of ink. Led by him toward the gate, the child took two steps, tipped up his face, sniffed hard, smelled his sleeve, and said, "Black." Jingjing looked down; sure enough, there was a streak of ink rubbed on his cuff.

That night Jingjing gave the child a name. He had thought of using a Syriac name, then thought that this child was born of a Tang mother, grew up on Tang soil, and would live his life among the Tang. Then he remembered a line on the stele, one he had written himself: The Law flowed through the ten circuits, and the state was rich in peace; monasteries filled a hundred cities, and every house abounded in Luminous blessing.

"He'll be Jingfu. Luminous Blessing."

Lingbao, beside him, said, "Brother's taking the words off his own stele."

"I wrote them. If I don't use them, who will?"

The child sat by the brazier hugging the little wooden horse, nearly asleep. Hearing someone say fu, he opened his eyes and looked, and closed them again.

That night the child slept on a straw mat newly laid beside the bed in Jingjing's room. In the middle of the night the child climbed up and felt his way out of the door in the dark. Jingjing thought he was going to the privy and waited; then he heard the well rope in the courtyard and the windlass creaking round, and sat up at once, threw on his clothes, and went out. The child was standing by the well holding a wooden ladle in both hands, gulping water—winter well water, cold enough to sting the teeth.

"Jingfu!"

The child turned round, drops of water hanging at his mouth. "Thirsty."

Jingjing meant to say you mustn't drink cold water; the words came to his lips and he did not say them. He went over, took the ladle and hung it back by the well, led the child back into the room, tucked him into the quilt, and sat by the bed himself to wait. He knew what came next: first the belly rumbling, then cramping, wave after wave, then running to the privy again and again till dawn, until your face was white from it. He had been like that from childhood. He had left the fire banked on the stove and put a piece of ginger by the stove. The child soon fell asleep, snoring a little, as even as if counting. Jingjing waited a watch, and there was no sound from the child; waited another watch, and the child turned over, smacked his lips, and slept on. Toward dawn Jingjing's own belly rumbled. He had drunk a bowl of broth the evening before, and the broth had probably gone a little cold. He went to the privy holding his belly, and when he came back his face was white. The child was still asleep, snoring louder than before. He sat down by the bed again and looked at that face flushed red with sleep, and thought this child's stomach wasn't like his, and his own stomach wasn't like Pa's either. He picked up the piece of ginger from beside the stove, cut two slices for himself, and chewed them.

The character he wrote on his board that morning came from Isaiah: Can a woman forget her sucking child… yea, they may forget, yet will I not forget thee. Behold, I have graven thee upon the palms of my hands. He wrote the character zhang, palm, and when he had written it held out his own palm and looked at it. There was ink on it, and an old scar where a stone had cut it when he was rebuilding the monastery at Xizhou.

Jingfu settled in at the monastery. He ate a great deal, and nothing he ate upset his stomach: cold rice, cold cakes, raw melon, well water, things of who knew what that the ward's children pushed on him—he took them and put them in his mouth, swallowed them, and nothing happened. After spring came Jingjing taught him his letters, Tang characters first—water, fire, gate, road—written on a newly whittled board, and had him trace them with his finger in a sand tray. Jingfu traced water, crooked and wobbly, and when he had traced it looked up and asked, "Can you drink it?" Jingjing said characters couldn't be drunk. Jingfu was a little disappointed, and bent his head and traced on.

But he sang well. Morning and evening the monastery sang the offices in Syriac. Jingfu didn't understand a word of it and sang along, louder than anyone. The tune he remembered after one hearing; the words he couldn't remember, so he made them up himself, and the sounds he made up were much like the real words and not quite the same, so that the old believers standing beside him wanted to laugh and didn't dare and could only bury their heads lower. Pa had once set a rule that at table they spoke Tang, because Jingjing was growing up in Chang'an and would have to live his life among the Tang. Jingfu had been born with a Tang mouth: the rustic speech from outside Chang'an, the coarse talk of old soldiers in the camp, the swearing of the ward's children—he knew every kind. Jingjing thought about it and set the rule the other way round. "At table, we speak the monastery's tongue."

"I don't know a single sentence. How can I speak it?"

"Then learn one sentence first."

The first sentence he taught him was the thanksgiving before meals. Jingfu learned it in three days, and from then on, before every meal, he said that sentence once in the sounds he had made up himself, and when he had said it touched his right hand to his forehead, his chest, and each shoulder, and then grabbed his chopsticks, touching very fast, as if afraid the food would get cold.

That winter Jingjing opened the cupboard and took out Pa's bowl. It was very coarse, fired out Lingzhou way, the glaze greyish; since Pa died it had sat upside down in the cupboard and no one had used it. He washed it twice in hot water, dried it, ladled in a bowl of mutton broth with turnip in it and meat, and set it in front of Jingfu. "Eat." Jingfu ate it, and when he had eaten it pushed the bowl forward and held it out again.

In the first year of Yongzhen the Son of Heaven in Chang'an changed twice, and people in the city said that this year the Sons of Heaven changed faster than the reign names. At one offering once in seven that autumn, Mi Jifen still came, held up by his servants, and sat in the last row by the door. Jingjing preached at the front; it was the Lord on the shore saying to the disciples, Children, have ye any meat? Halfway through, Mi Jifen dozed off again, his head nodding, his stick across his knees, one hand hanging down by his knee. Jingfu sat beside him, ten or so now, shooting up fast. He reached out and took hold of one of the old man's hanging fingers, held it, held it until worship was over. The two soldiers back from Huaixi sat on the old man's other side, the one missing an ear and the one lame in the leg, sitting very straight, and neither of them woke him.

When the offering bread came to the last row, Jingjing himself came down and went to Mi Jifen. The old man was still dozing. Jingjing did not wake him. He laid the little piece of bread in his open palm and closed his fingers over it.

That autumn Mi Jifen died. His family reckoned it up and said ninety-two. Later the Mi family carved an epitaph for him, saying he was a man of Mi, what offices he had held, that he lived to ninety-two, that he had two sons, and when it came to the younger son, it wrote one line: the monk Siyuan, residing at the Daqin Monastery. Jingjing heard that in the whole epitaph, the words Daqin Monastery appeared just that once. Once, he thought, is still once.
