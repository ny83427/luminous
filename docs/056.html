Volume Ten　Huichang

Chapter 56　The Manichaeans Go First

In the first month of the fifth year of Kaicheng, the Son of Heaven died.

The new Son of Heaven was his younger brother, twenty-seven, fond of hunting, fond of Daoist priests, not much fond of Buddhist monks. The next year the era was changed, and called Huichang.

When these things reached the Yining Ward, it was as in other years: they were spoken of a little at table, and passed. Sons of Heaven came and went, and the lamp at the monastery gate was lit every day as before, the board struck every day as before. An Yan said: the Son of Heaven is the business of heaven; we are the business of earth.

That autumn, a business of earth happened in the West Market.

Salang heard it at a Uighur warehouse in the West Market.

He often went to that warehouse. Its master was a Uighur, fat, three strings of beads round his neck, loud-voiced, who never paid porters' fees short, and never paid them over. The Uighurs in Chang'an had always been like that. For decades the Uighurs had traded horses for Tang silk, one horse for forty bolts, the horses lean, the silk fine. The court did not want to, and did not dare refuse. The Uighurs lived in Chang'an, wore brocade, rode fine horses, and when they got drunk beat people in the streets, and having beaten them, that was that.

That day Salang came in at the door with a bale on his back and saw the warehouse full of people, all Uighurs, not one of them talking.

The fat master sat in the middle with a letter clenched in his hand, his face grey.

Salang set down the bale and stood by the door waiting for his fee. He waited a long time, and no one paid him any heed. So he asked a boy beside him in a low voice what had happened.

The boy looked at him once and said: "The ordu is gone."

Salang did not understand.

The boy said: "The north. The Kyrgyz have come down; the Khagan is dead, the royal camp burned. More than a hundred thousand tents of people—scattered."

Still Salang did not quite understand. He knew only that in the north there was a Uighur kingdom, very large, very far, so large the Tang were afraid of it. He had never thought so large a thing could be gone.

He stood a while, did not ask for his fee, and went.

At the door, he heard the fat master behind him weeping. The weeping was not loud, like an ox breathing hard.

For the next two years, news from the north came in waves.

They said the Uighurs had scattered; one branch had gone west, one had come south, and the branch that came south, with a new Khagan, had camped by the Tiande and Zhenwu armies, asking for grain, asking for cities, asking the court to lend them ground to live on. The court gave grain, and no cities. So the Uighurs plundered. After a few times of plundering, the court gave no more, and sent troops to fight them.

The Uighurs in Chang'an all at once lost their swagger. The brocade robes were put away, the fine horses sold; when they got drunk they no longer went out into the streets. Several warehouses shut their doors, their masters gone who knew where.

At the one where Salang carried goods, the fat master was still there. He had grown a size thinner, and only one string of beads was left round his neck. He paid Salang more than before; Salang pushed it back, and he pushed it at him again, saying: "Take it. Who knows whether there'll be any after."

Salang took it.

That day when he got back to the monastery he gave the money to An Yan, saying it was for lamp oil.

An Yan weighed it in his hand and asked: "Where's it from?"

"A Uighur gave it."

An Yan said nothing, and put the money away. After a while he said: "The Uighurs believe in Mani."

Salang said: "I know."

"The Manichaean temples were opened because the Uighurs begged the court," said An Yan. "While the Uighurs are here, the Manichaean temples are here."

He did not go on. Salang did not ask.

In the first month of the third year of Huichang, there was a great victory in the north.

The Tang army raided the Uighur Khagan's camp by night; the Khagan was speared and fled with a few hundred horse, and of the people in the camp some died and some surrendered. The day the news of victory reached the capital, lanterns were lit in the city.

After the start of spring, the decree came down.

How the decree was worded, no one at the monastery ever saw. All that was known was these few things: the Manichaean temples in the capital and everywhere else were all to be sealed; their lands and goods taken into the government; the Manichaean scriptures and images taken and burned; and the Manichaean monks forbidden to wear their white clothes any longer, and made to change into Tang dress.

And one more thing, which got out only later. When it got out, some days had already passed, and those who told it kept their voices very low.

In the capital, more than seventy Manichaean women had died.

How they died, no one could say. Some said they were killed, some said they were starved, some said they died by their own hand. Of all the telling, only one number was the same.

More than seventy.

Salang was hired by the county to move things.

That morning a minor clerk of the county stood at the entrance of the West Market calling for porters, thirty cash a man a day and one meal. Salang pushed through; the clerk looked up at him once and said: "You. You'll do."

He went with them. Only when he got there did he learn it was the Manichaean temple.

The temple gate already stood open. At the gate stood a few constables with swords at their hips, chewing something. The courtyard was empty, the people all gone; only along the gallery stood row upon row of cupboards, their doors open. The clerk had them carry out what was in the cupboards and carry it into the street.

Salang carried one chest after another.

The chests were not heavy. In them were scriptures, roll upon roll, the paper very white, the writing very small, the ink very black—not Tang characters, not Syriac letters either. He could not read them. There were paintings too, rolled, painted on silk. One had not been rolled tight, and came open in his arms; he looked down at it once.

In the painting was a man in white, sitting in a mass of light. The light was gold, ring upon ring spreading outward. The man's face was painted very finely—fine brows, long eyes, the corners of his mouth turned up a little, as if smiling, and as if thinking.

Salang looked a while, rolled the painting up properly, and carried it out into the street.

There was already a heap in the street. The clerk had someone set fire to it.

The fire caught very fast. Paper burns fast, silk burns slow; the silk, catching, first curled up, then blackened, then broke into pieces, and the pieces were borne up by the heat and flew into the sky.

There was a wind that day.

The wind blew the ash east, blew it over the ward wall, over the street, until the whole sky was full of it. A flake of ash landed on the back of Salang's hand; he shook it, and it would not shake off. He rubbed at it with the other hand, and rubbed out a black streak.

Many people stood along the street watching. Some said: burn it well, the hu black arts. Some said nothing. One old woman watched and watched, and then turned round and went.

Salang stood by the fire with the last chest still in his arms.

The clerk shouted at him: "What are you gaping at? Throw it in!"

He threw the chest in.

In the evening he collected his thirty cash and ate that meal. The meal was millet rice, a bowl, with a few strips of pickle on top. He ate a mouthful, chewed a long time, and could not swallow it. He put the bowl down, pocketed the money, and went.

He did not go home to south of the West Market; he went to the Yining Ward.

When he got to the monastery, An Yan was wiping the stele in the front courtyard. A film of ash had settled on the stele, black and fine; every wipe left a streak. An Yan wiped it once, and again.

"Today's ash," said An Yan, "where's it from?"

Salang stood behind him, and did not answer.

That night, someone knocked at the monastery gate.

Very softly, three knocks, a pause, three more.

Salang had not gone home that day and was sleeping in the gallery of the front courtyard. He heard it first, got up, took his staff, went behind the gate, and asked: "Who's there?"

There was no answer from outside. After a while a woman's voice said: "Begging a mouthful of water."

Salang looked round. Jingfu had already come out from the back courtyard, and An Yan too, holding up an oil saucer.

An Yan shook his head at Jingfu.

Jingfu went behind the gate, drew the bolt, and opened it a crack.

Outside stood a woman of about forty in Tang cotton clothes, clothes that did not fit, the sleeves a length too long, the collar crooked. Her head was wrapped in a grey cloth, wrapped very tight, so that no hair could be seen. Her face was very white, white without a trace of colour, her lips dry and cracked.

The small lamp at the gate shone on her. She stood under the lamp, neither coming in nor stepping back.

"Begging a mouthful of water," she said again.

Jingfu opened the gate wider and stood aside to let her in.

An Yan said low behind him: "Master."

Jingfu did not turn his head. He said: "Salang, shut the gate."

The woman sat in the kitchen in the back courtyard and drank three bowls of water.

Jingfu brought her a bowl of mutton soup and a hu flatbread. She looked at the mutton soup and did not touch it; she broke off a small piece of flatbread, put it in her mouth, and chewed slowly.

"Have you any melon?" she asked.

Jingfu was taken aback. In the kitchen basket there were a few cucumbers, which Xiaoman had picked from the vegetable bed that day. He took one, washed it, and handed it to her.

The woman took the cucumber in both hands, looked at it a while, and only then bit into it. She ate very slowly, very carefully, mouthful by mouthful, even the stem.

When she had eaten it, she laid her hands on her knees and sat up straight.

"We don't eat meat," she said. "There's light in melons."

No one took her up.

She looked at Jingfu, then at An Yan, at Salang standing in the doorway, at Xiaoman hiding behind Salang's legs with only half his head showing.

"You are Daqin," she said.

"We are," said Jingfu.

"Our people used to say," she said, "that Daqin and we are the same. Both came from the west, both are hu teachings, both worship the light."

"We're not the same," said An Yan.

The woman looked at him, looked a while.

"In their eyes," she said, "we're the same."

An Yan opened his mouth, and no words came.

The kitchen was silent a long time. The fire in the stove was nearly out; only a few points of red were left. Xiaoman crept out from behind Salang's legs, went to the stove, squatted, and put in a stick of wood. The fire rose a little again.

The woman watched Xiaoman feed the fire, watched a long time.

"At our temple," she said, "there were children too."

She did not go on.

Near dawn, the woman was going.

Jingfu asked her where. She said north. In the north there were still some of their people, not many, in the hills.

Jingfu had An Yan fetch a clean set of clothes, left over from the monastery's giving of clothes—Tang, coarse cloth, but they fitted. The woman changed into them, folded the ill-fitting set she had come in, and laid it on the bench in the kitchen.

Jingfu also wrapped up a few flatbreads for her, two cucumbers, a small string of cash.

The woman took them, and did not thank him. She went to the back door, stopped, and turned round.

"When they burned the scriptures," she said, "I was watching in the street."

Jingfu nodded.

"There was a big fellow," she said, "holding a painting, who looked at it a long time before he threw it in."

She glanced at Salang.

Salang bowed his head.

The woman turned, went out of the back door, and into the lane. The lane was still dark, and in a moment her shape could not be seen. Only her footsteps were heard, very soft, further and further off.

Salang stood at the back door a long time.

Jingfu came and stood beside him.

"Thirty cash," said Salang.

Jingfu said nothing.

"When I carry goods," said Salang, "it's thirty cash a day too."

Still Jingfu said nothing. He reached out and pressed Salang's arm, pressed it a while, and let go.

That morning, An Yan did not go to strike the board.

He sat in the kitchen looking at that folded set of clothes on the bench. The clothes were Tang, blue cotton, long-sleeved, worn through at the collar and patched once, the stitches very coarse, not like a woman's patching.

When Jingfu came in, he did not raise his head.

"Master," he said, "if anyone saw her—going out of our back door."

"Then they saw," said Jingfu.

"If the constables ask, and the asking comes round to us, what do we say?"

"We say she begged a mouthful of water."

"More than a mouthful."

"Three bowls," said Jingfu. "And a cucumber."

An Yan raised his head and looked at him. There was no smile on Jingfu's face, and no annoyance; it was as always. An Yan looked a while, and lowered his head again.

"When the Master was alive," he said, "he wouldn't have done this."

Jingfu thought and said: "The Master would."

An Yan said nothing more.

He thought of many years ago, squatting on top of the monastery wall at ten, not a child of the monastery, nor anybody's invited guest—just a boy who had climbed a wall to see the excitement. An old man with a scar on his brow had come over, taken half a flatbread from his coat, and handed it to him. The old man had not asked whose boy he was, nor whether he believed.

He stood up, took that set of clothes, and went to the stove. The fire in the stove was not yet out; he held the clothes up, held them a while.

Then he put the clothes down, took them to the front courtyard, and pushed them into the wooden chest for giving clothes, at the very bottom.

He came back, took down the board from the gallery, and struck it.

Bang, bang, bang.

Half an hour later than usual.

A few days later, Jingfu went to the Buzheng Ward.

In the Buzheng Ward there was a fire temple, where Sogdians and Persians worshipped fire. In the temple was an old priest, older than An Yan, his beard hanging to his chest, his eyes cloudy, so that he had to bring them very close to look at you. When Jingfu was small he had come here with his master, and the old priest, not old then, had given him a honey cake.

The fire in the temple was still burning. The old priest sat beside it and did not rise.

Jingfu sat down across from him. The two of them sat with the fire between them a long time, and neither spoke first.

Then the old priest said: "First, them."

Jingfu said: "Yes."

The old priest nodded and put a piece of wood on the fire. He put it on very carefully, as if afraid of smothering the fire.

"Your stele," said the old priest. "Still standing?"

"Still standing."

The old priest nodded again. He said nothing more.

When Jingfu rose to take his leave, the old priest stood up too and saw him to the door. There was wind at the door, which blew the temple fire slantwise a moment, and it stood up straight again.

The old priest looked at the fire and said one thing. When Jingfu told An Yan about it afterwards, An Yan asked him what he had said, and he thought a long time and said: I can't remember.

In truth he remembered. What the old priest said was: "Fire can't be shut up. But houses can be pulled down."

At the end of that month, Lü Shao came.

He came at night, the ward gate about to shut; he had run all the way, and when he came in he was too out of breath to speak. Jingfu poured him a bowl of water, and he drank it, and only then spoke.

He said that someone had come to the Imperial Library and had them go through the library's books and pick out all the Manichaean scriptures. Those picked out—not one roll was to be kept.

"When they'd finished picking," said Lü Shao, "the man asked one more thing."

"What?"

Lü Shao looked at Jingfu.

"He asked whether the Daqin scriptures and the Manichaean scriptures were the same thing."

No one in the room spoke.

"What did you say?" asked Jingfu.

"I said I only copy," said Lü Shao. "Whatever the scripture is, it's not my concern."

"And the man?"

"The man said: good," said Lü Shao. "And went."

Jingfu nodded.

Lü Shao sat a while, rose, and went. At the door he looked back at the stele in the courtyard. At night the characters on it could not be seen; only a dark shape, taller than two men.

That night when Lü Shao got home, he took down the pile of scriptures in the corner one by one and moved them under the bed.

His wife woke and asked what he was doing.

He said: "Go to sleep."
