Volume Twelve　Baghdad

Chapter 64　Sogdiana

West from Yizhou they walked half a month, and reached Xizhou.

Xizhou city stood in an oasis, gobi all round it, and everywhere in the city were water channels, vine trellises and white poplars. The city wall was of rammed yellow earth, very thick, very high. All this Salang had heard of. The Master had told him; the Master's master had told the Master; the Master's master, at eleven, had written it on a roll of paper.

He went into the city, looked, and thought that the roll of paper had it right.

Only the people of the city were not quite as the paper said. Fewer spoke Tang, more spoke Tibetan, and some spoke Uighur. Tibetan soldiers stood at the city gate, and Uighurs went through the streets of the city in bands, on horseback, bows on their backs. Old He said that Xizhou had changed masters several times these last years, and nobody could say whose it was now.

Salang went to find the monastery north of the city.

He asked several people before he found out. It was an old man selling raisins who told him: north of the city, by the water channel, the one with a cross carved over the door.

"Anyone still there?" Salang asked.

The old man thought and said: "There's a gatekeeper."

The monastery was north of the city, by the water channel.

The gate was not large; a cross was carved on the lintel, the four ends of the cross rounded, like four leaves. One leaf of the gate stood open; the other hung askew, leaning against the wall. In the courtyard there was a mulberry tree, very old, its trunk split with great gashes, the gashes stuffed with mud.

Under the tree sat a man, weaving a basket.

The man was sixty or so, a hu, but dressed in Tang clothes, very old clothes. He looked up and saw Salang, saw the staff in Salang's hand, saw the iron tip of the staff, and the basket in his hands went still.

Salang stood at the gate and said: "I've come from Chang'an."

The man put down the basket and stood up.

He took Salang round the monastery once. It was not large: one hall, a few rooms. The hall was very dark, with a smell of earth and old wood. On the east wall a wooden cross was nailed, the wood gone black. There were no scriptures in the hall; the scripture cupboard was empty, its doors open.

"The scriptures?" Salang asked.

"In my grandfather's time," the man said, "they were sold. No one read them, and there was no money."

Salang nodded.

The man said that his grandfather had kept the gate of this monastery, and before his grandfather there had been an old elder, who came from the west and later went to Chang'an. When he left, he left the man's grandfather to look after it. His grandfather died, and he went on looking after it. Every year a dozen-odd people still came, to worship, to say the prayers; what the words meant, most of them no longer understood, so they said them to the tune.

"Of people from Chang'an," the man said, "you're the first."

He paused, and asked: "The monastery in Chang'an—is it well?"

Salang thought and said: "Buried."

The man looked at him, and asked no more.

When Salang was about to go, the man caught hold of him, led him round behind the hall, and pointed to a pillar.

The pillar was of white poplar, very thick, and at a man's height two characters were carved in it. Tang characters, carved very shallow, crooked and wobbly, scratched out stroke by stroke with the point of a knife.

Salang knew few characters.

But of these two, he knew the first. It was in the Master's name. When people at the monastery wrote the Master's name, they always wrote this character; he had looked at it for over twenty years, and knew it well.

"What is it?" the man asked. "My grandfather said a Tang boy carved it. Many years ago. When he'd carved it, he got a scolding."

Salang stood before the pillar and looked a long time.

He reached out and felt the two characters. They were carved shallow; under the fingers they were only little grooves, one after another.

"I can't read," he said.

The man nodded, and said he couldn't either.

Salang drew his hand back, stood before the pillar a while, and nodded to the two characters.

Then he went out.

West from Xizhou, Old He's caravan did not take the southern road but the northern one. Over the Tianshan, past Beiting, past Luntai, on and on west, the road went over the steppe.

The roads of the steppe were easier going than the gobi. There was grass, there was water, there were herdsmen. The herdsmen were Turks, Uighurs, Qarluqs, and some Salang could not tell what they were. They rode up, looked over the caravan, asked whether there were iron pots, whether there was silk, traded a little, and went.

They walked two months and came to a river, the river Talas.

Old He pointed to a stretch of flat ground by the river and said that a hundred years ago the Tang army had fought the Arabs here and lost; tens of thousands had died, and those who lived were carried off west by the Arabs.

Salang looked at the flat ground.

There was nothing on it but grass, very tall grass, which the wind blew down in swathes and which rose again in swathes. What was under the grass could not be seen.

"The ones carried off?" asked Salang.

"Some died, some stayed out there," said Old He. "Some came back too. When I was young I heard tell of a Tang man, a scholar, who lived in the Arab country a dozen years or more, and afterwards went home on a sea-going ship, and when he got home wrote a book."

Salang had never heard of this book.

The Master had probably heard of it. The Master's master had probably heard of it too. But the Master was not here.

He looked at the grass a while, and went on with the caravan.

After the start of spring, they reached Chach. Past Chach, south, over a range of hills, was Kang—the country of Samarkand.

The city of Kang was called Samarkand.

The day they reached Samarkand, Kang Zhen said nothing the whole way.

He rode on his camel, his eyes fixed ahead. The nearer they came, the harder he looked, so hard that when the camel slowed he did not notice, and Salang behind slapped the camel's rump, and the camel lurched forward, and he nearly fell off.

The city was on high ground. The city wall was of earth, very long, its end out of sight; outside the wall were channels, one after another, trees planted along the channels, and under the trees fields, the wheat in the fields just up, green.

At the city gate stood soldiers. The soldiers wore black and had cloths wound round their heads—not what Kang Zhen remembered. Over the city gate there was writing, in a script Salang had never seen, curving, run together, like a rope tied in many knots.

The people in the city—Kang Zhen understood half of what they said.

He had not spoken Sogdian for thirty-five years. In Chang'an there had been Sogdians in the monastery, but the monastery's people spoke Tang, and at table only Tang was spoken; it was the monastery's old rule. His Sogdian was still the Sogdian of fifteen.

He stood at the city gate, listening to the people in the streets talk, listened a long time.

"What they speak," he said to Elijah, "isn't what it was when I was small."

Elijah asked: "How not?"

Kang Zhen thought.

"There are a lot of words I don't know," he said. "And a lot of the ones I knew are gone."

Kang Zhen searched three days, and found the channel.

The channel was in the south-east of the city, coming down from the hills, passing through an orchard planted with peach and apricot trees. Along the channel was a row of houses, earth-walled, flat-roofed, their doors opening onto the channel; out of the door, and there was the water.

He stood by the channel and looked along the row of houses, one after another.

At the fifth, he stopped.

The door was new. The wall was old; at the corner of the wall there was a stone, and in the stone a crack. He remembered that crack. When he was small he had sat on that stone and put his feet in the channel, put them in a while, till they ached with cold, drew them out, and put them in again.

The door opened and a woman came out, thirty or so, holding a child.

She saw several people standing at her door—a white-bearded old hu monk, a fearsomely tall big man, and a yellow-faced man of middle years staring at the corner of her wall. She stepped back and asked them who they were looking for.

Kang Zhen said a name.

It was his mother's name.

The woman shook her head. She said she had never heard it. She said she had married into this house seven years ago; her father-in-law had bought the house, and when he bought it the family who had it before were long gone.

"Where did they go?"

"I don't know," said the woman. "My father-in-law said that when he bought it, the house had stood empty a good many years."

Kang Zhen stood there and said nothing.

The woman looked at him a while, and went back into the house with her child. She did not shut the door; it stood open a crack. After a while she came out again with a bowl of water in her hand, and held it out to Kang Zhen.

Kang Zhen took it and drank.

It was water from the channel. Very cold.

In the city of Samarkand there was a church.

The church was in the west of the city, a brick building, smaller than the Daqin monastery in Chang'an, larger than the one north of Xizhou. A cross was carved over the door; on the roof there was no cross. Old He said that in the old days there had been one on the roof; a few years ago it had been taken down, for the officials here would not allow a church's cross to stand higher than other people's roofs.

In the church there was a bishop, past seventy, his beard white as fleece. And two priests, a deacon, a few dozen of the faithful.

When the bishop heard they had come from Chang'an, he invited them into the back courtyard, sat them down, and had grapes and bread brought. He asked many things. He asked about the monastery in Chang'an, about the people of Chang'an, about the stele. Elijah answered each in turn, very slowly; when he came to Joseph, he stopped, stopped a long time.

When the bishop had heard it all, he made the sign of the cross.

Then he asked Kang Zhen what he meant to do.

Kang Zhen said he did not know.

The bishop thought and said that the children of the faithful in the city could none of them read Syriac any more. Those who could say the prayers could only say them to the tune; what they were saying, they did not understand. Of their priests here, one had lost an eye, and the other's hand shook, so that he could not write.

"Can you write?" the bishop asked.

"I can," said Kang Zhen. "Syriac letters, Tang characters, I can write both."

"And Sogdian?"

Kang Zhen thought and said: "I've forgotten half."

The bishop nodded. "That half," he said, "the people here will help you remember."

Kang Zhen said nothing.

He lowered his head and looked at his hands. His hands were all callus, worn there on this road, and worn there in those twenty-odd years in Chang'an. Mending shoes, mending clothes, sweeping the courtyard, copying scripture.

"In Chang'an," he said, "whatever the Master told me to do, I did."

The bishop looked at him.

"Here," said the bishop, "it's the same."

The next day, Kang Zhen was going to buy paper.

The bishop said that to teach children to write, one needed paper. Paper was bought in the paper-makers' street in the city; the paper of Samarkand, even the Arabs said it was good, and it was sold all the way to Baghdad.

Salang went with him, to carry it.

The paper-makers' street was in the north of the city, along a great channel. On both sides of the street were workshops, one against the next, and before their doors were vats of something soaking, whitish—rotted rags and hemp, giving off a sour smell. In the workshops men pounded with wooden mallets in stone mortars, dong, dong, dong, till the whole street was full of that sound. Further in, men took a bamboo screen, scooped it up through the vat, lifting a thin film of pulp, and slapped it on the wall, till the wall was covered, white, drying in the sun.

Salang stood at the door of one workshop and watched a long time.

He had seen paper in Chang'an. Heaped in Lü Shao's house, kept in the monastery's scripture cupboard, stack on stack in the Master's room. He had never once thought where paper came from.

The master of the workshop, an old Sogdian, hearing Kang Zhen speak Sogdian, and seeing Salang's face, asked where they had come from.

Kang Zhen said: Chang'an.

The old man was taken aback, and put down the bamboo screen in his hands.

"My grandfather's grandfather," the old man said, "learned this from the Tang."

He said that a hundred years ago, after that battle at Talas, the Arabs had brought back a good many Tang soldiers, and among them were a few who at home had been paper-makers. The people of Samarkand had made paper before, but made it badly; it tore if you tugged it. Those few Tang men came and taught them, and the paper of Samarkand was good, and had been good ever since.

"And those Tang men?" asked Salang.

The old man thought and said: "Died here. Their graves are outside the city. Nobody tends them now."

He peeled a sheet of paper from the wall, peeled it very carefully, and held it out to Salang.

"Take it," he said. "Your people's craft."

Salang took it. The paper was very thin, very white; held up to the sun, you could see the fibres in it, strand on strand. He held it in his hand and dared not grip it hard, for fear of tearing it.

He thought of the grassland by the river Talas.

Kang Zhen bought a ream of paper and paid. Salang hoisted the ream onto his shoulder. The single sheet he folded and tucked into his coat, beside the rubbing with white letters on black.

On the way back, Kang Zhen asked him: "You can't even read. What do you want paper for?"

Salang thought and said: "For the Master."

"The Master's on the steppe."

"Mm," said Salang.

He did not take the paper out.

Elijah and Salang stayed in Samarkand twenty days.

In those twenty days, Kang Zhen went to the church every morning and came back in the evening. When he came back there was ink on his hands and ink on his sleeves. He told Elijah how many children he had taught that day, how many characters. Children were hard to teach; they would not sit still.

Elijah said: when Jingjing taught you all, you wouldn't sit still either.

Kang Zhen thought and said: I sat still.

Elijah laughed. In all that road, it was the first time Salang had seen him laugh.

Old He's caravan was going on west, across the Amu, to Merv, to Nishapur. Elijah said they would go with it.

On the evening before they left, Kang Zhen did not go to the church. He went alone to the channel in the south-east of the city.

Salang followed. He did not follow too close; he stood far off, under an apricot tree.

Kang Zhen went up to the row of houses, to the fifth. The door was shut; inside it a child was crying and a woman soothing. He did not knock. He went to the stone at the corner of the wall, looked at the crack, and sat down.

He took off his shoes, and took off his socks too.

He put both feet into the channel.

The water of the channel came down from the hills; it was spring now, the snow on the hills was melting, and the water was colder than in winter. As his feet went in, his whole body shook once.

Salang watched from under the apricot tree. He thought Kang Zhen would draw them out.

Kang Zhen did not draw them out. He sat on the stone, both hands braced on it, both feet soaking in the channel, not moving at all. The water ran over the tops of his feet, rushing, and flowed away.

The sun went down. In the orchard the apricot trees darkened one by one.

Kang Zhen was still sitting there.
