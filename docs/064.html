Volume Ten　Morning Praises

Chapter 64　Lady An

The letter from Luoyang came early in the first month of the third year of Dahe, brought by a young man of the Mi family who traded silk between the two capitals. On the cover was written To the Great Worthy Lingbao, Daqin Monastery, Yining Ward, Chang'an. Inside were two sheets: one in Chinese characters, written by a monk called Qingsu; one in Syriac, written by Xuanying, head of the Luoyang monastery. They told of one matter.

Qingsu's mother, the Venerable Lady An, had been buried east of the city of Luoyang in the Yuanhe years, and before her grave stood a pillar with scripture carved on it and crosses carved on it. Now the ground had been flooded, and the grave had to be moved to a newly bought burial ground south of the city; the day was fixed for the sixteenth of the second month, and the pillar was to be moved with it and set up again. Only three old monks were left at the monastery in Luoyang: Xuanying was eighty and his legs could not walk; the Great Worthy of the Nine Grades, Zhitong, was ill; the Great Worthy of Deportment, Xuanqing, could not conduct it alone. Could Chang'an send someone to preside over the rite of this reburial?

That year thirty-one stood at the morning. Since the Baoli years some had gone and some had died; two more old monks had passed away, and two more of the ten who had left had come back to fill their places. There were still twenty names on the monk register, and the one kept for one who is to come was still empty.

When Lingbao had read the letter, he handed both sheets to Jingfu.

"I can't go," he said. "Without me, no one would bless the bread at the offering once in seven." That spring he was coughing again, coughing so that he couldn't sleep at night, and had to catch his breath after every sentence. "You go, and take Joseph. Joseph can recite the scriptures in Syriac; for the rite of reburial, the two of you together will be enough. As for the offering bread, at Luoyang there's Xuanqing. He's a priest Brother called to Chang'an to lay hands on in the years he was bishop. He can bless it, and you two can help him."

"Uncle Lingbao, what does the pillar look like?"

"I've never seen it. I only know that in the ninth year of Yuanhe the Luoyang monastery set up a pillar for the Venerable Lady An. Brother was still alive that year. The scripture carved on it was one Brother translated in Chang'an; the people of Luoyang copied it and carved it in stone." Lingbao folded the letter. "Your Master's hand—you've looked at it in the scripture room in Chang'an for more than ten years. Go to Luoyang and look at it in stone."

The two of them set out on the twentieth of the first month on the monastery's old donkey, riding by turns. Out by the Chunming Gate and east, through the Tong Pass, through Shanzhou; they travelled eleven days and entered the city of Luoyang on the first of the second month.

The Daqin Monastery of Luoyang was in the Xiushan Ward, smaller than Chang'an's: one hall, two rows of rooms, an old scholar tree in the middle of the courtyard. At the monastery gate stood a man of fifty or so, thin and tall, in a blue robe washed pale. When he saw them he first wished them peace in Syriac, and then again in Tang.

"I am Qingsu. The Venerable Lady An was my mother; Xuanying, head of the monastery, is my master." He took the donkey's reins. "My mother's grave I've kept fifteen years."

That night Jingfu saw Xuanying. Xuanying lay in a room on the east side, his eyes clouded, his hands still warm. He took Jingfu's hand and felt the back of it. "Whose disciple are you?"

"Jingjing's."

Xuanying's hand stopped.

"Jingjing—I've met him," he said. "In the winter of the fourth year of Dali he came to Luoyang with Elder Yonan to see old Jacob, and lived a winter in the Xiushan Ward. I was twenty then and he was twenty-three, and the two of us sat at night under the scholar tree talking scripture, stamping our feet with cold, talking till cockcrow."

He drew a breath.

"Old Jacob died that winter. Elder Yonan recited the scripture at his bedside, and when he'd finished went alone to the well to wash his hands, washed them three times. Jingjing and I stood in the gallery watching, and neither of us dared go over," he said. "That winter Jingjing told me, the scriptures must be translated into words the Tang can listen to, and then the Tang will be willing to listen; once the Tang have listened, they'll know Aluohe isn't the god of the hu alone."

He coughed several times. Jingfu brought him water; he drank a mouthful and took hold of Jingfu's hand again. "That scripture of your Master's is carved in stone. Go and look."

Early the next morning Qingsu took Jingfu and Joseph out of the city and seven or eight li east, to the edge of a hollow. The rains had been heavy that year, and the Yi and the Luo had both risen. The water in the hollow had gone down, but the ground was still wet; at every step your sole came up with mud. In the middle of the hollow was a grave, its mound half fallen in, and before the grave stood a stone pillar.

What Jingfu saw first was the pillar's shape. Eight faces, taller than a man, a cap on top with a ring of lotus petals carved round its edge, and under the shaft a base, with lotus on the base too. He had seen pillars like this in Chang'an: at the gate of the Jianfu Monastery, at the gate of the Daxingshan Monastery, at the gate of the little temple by the West Market—all Buddhist things, called sutra pillars, carved with the Uṣṇīṣa Vijaya dhāraṇī. The Buddhists said that if the shadow of the pillar fell on a man, his sins were washed away.

Joseph, beside him, spoke first. "This is a monk's pillar."

Qingsu did not take it up. He went forward and wiped the mud from the top of the pillar with his sleeve. Under the cap, on the face toward them, a cross was carved, and below the cross a lotus, and on either side of the cross a figure, ribbons flying up, soaring upward, hands holding something up—the flying celestials the Buddhists paint. Qingsu wiped one face and went round to wipe the next. Of the eight faces, four were carved with crosses and celestials, and on the other four were characters, cut in vertical lines. The first line Jingfu read aloud: The Scripture of the Luminous Teaching of Daqin on Proclaiming the Origin and Reaching the Root.

Joseph looked at those four crosses, and his face was not good to see.

"Director, this isn't right," he said in Syriac, very fast. "Why is there a lotus under the cross? Why are there those flying people beside the cross? Those are Buddhist things. In the monasteries of Merv, the monasteries of Baghdad, under the cross there are steps, there's a stone, there's a vine; no lotus, no flying people. If the Patriarchate's people saw this, they'd say the monasteries of Chang'an and Luoyang had all been eaten up by the Buddhists."

Qingsu understood Syriac. He stood on the other side of the pillar, a fold of his sleeve still gripped for wiping, and stopped.

"This pillar my elder brother and I set up," he said. "My brother died a few years ago too; in the inscription I'm the younger, and my name comes after his. In the ninth year of Yuanhe my mother died, and we brothers wanted to put up a mark for her. The burial ground we bought that winter from a man named Cui Xingben. The ground lay low; we didn't know, and bought it. The stele in Chang'an is stone and too big; we couldn't put up anything like it. I asked my master, and my master said the people of Luoyang all set up pillars. Set up a pillar and carve scripture on it; scripture is for the living to recite and for the dead too; people passing by see it and recite a line, and that's enough."

He turned and looked at the cross on the pillar.

"I got a stonemason from east of the city. The stonemason could only carve pillars, only carve lotuses, only carve celestials. I said I wanted a cross; he couldn't do it, so I drew one on paper myself and he carved it from that. The lotus he added himself, and the celestials too; he said a pillar without a lotus and without celestials wouldn't look like a pillar. I thought of having him grind them off, and in the end I didn't," he said. "The place where my mother knelt every morning at the monastery while she lived was an old Buddhist kneeling cushion, with lotuses embroidered on it. That cushion she knelt on thirty years, knelt on till she died."

Joseph did not swallow it.

"Brother Qingsu, I know the stonemason could only carve lotuses and the kneeling cushion had lotuses embroidered on it. But the Patriarchate's people don't know that. What the Patriarchate's people would see is just a monk's pillar." He turned to Jingfu. "Director, it was for exactly this that Elder David in his day stopped the hymn in Tang in Chang'an for a month. I was in the hall that month too, standing behind Elder David, watching the back rows empty, row by row. Elder David saw, and only then changed his word and said, sing it back again. But singing it back didn't mean letting everything go its own way. Before Elder David died he told me he couldn't read the Chang'an monastery, and told me and Elijah to watch it for him, watch that it didn't go too far; if it went too far, it couldn't come back."

He looked at the lotus.

"I've watched thirty years. How far is too far, I don't know. The lotus I can bear, the flying people I can bear; but a cross standing on a lotus—when I look at it, it's as if something in my heart were being trodden on."

"Brother Joseph, don't be hasty yet," said Jingfu. "Let me finish looking."

He looked at all four crosses, then went round to look at the faces with the characters.

The first face was the opening passage of the scripture, and he read it aloud: The Luminous-Pervading Dharma King, in the city of Nasaluo in the land of Daqin… What Nasaluo was, Jingfu knew: in the Syriac scriptures it was Nazareth, the little town where the Lord grew up. When his Master translated it he thought for three nights and used these three syllables, and said that when the Tang heard it they'd know it was a far-off place where there was a town, and in the town were people, and that was enough.

The second face told of the Dharma King opening the truth to living beings, spoke of the beginningless, the limitless, the boundless, of that which cannot be spoken, in the words the Master loved best: a miao, wondrous; a zhen, true; a ji, still. These words Jingfu had looked at in the scripture room in Chang'an for more than ten years. When the Master translated, he had ground the ink, prepared the vermilion, copied, read back to the Master; the Master would say this sentence doesn't run, change it; that word is too Buddhist, replace it. Changed and changed again, and at the end, what stayed were still those few words. The Master said that in the speech of the Tang there were only these few words that could let the Tang know there was something they did not know.

He read on sentence by sentence, in Tang; Joseph understood half. Qingsu stood on the other side of the pillar, his lips moving with Jingfu's; Qingsu knew it by heart.

The third face carried the record of the pillar, the words of Qingsu and his brother: their mother's name, when the pillar was set up, and that the pillar stood here so that those who passed should know that the one beneath believed in the Three-in-One.

Jingfu reached out to touch it. The stone was cold; the characters cut into it were packed with mud in their strokes, and he dug it out stroke by stroke with his fingernail. When he came to the sentence in the record which said that whomever the pillar's shadow fell on, his sins were forgiven by the God of the Three-in-One, his hand stopped. He had heard monks say this at the gate of the Jianfu Monastery, said of the Vijaya pillar; Qingsu had taken it over and put in another God. He did not know whether he should read on. He read on.

He lowered his hand, turned, and said to Joseph, "Brother Joseph, when the Master translated this scripture in his day, if the Patriarchate's people had seen it they'd have said the Chang'an monastery had been eaten up by the Buddhists too. They said it for decades, said it until he died, and he didn't change it."

Joseph did not answer.

"The cross Brother Qingsu drew, the lotus and the flying people the stonemason added, the words are the Master's. When the grave is moved, the pillar goes with the grave, and the lotus goes with it, and the flying people too; not one piece is ground off," said Jingfu. "Whether it's gone too far, I don't know either. What you said, I'll keep in mind for you. If one day you see me going too far, say it again."

Joseph looked at him, looked at the pillar again, neither nodded nor argued any more. He bent and wiped the mud from the pillar's base with his sleeve, and when he came to the ring of lotus on the base he wiped it very carefully, petal by petal.

The reburial was on the sixteenth of the second month, before it was light.

Those who came were Jingfu, Joseph and Qingsu; Qingsu's cousin Shaocheng, and his uncle on his mother's side, An Shaolian; a sworn uncle of Qingsu's, an unassigned officer in the Left Longwu Army of the capital, who had taken leave and hurried from Chang'an, a man in his forties in an army robe, no sword at his waist, bringing two soldiers to help carry; and the family servant who kept the grave, Chang'er, past fifty, who had built a straw hut at the edge of the hollow and kept watch fifteen years. Chang'er went down into the pit alone first and loosened the earth round the four corners of the coffin. Xuanqing came hurrying from the city, past seventy, on a donkey; at the grave he got down from the donkey, steadied himself, and took from his breast a cloth bundle. In the bundle were a piece of bread and a small flask of wine, which he had made ready at the monastery the night before.

Before the coffin was raised, Joseph recited the scripture in Syriac, the passage that says man comes from the dust and returns to the dust, but Aluohe does not forget those in the dust. He recited a sentence, and Jingfu said it in Tang for Qingsu, Shaocheng, An Shaolian and the others. The officer stood at the back with his hands hanging. He was not a believer; halfway through he bowed his head.

Xuanqing blessed the bread, broke it, and gave each believer present a small piece. Chang'er climbed out of the pit with both hands covered in mud, and Xuanqing laid the bread in his palm.

"Your Reverence, my hands are dirty."

"The hands are dirty; the bread isn't."

Chang'er wiped his hands on the front of his robe, took it, and ate.

The coffin came up, the wood rotted through at one corner by the water. Qingsu knelt at the edge of the pit and held up the rotted corner with his hands, held it all the way onto the cart. The new burial ground was south of the city, sought out with Qingsu's own savings from his robe-and-bowl money—three mu, high ground, against an earth bank planted with cypresses. The cart travelled most of the day to the new ground, where the pit had been dug a few days before. The coffin was lowered, and the earth shovelled in spadeful by spadeful: Chang'er a spadeful, Qingsu a spadeful, and Jingfu a spadeful too.

The pillar was set up the next day. Four men carried the shaft and the two soldiers carried the base. The base went down first, the shaft stood on it, the cap was set on last. Of the ring of lotus at the cap's edge, one petal was missing when it was set on, knocked off in the moving. Qingsu crouched on the ground, found the petal, and put it in his breast. When the pillar stood, the face with the cross, the lotus and the flying people faced the grave; the faces with the characters faced the road by which people came.

Qingsu added a passage to the record of the pillar, in words Jingfu thought of for him: on the sixteenth of the second month of the third year of Dahe the great matter of reburial was performed; where it was moved to; who was present—Xuanying, head of the monastery; Xuanqing, Great Worthy of Deportment; Zhitong, Great Worthy of the Nine Grades; two men came from the Daqin Monastery of Chang'an. When he got there, Qingsu stopped his brush.

"Brother Jingfu, your two names—shall I carve them or not?"

Jingfu thought. "Don't. This pillar is your mother's. Those carved on it are those who keep watch over her. The two of us, when the moving is done, go back to Chang'an. Carve us on it, and those passing who read it won't know who these two are."

"Then I'll write: two men came from Chang'an."

"Don't write the two men either."

The night before they went back to Chang'an, Jingfu went to Xuanying's room again. Xuanying was awake and heard him come in. "Is the pillar up?"

"It's up."

"Is the lotus still there?"

"It's there. One petal missing; Brother Qingsu is keeping it."

Xuanying laughed. "Of that petal, I keep a piece here too. In the ninth year of Yuanhe, when the pillar was set up, the stonemason spoiled one piece, and Qingsu picked it up and kept it in my room, and it's been kept here till now. Feel under the bed."

Jingfu reached under the bed and drew out a wooden box. He opened it; inside was a piece of stone the size of a palm, carved with half a cross, carved crooked. The stonemason had carved it crooked, chiselled it off, and carved it again.

"Take it and show Lingbao," said Xuanying. "Tell him the Luoyang monastery is still here, and this pillar is still here too. From now on, if there's anything in Chang'an, there are people in the Luoyang monastery; if there's anything in Luoyang, there are people in the Chang'an monastery too."

Jingfu wrapped the piece of stone and put it in his breast.

Early the next morning he and Joseph went out of the city of Luoyang and west. When they reached the earth bank outside the city they looked back once. The cypresses at the foot of the bank were too far off; they could not see the pillar, only a layer of white frost lying on the tips of the cypresses.
