Chapter 63　Sizhou

On the day Guangzheng left, thirty-seven stood at the morning; by the twelfth month of the fourth year of Changqing, thirty-four.

Of the three fewer, one was Old Cao. In the autumn, splitting firewood, he sat down on the woodpile with the axe still in his hand, and was gone. His name came off the monk register, and the younger Shi brother came back with his bundle and filled his place; when Lingbao reported to the inspectorate, there were still twenty on the register, and the one kept for one who is to come was still empty. One was Granny Wang, an old believer at the east end of the ward, who moved out of the city to her son's house. And one was Rijin of the Kang family. He was still in Chang'an, alive and well, and from the tenth month on he stopped coming.

The Kangs were old believers of the ward, Sogdians, who had lived several generations at the south end of the Yining Ward, in the stretch by the ward wall—three low rooms, and a jujube tree in the yard. Kang Da drove a cart for hire in the West Market; he was fifty-nine, an honest man of few words, and at the offering once in seven stood on the men's side nearest the wall. His wife the ward all called Mother Kang. In the sixth year of Zhenyuan her three-year-old child had diarrhoea, and at every offering once in seven she hid a piece of the offering bread and took it home and soaked it in well water for the child to drink, and the child did not live; the older people of the ward all remembered it. Afterward she went with Baaluo to the south of the city and lived more than ten years in the straw shed west of the Qujiang Pool. In the winter of the first year of Yuanhe, when Jingjing tramped through the snow to the south of the city, she came back with the seven households of An Yan and the others. She was forty-two the year she came back, and the next year she bore Rijin. She was sixty this year, and he was her only son.

Rijin was eighteen and drove carts in the West Market with his Pa—strong, quick-witted, good at both Tang and Sogdian. He came to the offering once in seven and came to the morning too, but when he came he stood at the back and dozed; Jingfu had seen it several times. Jingfu was eleven years older than he. When Rijin was five or six, Jingfu was carrying rice by the gruel shed in the ward street, and Rijin followed behind insisting on taking one end, hugging a corner of the rice sack with both arms; he carried it a few steps, then let go and ran after a dog of the ward. Jingfu dragged the rice back alone and looked round, and he was crouched at the foot of the wall, laughing at Jingfu together with the dog.

That winter the Son of Heaven wanted to build halls.

The new Son of Heaven had been on the throne nearly a year. He loved hunting and he loved polo; this year the palace built this hall, next year that court, and Chang'an County drafted labourers by household, drafted them again and again. The Yining Ward's turn came twice, and both times the Kangs were drafted. The first time Kang Da went and hauled stone in the palace twenty days, and came back unable to straighten his back. The second time Rijin went in his place, another twenty days. In the twelfth month the ward headman knocked at the door again: there was another draft in spring, to repair the embankment south of the city, and the Kangs were on the roll again. Kang Da's back had not mended, and Rijin was eighteen, a youth of the middle grade, and the county said youths of the middle grade could be drafted too.

When the ward headman had gone, Mother Kang came to the monastery to find Jingfu. Early the next morning Jingfu went to the Chang'an county office and waited in the gallery until past noon before he saw the clerk in charge of drafts.

"The Kang husband is fifty-nine and his back injury hasn't healed. There's only one son in the house, and he's only eighteen," said Jingfu. "Could they be excused this time? Or the monastery could send two men in the Kangs' place. The monastery has young fellows who could go and carry earth for the embankment in his stead."

The clerk turned the pages of the roll. "Labourers are drafted by household. The household is the Kangs' household, so the men have to be the Kangs' men. The monastery's people are monks, and monks don't do labour service. Monks carrying earth on the embankment breaks the rules; if the censors heard, they'd say the county was using monks as coolies."

"Then the monastery will pay to hire two men in his stead."

"Hiring men in one's stead for service—the Kangs have to do the hiring themselves. If the money comes from the monastery, that's the monastery paying service for a registered household, another matter no one can sort out." The clerk pushed the roll aside. "Your Reverence, take my advice and leave it. In spring it's only twenty days; once the hauling's done they come home. No one dies of it."

"The Kangs have already lost a child."

The clerk raised his head and looked at him. "What family in this city hasn't lost a child?" He closed the roll.

Jingfu came back and told Mother Kang exactly how it was.

"I understand," said Mother Kang. "The monastery can't manage the government's affairs; I don't blame the monastery. I only came to ask."

As she left she stood a moment before the lamp at the gate. Jingfu had blown it out that morning before he went out; the dish was still warm.

It was just in those days that a group of merchants from the Jiang-Huai region came to the West Market and said an ordination platform had been opened at Sizhou.

They said that Minister Wang of Xuzhou, military governor of Xu and Si, had opened a platform at Sizhou where people were tonsured; each paid two strings of cash and was given a certificate on the spot. With a certificate in hand, you were a monk with a name and a register, and monks did no labour service and paid no Twice-a-Year Tax, and the family's property could even be hung under the monk's name. The merchants said that in the Jiang-Huai country a family with three sons always sent one to have his head shaved; at the Suanshan ferry at Runzhou a hundred-odd people a day crossed the river, all going north to Sizhou to be shaved, and the boatmen had more business than they could take.

The people of the West Market who heard it—some told it as a joke, and some were tempted. A few young carters and porters met at night to reckon the distance: from Chang'an to Sizhou, east along the Bian Canal, seventeen or eighteen hundred li; ride the empty grain barges and you'd go fast, twenty-odd days. Rijin was one of that group.

Rijin did not tell his Pa. He told his Ma first.

"Ma, I want to go to Sizhou. I need those two strings, and travelling money besides. I've saved some myself; it's not enough."

Mother Kang heard him out and neither scolded him nor tried to talk him out of it. She sat at the mouth of the stove and fed in wood twice, and when the fire was high she got up, went into the inner room, and felt out from the bottom of a chest a silver hairpin. Her mother had given it to her the year she married. In those ten-odd years in the south of the city, when they were too poor to lift the lid of the pot, she had never pawned it. She pushed the pin into Rijin's hand.

"Take it and pawn it. That'll be enough money."

Kang Da learned of it that evening. He came back from carting in the West Market, came in, and saw Rijin packing a bundle.

"Where are you going?"

Rijin didn't say. Mother Kang said it for him.

Kang Da stood in the doorway, the whip still in his hand. "Shave your head?"

"Shave my head."

"Once your head's shaved, what are you?"

"I'm still me," said Rijin. "A certificate's a certificate, and a man's a man. With my head shaved I'll still believe in Aluohe just the same; only the government's register will say I'm a monk, so the labour service won't fall on me, and Pa's back won't have to go and haul stone in the palace again."

"You wear their clothes and live in their temple—who will you believe in then?" Kang Da's voice rose. "Why did our family come back from the south of the city? What did your Ma come back for?"

The whip in his hand went up and did not come down. Mother Kang stepped between the two of them.

"Beat him. Go on, beat him," she said. "I've lost one son already. I can't let this one go and haul stone in the palace again. If he's hauled to death, it'll still be me that lays him out."

Kang Da's whip came down and struck the door frame, and a layer of dust fell from it. He turned and went out of the door, and that night he did not come back.

Early the next morning, as those at the morning stood in the courtyard, Jingfu saw Kang Da standing in the last row by the door, his eyes red, wheat straw on the hem of his robe.

When the morning broke up, Kang Da did not leave. He waited in the courtyard until everyone had gone, and only then came up to Jingfu.

"Director, last night when I went out, the ward gate was already shut and I'd nowhere to go, so I went under the shed of the cart yard at the south end of the ward and sat on my own cart all night. The beast was chewing hay beside me, chewing all night, and I listened all night," he said. "I thought of that child in the sixth year of Zhenyuan. That year I sat on a cart too, coming back from outside the Kaiyuan Gate, with the cart empty. On that stake Elder Narsai wrote the characters. I can't read, and I asked the Elder what he'd written, and the Elder said: The Kang child, three years."

His two hands hung down, clenched into fists, and opened again.

"All my life I've never asked anything of the monastery. Rijin is the one shoot I got when I was past forty. Director, go and talk to him."

When he had said it, he knelt down. Jingfu went to pull him up and could not.

"Say you'll do it, and I'll get up."

"I'll go."

Jingfu sat more than an hour at the Kangs' that day. Kang Da had gone out with his cart and was not at home. Rijin sat on the edge of the bed; Mother Kang sat at the mouth of the stove with her back to them, stirring the ashes in the stove with a pair of tongs.

Jingfu spoke first of the south of the city. "Those ten-odd years your Ma was in the south of the city, in winter the wind came through the straw shed and the whole family crowded under one quilt. Why did your Ma come back, why did your Pa come back, and having come back, why do they go to the monastery before light to stand?"

"I know. Ma's told me."

Jingfu spoke of Aluohe too, of the One who all his life never dodged anything—when they came to seize him he let them seize him; when they came to nail him he let them nail him. Rijin listened with his head down and did not answer back.

"Rijin, being shaved and taking the precepts isn't just hanging a name somewhere and being done," said Jingfu. "At Sizhou the monks on the platform will make you kneel, make you bow, make you recite their scriptures. When you come back with the certificate, you won't be on Chang'an's monk register; you'll have to live in a Buddhist monastery. Once you're living there, every morning you'll have to go up to the hall, bow to the Buddha's image, eat their vegetarian meals, keep their precepts. A year or two—will you still remember who you are?"

Rijin raised his head.

"Director, I'll remember," he said. "I've stood at the back since I was small. At the morning I stand at the back, at the offering once in seven I stand at the back, and I doze. You've seen it, and I know it myself. I can't recite the scripture well; I don't know a single Syriac letter; I'm just a carter. But I remember my Ma dragging me out from under the quilt every day before light and dragging me to the monastery to stand. In winter it was cold, and my Ma tucked me under her robe, and from under her robe I looked out and saw an old man standing in the middle of the courtyard. That was the Master. The Master stood, so we stood. That I won't forget. Shaved, I won't forget."

He stopped, looked at Jingfu, and said, "Isn't there still a place empty at the monastery?"

Jingfu had not expected him to ask that.

"I've heard," said Rijin. "Three years ago Brother Guangzheng and the others, ten of them, left the monastery, and the register reported twenty. The quota's twenty-one; one is empty, and Uncle Lingbao wrote in the accounts kept for one who is to come. Since then, when one of the old ones died, one of those who went out came back—the elder Shi brother came back, the younger Shi brother came back—and that one has stayed empty all along. Director, I'm the one who is to come. I won't go to Sizhou; I'll enter the monastery. Add my name to the register, and I'm a monk of the Daqin Monastery. Monks of the Daqin Monastery don't do labour service either. I'd still be in the Yining Ward, still in Aluohe's monastery, and Pa wouldn't have to cry."

The sound of stirring ashes at the mouth of the stove stopped. Mother Kang turned round and looked at Jingfu, the tongs still in her hand.

Jingfu gave no answer that day. He went back to the monastery, into the little room. Lingbao was there going over the accounts. Jingfu told him what had happened, and Lingbao closed the ledger.

"First say what you think."

"I want to agree," said Jingfu. "That place has been empty three years. Guangzheng has been gone more than a year with no word at all; what year someone will come from the Patriarchate, whether anyone will come at all, no one knows. Rijin is someone the ward watched grow up. If he goes to Sizhou, this one is lost; if he comes into the monastery, at least the man is still here." He paused. "And yet I don't want to agree. I remember the day Guangzheng and the ten moved out, bundles on their backs, going out of the gate one by one. The place they gave up was kept for someone who can lay on hands, not for someone dodging labour service. I thought about it all the way back, and when I got to this, I couldn't think any further."

"Then don't think about the place first. Think about Rijin," said Lingbao. "To enter the monastery as a monk is to take the vow as a religious. A monk on the register, the Tang code doesn't allow to marry; those who live in the monastery have all taken the vow too—sleep in the monastery at night, don't marry, get up before light for the morning, stand at the front at the offering once in seven, all their lives like that. Is Rijin willing?"

"I didn't ask."

"Go and ask him. If he's willing, I'll give up the place too; who the one to come is, Aluohe knows and I don't. If he isn't willing—what he wants is only that certificate—then it isn't a question of the place at all."

The next day Jingfu went to the Kangs' again and asked. Rijin kept his head down and pinched his fingers, pinched those of his left hand and then those of his right.

"I've never thought about not marrying," he said. "I meant after the New Year to go to the Mi family in the West Market and ask for a match. The Mi family's third daughter—I've had my eye on her two years. Old Mi believes too; at the offering once in seven he stands beside my Pa."

Jingfu came back and told Lingbao. Lingbao said, "Then that's that." He said nothing else, and after noon went to the Kangs' himself.

That day Mother Kang was drying jujubes in the yard. The jujubes had been knocked down in the autumn and dried all winter until they were wrinkled; she was crouched under the jujube tree turning them when she heard the gate and looked up and saw Lingbao.

Lingbao was fifty-seven, a few years younger than she, and already dragging his feet a little when he walked. He was a priest, and when the people of the ward saw him they called him Your Reverence. He seldom came to the houses of the ward, and when he came, it was mostly because someone was dying. Mother Kang stood, wiped her hands on her apron, and was about to ask him in. Lingbao would not come in, but sat down under the jujube tree on the low stool she had been using to turn the fruit, and she had to crouch.

"Mother Kang, the place at the monastery can't be given to Rijin."

Mother Kang had a jujube pinched in her fingers and did not put it in the basket.

"What Rijin wants is to be free of labour service. The place can free him from labour service; it can't free him from anything else," said Lingbao. "Once in the monastery he has to keep the monastery's rule, take the vow, and never marry all his life. Rijin can't keep it; he's said himself he can't. Bringing him in would harm him, and harm the monastery too. This is my saying. Jingfu wanted to agree; it was I who didn't let him."

"Your Reverence," said Mother Kang, "in the sixth year of Zhenyuan I took your monastery's bread home and soaked it in well water for my child to drink, and the child died. Your monastery said the bread shouldn't be taken out of the hall door, it was my fault, and I owned it. I went to the south of the city ten-odd years, came back, and since I came back I've stood every day, eighteen years of standing, and I've never asked the monastery for one thing. Now I ask the monastery for one place. The place is empty, empty three years, and you keep it for someone who may come some year or may not, and won't give it to my son who's stood eighteen years."

"Yes," said Lingbao.

He did not argue. "You're right. The place is empty, and I won't give it."

"Then go."

Lingbao stood, dragged his feet to the gate, turned back, and said, "If Rijin goes, it's cold on the road. Tell him to take an extra lined jacket."

Mother Kang made no answer, crouched down, and went on turning her jujubes.

Jingfu learned only that evening that Lingbao had gone, and came to find him in the little room. "Uncle Lingbao, it was plainly the two of us who decided it together."

"It's enough for Mother Kang to hate one person," said Lingbao. "Let her hate two, and after this she won't even come in at the monastery gate. You're the Director. From now on at the offering once in seven, the one who has to greet her at the gate is you."

Rijin left on the twenty-third of the twelfth month with that group of Jiang-Huai merchants, riding an empty grain barge bound for Bianzhou. Twenty-odd people were crowded on the barge, most of them going to Sizhou to be shaved. On the morning he left his Ma saw him to the West Market, with an extra lined jacket stuffed in his bundle. His Pa did not go, but sat at home.

His uncle by marriage, the carpenter of the ward who whittled crosses to sell—baptized at the monastery in the fourth year of Zhenyuan, and on the day of his baptism weeping louder than anyone—had dim eyes now too, and was still whittling crosses. At the side of the barge he pressed a small wooden cross on Rijin, of jujube wood, two inches or so long, with the character fu, blessing, carved on the back, the same as those he sold on his table.

"Take it on the road."

Rijin took it and put it in his breast.

When the first month was over, early in the second, more Jiang-Huai merchants came to the West Market and said the platform at Sizhou had been shut down. Commissioner Li of Zhexi had submitted a report: since last winter Sizhou had hung up notices inviting people to be tonsured, and among the common people of Jiang-Huai, where a household had three adult men it always made one shave his head, to evade the royal labour service. He had examined at the Suanshan ferry those crossing the river for Sizhou, a hundred-odd a day, and on questioning, only fourteen were novices of long standing; the rest were common people of Suzhou and Changzhou. He said too that at the platform in Sizhou, everyone who went paid two strings of cash, was given a certificate and went straight back, with no other rite; if it were not forbidden, by the time of the Son of Heaven's birthday the lands south of Jiang-Huai would lose six hundred thousand able-bodied men. The report went up, and that same day an edict came down ordering Xuzhou to shut the platform.

When Mother Kang heard, she did not sleep for three days. Rijin had left at the end of the twelfth month; reckoning the days, he would reach Sizhou in the first month. When the platform was shut, the merchants couldn't say exactly—some said the end of the first month, some the beginning of the second. Whether Rijin had made it in time, no one knew.

That half month Kang Da came to the morning every day as always, standing in the last row; when it broke up he went to drive his cart in the West Market as always; at night he came back and sat at the door, and when Mother Kang asked whether he would eat, he said he would, picked up his bowl, ate two mouthfuls, and put it down. At the offering once in seven Mother Kang still came and stood on the women's side. Jingfu greeted her at the gate and called her name, and she answered and went in. Lingbao broke the bread at the table. She went forward in the line, reached the table, held out her hand and took the bread, and did not look up at him.

Rijin came back in the third month.

That day the morning had just broken up, and Jingfu, standing at the monastery gate, saw someone coming along the ward street in a grey cloth robe and an old turban cap, and showing beneath the cap a ring of blue—shaved scalp, with the first stubble just grown in. He came to the monastery gate, saw Jingfu, stopped, took off the cap, and put it on again.

"Director, I made it in time," he said. "I got to Sizhou on the twenty-eighth of the first month, and the platform was still open. I paid my two strings and knelt on the platform half an hour, and one monk scraped my head with a razor and another monk recited a few lines—I didn't understand a word—and they gave me a certificate. It has my name on it, and says I'm a monk of such-and-such monastery of Sizhou. The dharma name, I can't remember; the certificate's in my breast. I came out with it, and on the third day I heard the platform was going to be shut. Of the twenty-odd on the barge with me, six didn't make it in time, and cried all night outside the city of Sizhou."

"Where will you live from now on?"

"The certificate's for a monastery in Sizhou; the monasteries of Chang'an won't take me. I've found a little hermitage in the south of the city, just west of the Qujiang Pool, not far from the straw sheds where Baaluo's people lived in their day. There's an old monk in the hermitage, past seventy, hard of hearing. I carry water for him, split wood, sweep the yard, and he gives me a bed. The county came once to ask about labour service. I took out the certificate, the clerk looked at it, and he left." He lowered his head. "In the hermitage I have to go up to the hall every morning too, and bow to the Buddha. The old monk recites scripture and I move my mouth along with him; what it is, I don't know. What I recite in my heart is Aluohe."

"Have you been home?"

"No. First I've come to stand a while at the monastery gate. Stand a while, then go home."

He stood a while, took the jujube-wood cross from his breast, held it gripped in his palm, and, holding it, went off toward the south end of the ward. He did not come in at the monastery gate.

The next day, while it was still dark, those at the morning came one after another, came in at the gate, and stood in the courtyard. Jingfu stood in the middle of the courtyard, and when the sky greyed looked back and counted. When he came to the last row, Kang Da was standing by the door, leaning on the door frame.

Outside the gate stood one more person, standing beside that lamp, in a turban cap, hands tucked into his sleeves, head bowed, not coming in, and not leaving.

Kang Da had his back to the gate and could not see him; he could not see Kang Da either. Between the two of them lay the threshold.

When Jingfu had finished counting it was thirty-four. The one outside the gate he did not count in.
