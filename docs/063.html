Volume Ten　Morning Praises

Chapter 63　Sizhou

On the day Guangzheng left, morning devotion numbered thirty-seven; by the twelfth month of the fourth year of Changqing, thirty-four. Of the three fewer, one was Old Cao, who in autumn, splitting firewood, sat down on the woodpile with the axe still in his hand and was gone; his name came off the monk register, and the Shi family's Second came back with his bundle on his back and filled his place; when Lingbao reported to the inspectorate, there were still twenty on the register, and the one kept for one who is to come was still empty. One was Granny Wang, an old believer at the east end of the ward, who moved out of the city to her son's. And one was Rijin of the Kang family; he was still in Chang'an, alive and well, but from the tenth month he stopped coming.

The Kangs were old believers of the ward, Sogdians, who had lived for generations at the south end of Yining Ward, in the stretch by the ward wall: three low rooms, and a jujube tree in the yard. The Kang man, Kang Da, drove a cart for hire in the West Market; he was fifty-nine, honest, few of words, and at the offering once in seven stood on the men's side, in the place nearest the wall. His wife the ward called Kang Daniang, Old Mother Kang. In the sixth year of Zhenyuan, when her three-year-old had the flux, she carried a piece of offering bread home from every offering once in seven, soaked it in well water and gave it the child to drink; the child did not live; the old people of the ward all remembered it; Elder Narsai had set up the child's stake outside the Kaiyuan Gate. Afterwards she went with Baaluo to the south of the city and lived a dozen-odd years in the straw sheds west of the Qujiang Pool; in the winter of the first year of Yuanhe, when Jingjing went to the south of the city, she came back with An Yan and the seven households; she was forty-two the year she came back, and the next year she bore Rijin. She was sixty, and this was her only son. Kang Da had a brother-in-law, the carpenter of the ward who whittled crosses to sell; he had been baptized at the monastery in the fourth year of Zhenyuan, and wept louder than anyone the day he was baptized; now he too was old, his eyes dim; he still whittled crosses, and still carved the character fu, blessing, on the back.

Rijin was eighteen that year and drove the cart with his Pa in the West Market; strong, quick, good in both Tang and Sogdian; he came to the offering once in seven and to morning devotion too, but when he came he stood at the back and dozed; Jingfu had seen it several times. Jingfu was eleven years older; when Rijin was five or six, Jingfu, a dozen-odd, carried rice at the gruel shed in the ward street, and Rijin followed behind insisting on carrying one end, both hands hugging a corner of the rice sack; he carried it a few steps, let go to chase the ward's dog, and Jingfu dragged the rice back alone; he looked back, and Rijin was squatting by the foot of the wall, laughing at Jingfu along with the dog.

That winter, the Son of Heaven wanted to build a hall. The new Son of Heaven had been on the throne nearly a year; he loved hunting and polo; this year the palace built this hall, next year that court; Chang'an county levied labourers by household, levy after levy; Yining Ward's turn came twice, and the Kangs were caught both times; Kang Da went once, hauled stone in the palace twenty days, and came back unable to straighten his back; the second time, Rijin went for him, another twenty days. In the twelfth month the ward headman knocked at the gate again and said there would be one more in spring, repairing the dikes south of the city; the Kangs were on the roll again; Kang Da's back had not mended; Rijin was eighteen, a youth of middle grade, and the county said middle-grade youths could be levied too.

When the ward headman had gone, Old Mother Kang came to the monastery to find Jingfu, and early the next day Jingfu went to the Chang'an county office. He waited under the office colonnade till past noon, and saw the clerk in charge of levies; he said the Kang man was fifty-nine, his back injury not yet mended; the family had only one son, just eighteen; could this time be excused, or could the monastery send two men in the Kangs' place—the monastery had young men who could carry earth on the dikes for them. The clerk leafed through the roll and said: labour is levied by household; the household is the Kangs' household, and the men must be the Kangs' men; the monastery's people can't stand in; the monastery's people are monks, monks owe no service; monks carrying earth on the dikes breaks the rules, and if a censor heard he'd say the county was using monks as labourers. Jingfu said: then the monastery will pay to hire two men in their place. The clerk said: hiring men in place of service has to be done by the Kangs themselves; if the money comes from the monastery, that's the monastery paying service for a registered household—another thing no one could explain; he advised the Great Worthy not to meddle; it's only twenty days in spring; twenty days of hauling and they're home; nobody dies of it. Jingfu said: the Kangs have lost a child already. The clerk looked up at him once, said: whose family in this city hasn't lost a child? and closed the roll.

Jingfu came back and told Old Mother Kang; she said she understood; the monastery couldn't manage the government's affairs; she didn't blame the monastery; she had only come to ask. As she left she stood a moment before the lamp at the gate; Jingfu had blown it out that morning before he went out, and the lamp-dish was still warm.

It was in those days that a band of merchants from the Huai and the Yangzi came to the West Market, saying an ordination platform had been opened over at Sizhou. They said Minister Wang of Xuzhou, military governor of Xu and Si, had opened a platform at Sizhou to tonsure people: each paid two strings of cash and was given a certificate on the spot, and with the certificate in hand was a monk, named and registered; monks owed no corvée and paid no Two Taxes, and the family's property could be lodged under the monk's name too. The merchants said that over in the Huai and Yangzi country, a family with three sons always sent one to be shaved; at the Suanshan crossing at Runzhou over a hundred people a day crossed the river, all going north to Sizhou to be shaved, and the boatmen had more trade than they could take. The people of the West Market who heard it—some told it as a joke, some were moved; a few young carters and porters gathered at night to reckon the distance: from Chang'an to Sizhou, east along the Bian canal, seventeen or eighteen hundred li; ride back on an empty grain barge, and it went fast, twenty-odd days; and Rijin was among them.

Old Mother Kang knew first. Rijin did not tell his Pa; he told his Ma first: he wanted to go to Sizhou, he needed the two strings of cash and the journey money besides; he'd saved some himself, not enough. Old Mother Kang heard him out, did not scold him and did not try to dissuade him; she sat at the mouth of the stove, put wood on twice, and when the fire was high she rose, went into the inner room, and felt out from the bottom of the chest a silver hairpin her mother had given her the year she married; in those dozen-odd years in the south of the city, when they were so poor the pot lid would not come off, she had never pawned it. She pressed the pin into Rijin's hand and said: take it; pawn it; then the money's enough.

Kang Da knew that evening. He came back with the cart from the West Market, came in and saw Rijin packing a bundle, asked where he was going; Rijin would not say; Old Mother Kang said it. Kang Da stood in the doorway, the whip still in his hand, and asked one thing: shaving your head? Rijin said: shaving my head. Kang Da asked: head shaved—what are you then? Rijin said he'd still be himself; the certificate's the certificate and the man's the man; head shaved, he'd still believe in Aluohe; only on the government's books it would say he was a monk, so the corvée couldn't fall on him, and Pa wouldn't have to go to the palace to haul stone again. Kang Da said: you wear their clothes, live in their monastery—then whom do you believe in? He said: why did our family come back from the south of the city? What did your Ma come back for? As he spoke the whip in his hand rose, and did not come down; Old Mother Kang stood between the two of them and said: you hit him—go on, hit him; I've lost one son already; I can't let this one go haul stone in the palace again; if he's hauled to death, it's me that collects the body. Kang Da's whip came down and lashed the doorframe, and a layer of dust fell from the doorframe. He turned and went out of the gate, and that night did not come back.

The next morning, with the people at morning devotion standing in the courtyard, Jingfu saw him standing in the last row, by the gate, his eyes red, wheat straw stuck to the hem of his robe. When morning devotion broke up, Kang Da did not leave, but waited in the courtyard for Jingfu, waited till everyone had gone, and told him. He said last night he went out of the gate; the ward gate was already shut; he had nowhere to go, so he went under the shed of the cart yard at the south end of the ward and sat on his own cart, sat all night; the beast beside him chewed hay, chewed all night, and he listened all night. He said he remembered that child of the sixth year of Zhenyuan; that year too he sat on the cart coming back from outside the Kaiyuan Gate, the cart empty; the characters on the stake were written by Elder Narsai; he couldn't read, and asked the elder what was written, and the elder said: little child of the Kang family, three years old. He said in all his life he had never asked the monastery for anything; Rijin was the one shoot he got at forty; Leader, go and talk him round. When he had said it he knelt down; Jingfu went to pull him up and could not; he knelt and said: when you agree, he'll get up. Jingfu said he would go.

Jingfu went. That day he sat over an hour in the Kangs' house; Kang Da had gone out with the cart and was not home; Rijin sat opposite him on the edge of the bed; Old Mother Kang sat at the mouth of the stove with her back to them, raking the ashes in the stove with tongs. Jingfu spoke first of the south of the city: your Ma in those dozen-odd years in the south of the city, the wind coming through the straw shed in winter, the family crowded under one quilt—why did your Ma come back, why did your Pa come back, and having come back, why do they go to the monastery before light to stand? Rijin said he knew; his Ma had told him. Jingfu spoke of Aluohe, of that One who all his life dodged nothing: when men would take him he let them take him; when men would nail him he let them nail him; Rijin listened with his head down and did not answer back. Jingfu said further: shaving the head and taking the precepts isn't just hanging a name somewhere and done; at Sizhou the monks on the platform will have you kneel, have you bow, have you chant their scriptures; you'll come back with the certificate, and you won't be on Chang'an's monk register; you'll have to live in a Buddhist monastery; once in, every morning you'll go to the hall, bow to the Buddha's images, eat their vegetarian meals, keep their precepts; a year, two years—will you still remember who you are? Here Rijin looked up and said: Leader, he would. He said that from a child he had stood at the back—at morning devotion at the back, at the offering once in seven at the back; he knew he dozed; the leader had seen him; he knew it himself; he wasn't the monastery's kind of man; he couldn't chant the scriptures, didn't know a single Syriac letter; he was just a carter; but he remembered; he remembered his Ma every day before light hauling him out of the quilt and hauling him to the monastery to stand; in winter it was cold, and his Ma tucked him under her own robe, and from under the robe he looked out and saw an old man standing in the middle of the courtyard; the old man was Master; Master stood, so they stood. He said this he could never forget; head shaved, he still couldn't forget it. When he had said it, he looked at Jingfu and asked one thing. He said: isn't the monastery still keeping one place empty?

Jingfu had not thought he would ask this. Rijin said he'd heard: three years ago Guangzheng and the other ten left the monastery; the register reported twenty; the quota was twenty-one; one was empty; Uncle Lingbao wrote in the accounts kept for one who is to come; later, when an old one died, one of those who went out came back—the Shi eldest came back, the Shi Second came back—and that one stayed empty all along. He said: he is the one to come; he won't go to Sizhou; he'll enter the monastery; add his one name to the register and he's a monk of the Daqin Monastery, and monks of the Daqin Monastery owe no corvée either; he'd still be in Yining Ward, still in Aluohe's monastery, and Pa needn't cry. When he had said it, the sound of raking at the stove-mouth stopped; Old Mother Kang turned round and looked at Jingfu, the tongs still in her hand.

Jingfu gave no answer that day. He went back to the monastery and into the small room, where Lingbao was checking accounts, and told him; Lingbao closed the account book and asked: first tell me what you think. Jingfu said he wanted to agree. He said that place had been empty three years; Guangzheng had been gone over a year with not a scrap of news; what year the Patriarchate's man would come, whether he'd come at all, no one knew; Rijin was a child the ward had watched grow up; if he went to Sizhou, this one would be lost; in the monastery, at least he'd still be here. He said he wanted to agree, and yet he didn't; he remembered the day Guangzheng and the ten moved out, bundles on their backs, going out of the gate one by one; the place they gave up was kept for someone who could lay on hands, not for someone dodging corvée. He said he had thought about it all the way back, and when he got this far he couldn't think further.

Lingbao said: then don't think of the place for now; think of Rijin. He said: entering the monastery as a monk means taking vows as a brother; a monk on the register may not marry under the Tang code, and those who live in the monastery have all taken vows—sleep in the monastery at night, don't marry, rise before light for morning devotion, stand at the front at the offering once in seven, all one's life; would Rijin be willing? Jingfu said he hadn't asked. Lingbao said: go and ask him. He said if he was willing, he could part with the place; who the one to come is, Aluohe knows, he doesn't; if he wasn't willing, what he wanted was only that certificate, and then it wasn't a matter of the place.

The next day Jingfu went to the Kangs' again and asked. Rijin bowed his head and pinched his own fingers, pinched the left hand and then the right, and said he had never thought of not marrying. He said he meant after the new year to go and propose to the Mi family in the West Market—the Mis' Third Daughter, he'd had his eye on her two years; her Pa was a believer too, and stood beside his Pa at the offering once in seven. Jingfu came back and told Lingbao that Rijin was not willing. Lingbao said: that's that; he said nothing else; past noon, he went to the Kangs' himself.

That day Old Mother Kang was drying jujubes in the yard—jujubes knocked down in autumn and dried all winter, dried till they wrinkled; she squatted under the jujube tree turning them, heard the gate, looked up and saw Lingbao. Lingbao was fifty-seven, a few years younger than she, and already dragged his feet a little when he walked; he was a priest; the people of the ward called him Great Worthy when they saw him; he did not often come to the homes of the ward, and when he came, mostly someone was about to die. Old Mother Kang stood up, wiped her hands on her apron, and invited him into the house; Lingbao would not go in, but sat under the jujube tree, on the low stool she had been turning jujubes from, so she had to squat. Lingbao said: the monastery's place can't be given to Rijin. He said: what Rijin wants is to be excused the corvée; that place can excuse the corvée, but nothing else; once in the monastery he must keep the monastery's rule, take vows, never marry all his life; Rijin can't keep that—he said himself he can't; bringing him in would harm him, and harm the monastery too. He said: these words are his; Jingfu wanted to agree; it was he who would not let him. Old Mother Kang squatted there with a jujube pinched in her fingers, pinching it, not putting it in the basket. She said: Great Worthy, in the sixth year of Zhenyuan she carried your monastery's bread home, soaked it in well water for her child to drink, and the child died; your monastery said the bread shouldn't be carried out of the hall door; it was her fault, and she owned it; she went to the south of the city a dozen-odd years, came back, and having come back stood every day, stood eighteen years, and never asked the monastery for one thing. Now she asks the monastery for one place, a place that's empty, empty three years, and you keep it for someone who'll come who knows what year, if he comes at all, and won't give it to her son who's stood eighteen years. Lingbao said: yes. He did not argue; he said yes, she was right; the place is empty, and he won't give it. Old Mother Kang said: then go. Lingbao stood up, dragged his feet to the gate, looked back and said: if Rijin goes, it's cold on the road; have him take an extra lined coat. Old Mother Kang did not answer him; she squatted down and went on turning her jujubes.

Jingfu learned only that evening that Lingbao had gone, and went to find him, and said: Uncle Lingbao, we decided it together, the two of us. Lingbao said: it's enough for Old Mother Kang to hate one man; if she hates two, from now on she won't even come in at the monastery gate; you're the leader; at the offering once in seven it's you who'll greet her at the gate.

Rijin left on the twenty-third of the twelfth month, with that band of merchants, on an empty grain barge bound for Bianzhou; twenty-odd people crowded the barge, most of them going to Sizhou to be shaved. The morning he left, his Ma saw him to the West Market, and stuffed an extra lined coat into his bundle; his Pa did not go, and sat at home. His uncle, the carpenter who whittled crosses, pressed on him at the boat's side a little wooden cross of jujube wood, two inches or so long, with fu carved on the back like the ones he sold on his stall, and said: take it on the road; Rijin took it and tucked it into his robe.

When the first month was over, at the beginning of the second, more merchants from the Huai and the Yangzi came to the West Market and said the platform at Sizhou had been stopped. They said Surveillance Commissioner Li of Zhexi had sent up a memorial saying that since last winter Sizhou had hung up notices calling people to be tonsured; of the common people of the Huai and Yangzi, wherever a household had three adult males one was always made to shave, to evade the king's service; he had counted at the Suanshan crossing those crossing the river for Sizhou, over a hundred a day, and on questioning only fourteen were novices of the old sort; the rest were common people of Suzhou and Changzhou without even their own prefecture's papers; he said too that at the platform set up at Sizhou everyone who came paid two strings and was given a certificate and sent straight back, with no other rite; if it were not forbidden, by the time of the Son of Heaven's birthday the lands south of the Huai and the Yangzi would have lost six hundred thousand able-bodied men. The memorial went up, and that same day an edict came down ordering Xuzhou to stop the platform.

When Old Mother Kang heard, she did not sleep for three days. Rijin had left at the end of the twelfth month; reckoning the days, he would reach Sizhou in the first month; when the platform was stopped the merchants could not say either—some said the end of the first month, some the beginning of the second; whether Rijin had made it in time, no one knew. That half month, Kang Da still came to morning devotion every morning, standing in the last row; when morning devotion broke up he still went to the West Market to drive the cart; at night he came back and sat at the door; Old Mother Kang asked him whether he'd eat, and he said he would, took up the bowl, ate two mouthfuls, and put it down. At the offering once in seven Old Mother Kang still came, and stood on the women's side; Jingfu greeted her at the gate and called her name, she answered once and went in; Lingbao broke the bread at the table; she went forward in the line, reached the table, put out her hand and took the bread, and did not look up at him.

Rijin came back in the third month. That day morning devotion had just broken up; Jingfu was standing at the monastery gate and saw a man coming along the ward street in a grey cloth robe, an old futou cap on his head, and below the cap showed a ring of blue—scalp that had been shaved, just grown a layer of stubble. He came to the monastery gate, saw Jingfu, stopped, took off the cap, and put it on again. He said he'd made it: he reached Sizhou on the twenty-eighth of the first month, and the platform was still open; he paid his two strings, knelt half a watch on the platform; one monk scraped his head with a razor, another recited a few lines, not one of which he understood, and gave him a certificate; on the certificate was written his name, written that he was a monk of such-and-such monastery at Sizhou; what his religious name was he couldn't remember, he said; the certificate was in his robe. He said he came out with the certificate, and on the third day heard the platform was to be stopped; of the twenty-odd who went on the same boat, six didn't make it in time, and cried all night outside the walls of Sizhou.

Jingfu asked where he'd live from now on. Rijin said the certificate named a monastery at Sizhou; the monasteries of Chang'an wouldn't take him; he'd found a little hermitage in the south of the city, just west of the Qujiang Pool, not far from that patch of straw sheds where Baaluo and the others had lived long ago; in the hermitage there was an old monk past seventy, hard of hearing; he drew water for the old monk, split firewood, swept the yard, and the old monk gave him a bed; about the corvée, the county had come to ask once; he took out the certificate, the clerk looked at it, and left. He said in the hermitage every morning he had to go to the hall too and bow to the Buddha; the old monk chanted the scriptures and he moved his mouth along; what they were he didn't know; in his heart he was saying Aluohe. Jingfu asked whether he'd been home; Rijin said no; he said he'd come to the monastery gate first to stand a while, and when he'd stood a while, he'd go home. He stood a while, took the jujube-wood cross out of his robe, gripped it in his palm, and, gripping it, went off toward the south end of the ward without coming in at the monastery gate.

The next day, while it was still dark, those at morning devotion arrived one after another, came in at the gate and stood in the courtyard; Jingfu stood in the middle of the courtyard, and when the sky greyed he looked back and counted, and when he came to the last row, Kang Da was standing by the gate, leaning on the doorframe. Outside the gate stood one more person, standing beside that lamp; the lamp was still burning, its flame shrunk to the size of a bean; the man wore a futou cap, his hands folded in his sleeves; he stood with his head bowed, did not come in, and did not go. Kang Da had his back to the gate and could not see him, and he could not see Kang Da; between the two of them was a threshold. When Jingfu had finished counting, it was thirty-four. The one outside the gate he did not count in.
