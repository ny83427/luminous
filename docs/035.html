Volume Six　The Shuofang Army

Chapter 35　Pugu Huai'en

In the third month of the second year of Guangde, Lingzhou heard its first thunder.

There was no rain that night, only wind. The wind came across from the Helan Mountains and set the window paper of the governor's residence going puh, puh. At first the thunder rolled very far off; it rolled a few times, and suddenly it was overhead, a crack as if someone had split the sky open.

Huai'en sat in the hall and did not sleep.

Spread before him was a document. It had come from Chang'an, and its cover bore a vermilion seal. He read it once, and read it again.

The document said that the court, mindful of his former merits, would not pursue what had passed; let him only come to court, and all could be settled.

An attendant brought in a basin of hot water to wash his feet. The attendant was a boy of fifteen or sixteen, taken into the residence only the year before, still clumsy with his hands and feet. As he set down the basin, thunder sounded again outside; his hand shook, the rim of the basin knocked against the floor, and hot water splashed out onto Huai'en's boots.

Huai'en looked down at his boots.

He stood up, took his horsewhip down from the wall, and brought it down.

The boy did not dodge. He knelt on the floor, both hands over his head, and made no sound.

Huai'en whipped him stroke after stroke. The whip came down on his back, pa, on his shoulders, pa, on his arms, pa. Outside the thunder came stroke after stroke too, so that inside and out you could not tell which was thunder and which was the whip.

A few guards stood below the hall, and not one came forward.

In the end the lash broke. Huai'en went on beating with half the whipstock. The clothes on the boy's back had been torn to strips.

An old officer came in from outside, took one look, went up and caught Huai'en's arm from behind.

"Governor," said the old officer, "that's enough."

Huai'en turned his head to look at him. Those long narrow eyes had gone red.

The old officer did not let go. The two of them stood like that a while. Slowly Huai'en's arm went slack, and the whipstock fell from his hand.

Outside, all at once, it was bright as day.

The lightning had struck the flagpole outside the residence gate. The flagpole was an old pine trunk set up twenty years before, and on it hung the great banner of the Military Governor of Shuofang. The stroke split the pole from the top, and the banner went up with a roar. There was no rain, and it burned very fast; in the blink of an eye it was a ball of fire, spinning in mid-air.

Everyone in the residence ran outside. Some shouted to put it out, some shouted it was fire from heaven, some knelt and knocked their heads on the ground.

Huai'en went out too.

He stood at the residence gate, his head tipped back, watching the banner burn. The firelight shone on his face, made his cheekbones higher and his eyes sunk deeper. When the banner had burned away, the fire fell, fell to the ground in pieces, and slowly went dark.

Someone behind him said in a low voice: "Governor, this is an ill omen…"

Huai'en gave a laugh.

"Ill omen?" he said. "Them saying I'm rebelling—that's the ill omen."

He turned and went back into the hall. The document from Chang'an still lay spread on the table, one corner curled up by the wind. He picked it up, set it alight at the lamp, and threw it into the brazier.

The boy who had been beaten was gone the next day. Some said he had run away, some said he had died. No one went to look.

Huai'en's old mother had not come with him to Lingzhou.

It was said that on the day he left Fenzhou the old woman ran after him out of the camp gate with a knife in her hand, cursing as she ran: "Where has the realm ever done you wrong, that you should do such a thing! I'll kill you for the realm, and cut out your heart, and show it to the three armies!"

Huai'en galloped off. He did not look back.

Zhang Shao did not live out that year either. Some said Huai'en, finding him a man without loyalty, had his two legs broken and left him in a small town with no one to look after him, and so he died. There were other accounts too. When the people of Lingzhou spoke of him, none of them cared to say much.

That autumn, Huai'en led Tibetans and Uighurs, a hundred thousand horse and more, bearing down from the north, all the way to Fengtian.

Guo Ziyi was holding Fengtian.

The generals asked to fight, and Guo Ziyi would not allow it. He said: they've come far, and it's in their interest to fight quickly. If we don't come out, they can't stay many days.

The Tibetans circled outside the city for some days, carried off some cattle and sheep, burned some villages, and, just as he had said, went away.

All those days Yisi was on the walls. He watched the campfires outside the city, swathe upon swathe of them, like stars grown up out of the earth. He thought: among those campfires, one is Huai'en's.

He did not know which one.

In the ninth month of the first year of Yongtai, Huai'en came again.

This time there were more of them. Tibetans, Uighurs, Tanguts, Tuyuhun—hundreds of thousands, it was claimed—bearing south by several roads. Chang'an was in confusion again; the city gates were shut even by day, and in the wards people buried their rice in the ground.

Guo Ziyi led his troops and camped at Jingyang.

One day in the middle of the ninth month, a man came to the camp gate.

He wore a Uighur's fur robe, but spoke the speech of Shuofang. He was brought to the command tent, knelt, and said nothing else but that he wanted to see the Great Virtue Yi.

Yisi came out.

The man looked at him, looked a while, and only then said: "I'm from Military Governor Pugu's command. The Governor is ill."

Yisi said nothing.

"Ill half a month," said the man. "The flux—passing nothing but bloody water, throwing up whatever he eats. The physician with the army has seen him, and says he can't cure it. A few of us old brothers talked it over and thought of the Great Virtue Yi. In the old days in the Shuofang army, when there was dysentery in camp, it was the Great Virtue Yi who went to see the men tent by tent, and saved a good many."

He knocked his head on the ground.

"The Governor doesn't know I've come," he said. "If he knew, he'd kill me."

Yisi turned and looked at Guo Ziyi.

Guo Ziyi sat in the tent looking at the man, looked a long time.

"Go," said Guo Ziyi.

Yisi travelled four days.

The man who guided him was that same man. Two men, two horses, by the byways, hiding in gullies by day, travelling by night. Several times on the road they met Tibetan patrols, and each time got clear.

On the third night the two of them rested in a dry gully, not daring to light a fire, leaning against their horses, chewing dry flatbread.

The man chewed a long while, and suddenly said: "Great Virtue Yi, this year the Governor hardly sleeps at night."

Yisi did not take him up.

"He lies down, and after a while he wakes," said the man. "And when he wakes he asks: has anyone come from Chang'an? We say no. He lies down. After a while he wakes again, and asks again."

"Has anyone come?"

"A few times. All of them to urge him to come to court," said the man. "Every time one comes, he beats someone."

He tucked the remaining half of his flatbread back into his coat.

"But when no one comes," he said, "he still asks."

A gust of wind went over the top of the gully, and sand fell, covering both their heads and faces. The man brushed the sand off his head, leaned back against his horse, and closed his eyes.

On the evening of the fourth day they reached Mingsha.

Mingsha was a small county south of Lingzhou, by the Yellow River, and outside the town was a stretch of sand hills; when the wind blew the sand would sound, a humming, like many people chanting far off. Huai'en's main army had already gone on south; he was too sick to travel and had been left here, with only a few hundred of his own men about him.

The tents were pitched by the river.

When Yisi went into the tent, a smell rushed out at him. He had smelled that smell at Changshan, and at Xiangzhou too.

Huai'en lay on a felt.

He had wasted until there was nothing left but a frame of bones. The flesh of his face was all gone, the skin drawn tight over the bone, his cheeks sunk into two pits, his cheekbones like two stones pressing up under the skin. His lips were cracked, with black scabs in the cracks. His eyes were open, still long and narrow, but sunk so deep that when he looked at you it was as if from far back in a cave.

He saw Yisi.

He looked a while, and knew him.

"The juggler," he said.

His voice was very hoarse and very faint, but Yisi heard it clearly.

Yisi squatted down beside him.

He had a pot of water boiled. When the water was boiling he took from his own saddlebag a small packet of salt and a small packet of rice. A pinch of salt went in, a handful of rice went in, and he boiled it into a very thin rice-water, so thin you could see your face in it. When it was done he ladled out a bowl and set it on the ground to cool.

Huai'en watched him do all this.

When the rice-water was cool enough not to scald, Yisi took up the bowl, raised Huai'en's head with one hand, and brought the bowl to his lips.

"Drink a little," said Yisi. "A mouthful at a time. Drink it and throw it up, throw it up and drink again."

Huai'en did not open his mouth.

He looked at the bowl of rice-water, and at Yisi, and the corner of his mouth pulled up.

"What's this?"

"Water. Salt. Rice."

"That's all?"

"That's all."

Huai'en laughed. He laughed with great effort, laughed till his chest heaved, laughed until he began to cough and coughed up a little bloody froth that hung at the corner of his mouth.

"You Luminous people," he said, "talk of mercy. Mercy enough to keep me alive on a bowl of water?"

Yisi held the bowl and did not move.

"I, Pugu Huai'en," said Huai'en, "have fought twenty years, and cut off more heads than I can count. Forty-six of my family died in the king's service. And in the end it's a hu monk holding a bowl of salt water, feeding me."

He turned his face away.

"Take it away."

Yisi did not move.

After a while Huai'en turned his face back.

"Guo Ziyi sent you?"

"Men of your command asked me to come."

"Who?"

Yisi did not say.

Huai'en stared at him a while and grunted. "Don't say, then. I know who." He breathed hard a few times and asked again: "Is Guo Ziyi well?"

"He is."

"Still drinking that millet gruel?"

"Still drinking it."

The corner of Huai'en's mouth moved—whether in a smile, there was no telling.

"In Shuofang," he said, "I ate in the same tent with him three years. In the mornings he drank millet gruel with pickles. I found it tasteless and had them roast me a leg of mutton. He'd watch me eat and not say a word."

He closed his eyes, and opened them again.

"That's how he looks at people. Without a word."

Yisi did not take it away.

He set the bowl by Huai'en's head, right beside the felt, where a hand could reach it. Then he went and sat in a corner of the tent.

Huai'en did not look at him again.

Night fell. A lamp was lit in the tent; there was little oil, and the flame was very small. Outside the wind rose, and the sand hills began to sound again, a humming, wave after wave.

In the middle of the night Huai'en began to talk in delirium.

He spoke in the Pugu tongue, and Yisi could not understand much. He could make out only that he was calling names. He called one, and then another. Over and over, those two names.

The old officer keeping watch at the tent door heard, and bowed his head, and for a long time made no sound.

Afterwards he said quietly to Yisi: "His two sons. One he killed himself. One someone else killed."

Huai'en was still calling. As he called, his voice grew smaller, became a moan, and the moan became words again. These words were Tang, and Yisi understood.

"I won't go."

He said it once, and again.

"I won't go. If I go, I won't come back."

Near dawn, Huai'en woke.

He opened his eyes, looked up at the roof of the tent, then turned his head and saw the bowl by his head. The rice-water was still there, a thin skin formed on its surface.

He looked at it a long time.

Yisi watched him from the corner.

Huai'en put out his hand. It shook terribly, and his fingers touched the rim of the bowl. The bowl rocked, and did not tip. His hand rested on the rim of the bowl, rested there a while.

Then he pushed the bowl away.

Not far—only a foot. The bowl turned half round at the edge of the felt and stopped; a little rice-water spilled out and soaked into the sand.

Huai'en drew his hand back, laid it on his chest, and closed his eyes.

He never opened them again.

The day he died was in the ninth month; what day, Yisi could not afterwards remember.

His men did not weep. Without a word they went out, cut down willows by the river, broke up a few carts, and piled the wood into a stack. They piled it very high, higher than a man. They carried Huai'en out and laid him on the stack, dressed him in that old robe, and laid his sword beside him.

The old officer stood before the stack with a torch in his hand, stood a long time.

He looked back once at Yisi.

Yisi said nothing.

The old officer threw the torch up onto it.

The willow was green, and at first would not burn, only sent up white smoke. Then the dry wood from the carts caught, and the fire leapt up all at once, higher than the stack. The flames roared, mingling with the sound from the sand hills, so that you could not tell one from the other.

The men stood round the fire in a ring, and someone began to sing something low in the Pugu tongue, very slow, very long, no words, only a tune.

Yisi stood outside the ring.

The fire burned all day. By evening the stack had collapsed, the fire had sunk, and there was only a heap of red embers, glowing and fading in the wind. When the embers had cooled, the men picked the bones out of the ash, put them in a leather bag, and buried it in the sand by the river.

They set up no stone, and raised no mound. When they had buried it, someone led a horse back and forth over it a few times, trampling the ground flat.

That night Yisi did not leave.

He sat by the river, not far from that patch of trampled sand. The moon came out and shone on the river; the water ran very fast, rushing north.

The old officer came over and sat down beside him.

"Great Virtue Yi," said the old officer, "that teaching of yours—when a man dies, you say something over him, don't you?"

"We do."

"Then say something over him," said the old officer. "He didn't believe your teaching. But he's dead, and there's no one to say anything over him."

Yisi nodded.

He took something from inside his coat and held it in his palm. He thought a long time, and did not know what to say. He thought of Huai'en in the tent laughing at him for a juggler, of Huai'en at Xiangji Temple following his horse through the fog, of Fenzhou, of the gate passage at Lingzhou, of that bowl pushed away.

And at the last he thought of those words spoken in delirium.

I won't go. If I go, I won't come back.

Yisi bowed his head.

"Lord," he said, "have mercy on him."

He paused.

"Have mercy on his fear."
