Volume Eight　The Daqin Monastery

Chapter 47　Lü Xiuyan

On the third day after the Patriarchate's letter of leave arrived, Jingjing brought out his draft.

The same small room in the back courtyard. The same few people. The three elders Azarel, Marya and Samaan sat at the head, Babai and Sabrisho on either side, Yisi by the door. Jingjing stood in the middle holding a pile of paper.

The paper he had copied out four times over. The first draft was too messy, the second too much corrected, the third had a corner soaked by steam from the stove. This was the fourth, the characters very neatly written, ten lines to a page, eighteen characters to a line, more than seventeen hundred characters in all.

Azarel said: "Read it."

So Jingjing read it.

He read it not fast. A Tang composition was hard going for the elders to follow, so after each passage he stopped, explained the sense in Syriac, and read on.

The first passage was about the Lord.

"Behold: the Eternal, true and still, before all firsts and Himself without beginning; the Unfathomable, spirit and void, after all lasts and wondrously enduring; who holds the hidden pivot and makes and transforms all things, who gives the holy ones their wonder and is honoured by them as the Origin—who is this but our Triune Wonder, the beginningless true Lord, Alaha!"

He explained: this says that before all things there is One who has no beginning; after all things it is still that One, who has no end. Heaven and earth He made. He is three, and also one. His name is Alaha.

The elder Samaan nodded.

The second passage told of the making of heaven and earth, the making of man; told how man was led astray by the devil and lost the road, and three hundred and sixty-five false teachings sprang up together—some sank into duality over emptiness and being; some prayed and sacrificed to win good fortune. The third told of the birth of the Messiah—a virgin bore the Holy One in Daqin; a luminous star announced the blessing—and the men of Persia who saw the star and came with gifts. Told how He set up the new teaching—He hung up the luminous sun to break open the dark realm—He rowed the boat of mercy up to the palace of light.

At the words boat of mercy, Samaan's brows moved.

Jingjing saw it, did not stop, and read on.

The fourth passage told of the rules of the teaching. They strike the wood, and the sound of kindness and mercy rings out—meaning no bell, but a board. They worship toward the east—meaning they pray facing east. They keep their beards; they shave the crown. They keep no slaves. Seven times a day they offer praise; once in seven days they make the offering, to wash the heart and return to simplicity.

When Sabrisho heard they keep no slaves, he raised his head and glanced at Yisi. Yisi did not move.

Then: The true and constant Way is wondrous and hard to name; its works shine so plainly that we are forced to call it the Luminous Teaching.

Then Taizong, Alopen, Duke Fang, the edict.

Then Gaozong, the Empress Wu, Xuanzong, Suzong, Daizong, reign by reign down to the present emperor.

Then Yisi.

When Jingjing read the passage about Yisi, his voice dropped a little, and he read a little faster. Yisi sat by the door looking out at the jujube tree.

Last came the hymn, four characters to a line, thirty-odd lines. When he had read it, Jingjing put the paper down and waited.

The first to speak was Samaan.

As usual he first tucked his two hands into his sleeves, and then asked: "The boat of mercy—whose words are those?"

"The Buddhists'."

"And true and still?"

"The Buddhists say it, and the Daoists too."

"And without striving?"

"Laozi's."

"Among those three hundred and sixty-five false teachings," said Samaan, "are there Buddhists? Are there Daoists?"

Jingjing thought and said: "There are."

"Then you take their words to speak of our Lord?"

Jingjing did not answer at once.

Samaan went on: "Last year the new Son of Heaven issued an edict that the various hu should each wear their own dress and not copy the Tang. On this stele of yours, our Lord from beginning to end is wearing other people's clothes."

The room was very quiet.

The elder Marya coughed, his voice hoarse as ever: "Samaan, I've preached sixteen years. When I say Alaha, the Tang people below me don't understand. When I say the Heavenly Honoured One, they understand. And when I've said it, then little by little I tell them this Heavenly Honoured One is not that Heavenly Honoured One. If you don't first take words they know, how do you lead them to places they don't know?"

"Lead them and lead them," said Samaan, "and you'll lead them back to places they know."

Here Jingjing spoke.

"Elder Samaan," he said, "the words profound and free of striving—I didn't add them. They're the very words of Emperor Taizong's edict. A hundred and forty years ago the Son of Heaven used those words to speak of us. This suit of clothes of ours, we've been wearing it since that day."

Samaan looked at him.

"I'd like to write our own words too," said Jingjing. "But of our own words, the Tang know not one. The stele stands in Chang'an, not in Baghdad."

Samaan said nothing. After a long while he drew his hands out of his sleeves, laid them on his knees, and said: "There's one more thing."

"And the wood?" asked Samaan.

Jingjing knew what he was asking.

"The Messiah's birth, you've written. His ascension, you've written—at high noon He ascended to the Truth," said Samaan. "And in between? How did He die?"

Jingjing lowered his head and looked at the paper in his hands.

"It isn't written."

At once the little room was so quiet you could hear the wind in the courtyard. Babai looked at Jingjing, Sabrisho looked at Samaan, Azarel kept his eyes shut.

"Why isn't it written?"

Jingjing said: "If I write it, the mason can carve it and the Tang can read it. And the first thing the Tang will read is that our Lord broke the king's law and was nailed to wood by the authorities till he died."

"He was nailed till he died."

"Yes."

"Then write it."

Jingjing raised his head. He opened his mouth, and no words came.

Then Yisi spoke. He was still sitting by the door, still looking out at the jujube tree, and did not turn his head.

"Carve it at the very top," he said.

They all turned to look at him.

"At the head of the stele," said Yisi, "carve a cross. Carve it large. Whoever comes up to the stele sees that first, and then the characters below. What the words haven't said in full, it says above them."

Samaan thought a long time.

He said: "In the words, at least, there must be the word cross."

Jingjing turned the pages of the draft, came to a place, and said: "There's one line. The cross is their seal; it gathers the light of the four quarters and joins all without hindrance."

Samaan nodded. He said neither good nor bad. He tucked his hands back into his sleeves.

The draft was revised at the monastery for a month.

Azarel laid down the rule: the inscription must be shown to every teacher still living, and to a Tang official who held the faith. The teachers would look at whether the doctrine was right; the Tang official at whether the writing was sound.

Few of the teachers were left. Of the seven who had come east from Ctesiphon in those days, Yonan was gone, Narsai could no longer go up to the lectern, and of the others some were at monasteries elsewhere, and some had eyes too dim to read small characters. Those who read the draft this time were Babai and Sabrisho. Babai read most closely; he checked the dates in Alopen's book against the era names in the inscription one by one, and corrected two places. Sabrisho read it once and said only one word: "Allowed."

It was the first time Jingjing had heard Sabrisho say allowed.

The Tang official was Official Pei.

Official Pei was past sixty that year, his brow ridges as high as ever, but he had grown much thinner; he had retired the year before and spent his days at home growing flowers. He took the draft home and read it five days, and on the sixth brought it back, the paper dotted in vermilion in a dozen and more places. All the dots were about characters—this character should be such-and-such, this allusion comes from such-and-such a book, the tones of this line don't fit. Only in one place had he written a sentence in the margin.

That place was the passage about Yisi. What Official Pei wrote was:

"This passage is too short."

Jingjing read it, and did not change it.

Official Pei said one more thing. He said the stele must bear a year, month and day, and the day of raising it should be looked into by someone who knew.

The one who looked into the day was Li Su.

Li Su was a Persian born in Chang'an, thirty-six that year, an official at the Astronomical Bureau in charge of calculating the calendar. He was one of the monastery's people, came to worship every seventh day, stood at the back, and said little. When Jingjing went to find him at the Astronomical Bureau he was bent over a great table with a star chart spread on it, close-packed with dots and lines.

Jingjing told him what was wanted. When Li Su had heard it, he asked: "When would you like it?"

"At the start of spring."

Li Su pulled a calendar from under the table, turned its pages a while, then took a sheet of paper and reckoned on it with his brush. He reckoned a long time. Jingjing stood beside him waiting, watching him fill a whole page with figures, and when he had filled it, circle one.

"Next year, first month, seventh day," said Li Su. "The year being xinyou—xinyou of old was called zuo'e. The first month, in the pitch-pipes, is taicou."

"Good."

"One more thing." Li Su raised his head. "The seventh of the first month, by the Persian reckoning, is the first day of a week."

Jingjing was taken aback.

"Yaosenwen day," said Li Su. "The day of the Lord's rising."

Jingjing took the sheet and looked at it a long time. The paper was layer upon layer of figures, and he understood none of them; he understood only the few words Li Su had circled at the end.

He went back to the monastery and added one line at the end of the draft:

"Erected in the second year of Jianzhong of the Great Tang, the year being zuo'e, on the seventh day of the month of taicou, a great yaosenwen day."

The one to write the characters was recommended by Official Pei.

His name was Lü Xiuyan, fifty or so, once an administrator of Taizhou, later out of office, making his living in Chang'an by selling his calligraphy. His hand was the regular script—upright, solid, not flighty. Several epitaphs in Chang'an were in his hand.

Yisi and Jingjing went to his house in the Chongxian Ward to ask him. Lü Xiuyan's house was very small; as soon as you came in there was a great table piled with paper, and a dozen and more brushes hanging on the wall, arranged from thick to fine.

He read the inscription, read it very fast. When he had read it, he asked one thing: "What is Alaha?"

Jingjing told him. Told him at length.

Lü Xiuyan listened, nodded, shook his head, as if he had understood, and as if he had not. He said: "You believe this?"

"We do."

"I don't," said Lü Xiuyan. "I'll write it."

He named his price too. It was not low. Yisi said: very well.

Lü Xiuyan rolled up the inscription, put it in his sleeve, and saw them to the door. At the door he suddenly stopped, turned and asked Jingjing: "On this stele, if the characters I write get the meaning wrong, whose fault is it?"

Jingjing said: "Mine. The composition is mine."

Lü Xiuyan said "Mm."

"Once the characters are written on the stele," he said, "they're the stele's business."

The stone was brought into the monastery in the tenth month.

It was a blue-black stone, quarried from the northern hills; twenty-odd men pushed it on rollers from the Jinguang Gate all the way to the Yining Ward, and pushed it a whole day. Stood up, the stone was taller than two men, broader than a man's height, a foot thick. The mason first carved out the head of the stele, and on the head two hornless dragons coiled, and between the two dragons a space was left empty.

Samaan came to look once. He walked once round the stone, tipped his head back and looked at the two dragons, looked a long time.

"The dragons are someone else's too," he said.

The mason beside him did not understand, thought he was complaining that the dragons were badly carved, and said hastily that this was the rule—every stele head was like this; of a thousand steles in Chang'an, a thousand had dragons.

Samaan said no more.

That empty space was afterwards carved with a cross. Beneath the cross were clouds, and beneath the clouds a lotus. Samaan came to look once more, and when he had looked, went back, and said nothing at all.

Lü Xiuyan came in the eleventh month to write in vermilion.

To write in vermilion is to mix cinnabar with ink and write directly on the stone. When it is written, the mason carves along the red characters stroke by stroke. Write wrong, and there is no changing it.

Lü Xiuyan put up a wooden scaffold before the stone and stood on it to write. He wrote very slowly, only a hundred and some characters a day. It was cold in winter and the cinnabar froze, so he had a brazier lit under the scaffold, and wrote a while, and came down to warm his hands.

The people of the monastery came to watch him write whenever they were free. He took no notice of anyone, and said nothing, only wrote. When he came to the three characters 阿羅訶, Alaha, he paused, as if wondering whether to write them heavier or lighter. In the end he wrote them the same as every other character.

He wrote twenty-odd days. When he came to the last line, he climbed down from the scaffold, stepped back a few paces, and looked a while.

"Both sides," he said to Jingjing, "and the bottom—they're still empty."

"That's where the hu writing goes."

"I can't write hu writing."

"You needn't," said Jingjing. "My pa will write it."

Yisi wrote the Syriac in the twelfth month.

He did not stand on a scaffold. He had the mason grind smooth the face of the stone along the bottom section first, brought a low stool himself, and sat at the foot of the stele to write. His letters were still big and clumsy, but written on stone, the bigness and clumsiness became a virtue.

First he wrote the date: "In the year one thousand and ninety-two of the Greeks."

Then he wrote: "In the days of the Father of Fathers, Mar Hnanisho, the Catholicos Patriarch."

He wrote Jingjing's name, wrote Azarel, Marya, Samaan, wrote Babai and Sabrisho, wrote the monastery's priests, deacons, monks, name after name, down onto both sides of the stele, more than seventy of them.

When he came to Sargis, he paused. Sargis had been dead two years by then. He wrote him in all the same.

Last, he wrote himself.

This was the last line of writing at the bottom of the stele, and he thought several days before he settled how to write it. What he wrote was:

"Yazdbozid, priest and chorepiscopus of Kumdan, the royal city, son of the late Milis, priest, of Balkh, a city of Tokharistan, set up this tablet of stone."

When he wrote the late Milis, his hand stopped.

The mason beside him waited, waited a long time, thought he was tired, and held out a bowl of hot water. Yisi did not take it. He sat on the low stool, the brush in his hand, looking at his father's name on the face of the stone.

It was the first time since he came to the East that he had written that name.

He dipped the brush in cinnabar again, and wrote on: Balkh.
