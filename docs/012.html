Volume Three　The Pamir Road

Chapter 12　Tang Characters

They crossed the Pamirs in the first month.

In the passes the snow came up to the horses' bellies; the men went in front treading it down and the camels came after. Two camels died, and one horse. One of the monk-soldiers had his toes frozen, and Ishaq cut off two of them while he bit on a stick and made no sound. Jingjing wore Old Duan's felt cap, which was so big it came down to his eyebrows, and from under its brim he watched the men in front and edged down step by step.

Down from the mountains, past Kashgar, past Qiuci, past Yanqi, the snow melted.

At the beginning of the fourth month the caravan entered the city of Xizhou.

Xizhou was still Tang land. At the city gate stood Tang soldiers in Tang armour, speaking Tang speech. As soon as Jingjing saw them his tears came. He did not know himself why he was crying. Old Duan, beside him, glanced at him, said nothing, and turned his face away.

Xu Jing went to talk to the soldiers at the gate and showed the bronze tally of the Headquarters of the Military Governor of Shuofang. The soldier at the gate looked at it, and then at the long string of camels behind them and all the Hu in grey robes, and looked troubled, and went in and called a squad leader. The squad leader went in and called a company commander. The company commander came out, looked at the tally, asked a few questions, waved his hand, and let them in.

Once they were inside the city Xu Jing said that there were not many troops in the city; most had been sent east, and those that were left were frightened too.

"What's happened in the east?" Jingjing asked.

Xu Jing did not answer at once. He had talked a long while with the company commander, and only now did he bring out what he had heard, one thing at a time.

Chang'an had fallen. It had fallen in the sixth month of last year. The Son of Heaven had gone to Shu, and the Crown Prince had taken the throne at Lingwu, and was the new Son of Heaven. Lord Guo had brought the Shuofang troops to Lingwu, and later gone to Hedong, and was now around Fengxiang, following the new Son of Heaven, preparing to fight his way back into Chang'an.

And one more thing. In the first month, An Lushan had died. His own son had killed him.

As Jingjing listened his head was in a whirl. Chang'an had fallen. He thought of the monastery in the Yining Ward, of the great locust tree at the monastery gate, of the people in the monastery. Where were they now?

Then he thought: Pa is with Lord Guo. Lord Guo is at Fengxiang. Pa is at Fengxiang.

He gripped the wooden cross at his neck.

They found the church in the north of the city.

It was the one the old bookseller had spoken of when Jingjing passed through the year before. There was a cross carved on the gate, its four ends rounded. The gate was shut, the lacquer flaking, the ring rusted. Haddad went up and pushed, and it was not locked; it opened with a creak.

The courtyard was overgrown with grass half a man high. The door of the sanctuary was open too; inside it was dark and smelled of mould. On the east wall of the sanctuary hung a wooden cross, thick with dust.

The seven teachers stood in the courtyard, and none of them spoke.

After a good while Narsai went in first. He went up to the east wall, put out his hand, and wiped the dust from the wooden cross with his sleeve. He wiped it once, and then again.

Then he knelt.

The other six followed him in and knelt.

The twenty-four monk-soldiers in the courtyard knelt too.

Old Duan and Xu Jing stood at the courtyard gate, not knowing whether to kneel or not. Xu Jing glanced at Old Duan. Old Duan thought about it and went to take off his felt cap—and only then remembered that the cap was still on Jingjing's head. He reached over, took the cap off Jingjing's head, and held it in his own fist.

Jingjing stood between them. He looked at the grey robes kneeling in the grass, and suddenly thought of the church at Balkh, of the two old men under the mulberry tree.

He knelt too.

When he had knelt he got up and took an oilcloth packet from his bundle.

The oilcloth had been wrapped round it a year and was worn through at the edges, but the roll of scripture inside was still sound. He went to the west side of the sanctuary, where a scripture cupboard stood against the wall. Its door was half open and inside it was empty, only a few boards that had held the rolls, covered in dust.

Babai came after him. He looked at the roll, then at Jingjing.

"The elder Ahad bought it in the market last year," said Jingjing. "It belongs to this church. He told me to put it back on my way home."

Babai said nothing. He wiped the top board of the cupboard clean with his sleeve, took the roll, held it in both hands, and laid it there.

An empty cupboard with one roll of scripture in it looked emptier than before.

But Babai looked at it a while and nodded, as if this was right.

The next day they began pulling grass.

It took three days to pull the courtyard clean. Five more days to clean out the sanctuary and the side rooms, mend the leaking roof, and repair the broken doors and windows. Haddad made several trips into the city and bought timber, plaster and oil lamps, and found a few hired hands.

Among the hired hands was a woman of forty or so, a Han, whose husband had been called up to fight in the east last year and never been heard of since. She came to the church to cook and wash clothes for a few coppers a day. The first few days she was very afraid of these Hu, did not dare speak to them, and when she brought the food kept her head down and set it down and left.

The old Hu bookseller came once too. He stood at the church gate looking in, saw the scripture cupboard in the sanctuary, and looked at it a long time. The next day he came with two rolls of scripture in his arms, laid them on the step at the gate, did not come in, and turned and left. Babai ran after him, but he was already far off.

There were three rolls in the cupboard.

Over the next ten days and more, people from the city came by in ones and twos.

Hu and Han, old people and children. They stood at the church gate peering in, and when they saw the men in grey robes in the courtyard, they did not go away. One old man came in, walked to the door of the sanctuary, saw the wooden cross wiped clean on the east wall, and dropped to his knees with a thud, kneeling on the ground and weeping, weeping till his whole body shook.

Ishaq went over and helped him up and asked him in a Hu tongue who he was.

The old man said he was an old believer of this church; after the elder of the church died, everyone had scattered and not dared to come. He had heard that people had come to the church again, and had come to see.

Ishaq asked him: how many are there still in the city?

The old man said: Some. Not many any more.

Jingjing began to teach the seven teachers to write Tang characters.

On the road the elder Ahad had taught them to speak. Now the seven could speak several dozen sentences of Tang speech—"thank you," "please sit down," "how much," "where is the water"—crookedly, but they could get them out. But not one Tang character could they write.

His uncle had said: if you don't teach them, who will? The elder had said: when you get to Xizhou, you'll be teaching alone.

The first day he stood in front of the seven, his face burned red as fire, and he opened his mouth a long time and not a word came out. All seven were decades older than he; all were teachers ordained at the Patriarchate, who had preached and baptised. He was a child of eleven—he was to teach them to write?

Ishaq saw that he was frightened, and said with a smile: "Child, think of us as seven little boys who can't write."

Jingjing thought about it and decided that was a good idea.

He did not teach them God, nor the Way. He taught them the simplest characters first. Water. Fire. Gate. Road. Hungry. Cold. Sick. Peace.

He wrote these characters on a board, very large, each stroke separate, and had them trace them with their fingers in trays of sand.

Babai learned fastest. He had charge of the scriptures anyway, his fingers stained with ink all day, and when he wrote Tang characters there was nothing raw about it. In three days he could write water, fire, gate, road square and neat.

Narsai learned slowest. He held the brush the way a man holds a knife, gripped so hard his fingers went white. The character for man that he wrote, 人, its two strokes like two crossed spears, stuck stiff into the paper. Jingjing looked at it a long time and did not know what to say to him.

Narsai looked at his character too, looked a long time, and said: "Wrong."

"It is a bit wrong," said Jingjing in a small voice.

"Where is it wrong?"

Jingjing thought and said: "The character for man is a man standing. Yours looks like two men fighting."

Narsai's brows drew together. Jingjing thought he was going to lose his temper and shrank back.

Narsai did not lose his temper. He stared at the character a while longer, then crumpled the paper into a ball, spread out a fresh sheet, and wrote it again.

He wrote the better part of the day. By dark the man he wrote no longer looked like a fight.

Ishaq did not learn fast, but he used the most.

Every character he learned, he took and used at once. When he learned eat, he went to the kitchen and said to the woman who worked there: "Eat." When he learned slow, he said to her: "Slow. Slow." When he learned mo pa, don't be afraid, he said to her: "Don't be afraid."

His Tang speech was very ugly; his tones were all crooked, so that mo pa, "don't be afraid," came out sounding like "grind and crawl." The hardest to mend was rice. On the road the elder Ahad had taught him to say wan, and his mouth had got used to it, so that even in Xizhou he could not change. The first day, he took a bowl to the kitchen and said to the woman: "Wan. Is the wan ready?"

The woman stared a long time, not knowing what he wanted. Jingjing, nearby, amused and anxious at once, ran over and said: "He's asking if the rice is ready."

The woman looked at Ishaq, then at Jingjing, and could not help laughing. It was the first time she had laughed since she came to the church.

After that, every time Ishaq went to the kitchen he first said "wan," then corrected himself to "fan." The woman grew used to it, and sometimes, before he could correct himself, she would hand him the bowl.

One day Jingjing saw the woman crying as she washed clothes. Ishaq stood beside her, at a loss, saying "don't be afraid" over and over, and she still cried. Ishaq thought, took a dry flatbread from inside his robe, broke off half, and held it out to her.

The woman looked at the half flatbread and cried harder.

Only later did Jingjing learn that that day a soldier had come into the city, fled back from the east, and said that the company her husband was in had all died outside Tong Pass.

Gabriel learned earnestly, but he always wanted to learn the hard ones.

He asked Jingjing: "How do you say grace?"

Jingjing taught him.

He asked: "How do you say forgiveness?"

Jingjing taught him that too.

He asked: "How do you say the Word made flesh?"

Jingjing thought a long time and did not know how to say it. In Chang'an, when the elders of the monastery preached, they used words like Mishihe, Alaha, the Three-in-One. He had heard them, but what these words meant, he could not quite say.

Babai, beside them, said: "The monastery in Chang'an has scriptures already. Translated in Alopen's time. Whatever words they use, just use those."

Gabriel said: "Those words, I've heard, are many of them borrowed from the Buddhist scriptures."

Babai said: "And what if they're borrowed?"

The two of them began arguing in Syriac. Jingjing could not follow much; he only heard the sounds of Fo, Buddha, and Dao, the Way, mixed into the Syriac, popping up now here and now there.

When they had argued a good while, both turned their heads to look at Jingjing, as if waiting for him to speak.

Jingjing was a little flustered. He thought, and remembered something Pa had said.

"Pa says," he said, "using other people's words is like living in other people's house. You can live there. Only, when you've lived there long, don't forget it isn't your own house."

Gabriel and Babai both stopped short.

Babai scratched his head and argued no more. Gabriel looked at Jingjing, nodded, said the words over in Syriac, and wrote the character for house in his sand tray, and asked Jingjing whether he had written it right.

Yonan learned most quietly.

He was not as fast as Babai, nor as slow as Narsai. He asked neither the hard ones nor the easy ones. Whatever Jingjing taught, he learned, and when he had learned it he sat alone in a corner writing it over and over in his sand tray.

Jingjing noticed that what he wrote most was that character 心, heart.

As he wrote it he said it. Xin. Xin. Xin. He said it very softly, as if afraid of being heard.

One day Jingjing went over and watched him write.

When Yonan had finished a heart he looked up, saw Jingjing, smiled a little, and asked him: "This character—have I written it right?"

Jingjing looked. The three dots fell very steady, and the curving hook curved very beautifully. More beautiful than his own.

"Yes," said Jingjing. "Better than mine."

He said it a little louder than he meant to. At the next sand tray, Narsai stopped.

Narsai glanced into Yonan's sand tray, looked a while. Then he bent his head, smoothed out the man he had just written in his own tray, and wrote another. This one he wrote very hard; his finger nearly poked through to the bottom of the tray.

Yonan did not look up. He smoothed out his own tray, and wrote another heart.

In the fifth month, Old Duan was to leave.

He told Jingjing he had to go back. Lord Guo had sent him to escort Jingjing to the Patriarchate. They had been to the Patriarchate, and he had brought the boy back; he had to go back and tell Master Yi, and tell Lord Guo. Lord Guo was waiting to hear about the Arabs.

"Xu Jing stays," said Old Duan. "He was supposed to go back home anyway, and home's in Hedong, and there's still fighting in Hedong, so he can't get back. He'll stay here and look after you."

Jingjing asked: "Uncle Duan, will you come back?"

Old Duan thought about it and said: "When Chang'an's taken back, you'll all go to Chang'an. I'll wait for you in Chang'an."

Jingjing nodded. He took out of his bundle the rolls of paper, the ones he had written on all along the road. He had filled two whole rolls, and the second was nearly full. He gave both to Old Duan.

"For Pa," he said.

He took out two more sheets. One he had written the night before: the names of the seven teachers, one by one, in Tang characters, with a note beside each of the characters he had learned. The other was the sheet from that night at the Patriarchate, when the elder Ahad had said one thing and he had written one thing—keys, rafter, Auntie Kang, the old groom, the apricot tree. After it a line had been added, under the mulberry tree at Balkh.

"These two are for Pa too," he said. "So he knows the people are on the road. And the elder's words are on there."

Old Duan took them, wrapped them carefully in oilcloth, and pushed them inside his coat, against his chest.

On the day Old Duan left, Jingjing saw him out through the city gate.

Outside the gate was gravel desert, and across the desert a road ran east, drawn out to the edge of the sky. Old Duan sat on his grey horse and turned to look at Jingjing.

"Take care of yourself," he said. "Don't drink cold water."

Jingjing's face went red again. "Uncle Duan!"

Old Duan laughed, only half his face moving. He raised his hand and waved to Jingjing, and waved to Xu Jing standing behind him, then turned his horse and went east.

Jingjing stood at the city gate and watched him go.

The grey horse grew smaller and smaller, and became a little black dot on the gravel. The black dot went on a long time before it was gone.

As Jingjing turned to go back, he saw Yonan standing inside the gate. He did not know how long he had been standing there.

Yonan was looking east too.
