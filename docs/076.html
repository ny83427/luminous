Volume Eleven　Huichang

Chapter 76　Packing

The inspectorate came to inventory the day after the stele was buried. It was the young judge, the one who had come in the fourth month to check ages, with four scribes, two officers, a dozen-odd clerks, and men from Chang'an county; the ward headman came too. The judge set a table under the colonnade before the hall; the scribes spread their registers; the officers stood on either side; the clerks split into parties—one to the hall, one to the scripture room, one to the back courtyard, one to the west wing—and went room by room, counting a thing, calling a thing, the scribes recording a thing.

Jingfu stood by the table; whatever the judge asked, he answered. Hall, how many bays: five. Colonnade, how many bays: twelve. West wing, how many: six. Back courtyard, how many: four. Well: one. Jujube tree: one; locust trees: two. Scripture room: one; scripture cabinets: three. Stove: one; gruel pots: two, one cracked and mended. The judge asked what was in the scripture cabinets; Jingfu said scriptures, in Syriac and in Tang—some brought from the Patriarchate, some translated by the monastery, some copied by the monastery. The judge sent a clerk to count them; the clerk counted over a watch and could not get them straight, came out and said: the cabinets are full of writing, some written across, some written down; those written across look like crawling insects; can't read one. The judge asked Jingfu how these scriptures were to be disposed of; Jingfu said the dispatch says scriptures, images, articles and stele: the leader to dispose of as he judges fit; he wanted to take the scriptures away; the scriptures were the monastery's; with the monastery confiscated, the scriptures were not money, not land, not bronze; the government would have no use for them; he'd take them, not to sell, not to burn; later, if anyone wanted to read them, there'd be somewhere to read them. The judge thought, and had the scribe record: scripture rolls, a number; disposed of by the leader as he judged fit; to go with him.

They came to the things in the hall. On the table was that glass bowl, Sasanian, its surface ground with round facets, bestowed by the Son of Heaven on the Great Worthy Yisi in the first year of Qianyuan, used at the offering once in seven for more than eighty years. The judge asked what it was; Jingfu said: a bowl, for the offering bread, bestowed by a Son of Heaven. The judge picked it up and held it to the light; on the bowl's wall were rings of round facets, and the light came through the facets and cast rings of shadow on the table. He looked at those rings of shadow and asked: bestowed by a Son of Heaven—on whom? Jingfu said: on the monastery's Great Worthy Yi; the Great Worthy Yi was under Lord Guo's command; his name is written on the stele; in that battle at the Xiangji Monastery he took three arrows. The judge said: and the stele? Jingfu said: buried; yesterday the county's Shaofu came; it's recorded in the register. The judge said he'd heard. He put the bowl back on the table and told the scribe: an imperially bestowed object; record: glass bowl, one; disposed of by the leader as he judged fit. He said nothing else.

They came to Du De's bowl, that coarse pottery bowl with a notch, still kept in a small wooden box beside the scripture cabinet behind the hall, beside Jingjing's and Yisi's. The judge asked what these three bowls were; Jingfu said: three men's bowls—one a brother of the Great Worthy Yi's from his days in Hebei, one the Great Worthy Yi's, one the Great Worthy Jingjing's; all three are dead; the bowls are kept. The judge glanced at them and said: worthless; don't record them.

When they had finished counting, the sun was already slanting west. The judge put his mark on the register and had Jingfu put his mark too; Jingfu did. The judge said: the buildings are confiscated; from today the monastery gate is sealed by the ward; the community is to move out within three days; the Tang go back to their native places to be registered; the foreigners, once the Office of Receptions has examined them and made its roll, are to be sent back to their own places. He said: for your monastery, the dispatch says foreigners may travel with caravans going west; the inspectorate has already informed the Jingzhao prefecture; the Jingzhao prefecture will issue a dispatch and the Office of Gates of the Department of State will give travel permits; find a caravan, report it, and you can go.

He stood up to leave, then stopped and looked once at the empty ground in the courtyard. He said: Leader, in the fourth month when he came, you took off your robe and he told you to put it back on; he remembers. He said: today when he came, you told everything as it was; he'll remember that too. When he had said it, he left with his men.

Out within three days. The monastery's people moved to the south end of the ward. Into Lingbao and He Siniang's little courtyard—two rooms, a well, a pomegranate tree—crowded Joseph and Elijah and the two bedridden old brothers; the old brothers lay in the kitchen, where He Siniang spread straw and felt on the floor. Salang and the Shi brothers went to live at the Kangs'; Old Mother Kang had lived alone in those three low rooms ten years, Rijin had lived there half a year since coming back, and now three more crowded in. Jingfu took Ayi and Xiaoman and lived in Headman Liu's woodshed; Headman Liu met him at the ward gate that day and said his woodshed was empty; it was winter; there wasn't much firewood; if the leader didn't mind, move in; he wouldn't take money; only, later, if the ward's people asked, he'd say he'd lent it to distant relatives. Jingfu said he wouldn't say they were distant relatives; if people asked, he'd say Headman Liu lent it. Headman Liu said: as you like.

The scriptures were stored in several places. The Syriac scriptures Joseph and Elijah kept; they were going west and would take them back to the Patriarchate; the Tang-language scriptures, Jingjing's translations, and the eleven newly copied rolls Qushen had brought back, Jingfu kept; the few bark-skin cases from the scripture room, brought from the Patriarchate in Elder Alopen's time—the oldest rolls—Lingbao kept. Lingbao said he couldn't see, and keeping them he couldn't read them; but these few rolls Elder Alopen had brought, brought from the Patriarchate, and he wanted to take them back to the Patriarchate. Samaan's nine account books, Luo Haoxin's sheet, Jingjing's slip with the wrong shi, Official Pei's register—Lingbao put them all in an oilcloth bundle, had He Siniang sew it shut, and carried it on his own back.

The glass bowl Jingfu kept. He wrapped it in an old cloth, several layers, and put it at the very centre of his bundle. Du De's bowl, the Great Worthy Yisi's bowl, his Master's bowl—the three bowls—Jingfu thought about all night, and the next day told Lingbao he wanted to leave these three bowls to Rijin. Lingbao said the bowls weren't worth anything and were heavy to carry; leaving them was good.

That old board from Xizhou Jingfu gave to no one but kept himself. On the morning of the inventory he stood morning devotion as usual and went into the scripture room—the last time he went into the scripture room—read a passage of scripture, took up the charcoal, and wrote four characters on the board; what he wrote was as he judged fit. When he had written it, he looked at it, did not rub it out, tucked the board under his arm, and came out.

The Office of Receptions examined them in the ninth month. After the decree of the eighth month, the monks and nuns of the monasteries kept in the empire no longer belonged under the Commissioners of Merit but were transferred to the Office of Receptions. People in the city said the Office of Receptions was the office under the Ministry of Rites that managed the audiences of foreigners; putting monks and nuns under the Office of Receptions meant monks and nuns were a foreign teaching and monks were foreigners, to be managed from now on, like hu merchants and hu envoys, by those who managed foreigners. The West Market laughed and said: more than half of Chang'an's monks are Tang; put under the Office of Receptions, the Tang have become foreigners too.

The people of the Daqin Monastery went to the Office of Receptions on the seventh of the ninth month. Jingfu led them—Lingbao, Joseph, Elijah, Salang, and the two bedridden old brothers, one carried, one on a stick. The Office of Receptions was in the Imperial City, a side courtyard by the offices of the Ministry of Rites of the Department of State; the courtyard was packed with people—of the Magi, of the Zoroastrian shrines, from the hu monasteries of the West Market; Persians, Sogdians, Tokharians, and a few Uighurs too—all squatting under the colonnade, waiting for their names to be called.

The Office of Receptions' minor clerk sat behind a table, twenty-odd, with a few pimples on his face, a brush in his hand, a register spread before him. When the Daqin Monastery was called, Jingfu led them in and they stood before the table. The clerk did not look up, and said: report one by one—name, age, native place, what country.

Joseph reported first. He said he was called Joseph, fifty-eight, a man of Merv, who came by sea in the fourth year of Yuanhe with the Patriarchate's Elder David. The clerk asked what country Merv was; Joseph said Persia, governed now by the Tazi. The clerk wrote: Daqin monk Joseph, aged fifty-eight, of Merv in Persia; to be sent back to his own place. Elijah the same. When Salang reported, the clerk asked what country he was; Salang said his grandfather's father came from the Patriarchate, called Sargis, a guard of the monastery; his grandfather was born in Chang'an, and so was he. The clerk asked what country the Patriarchate was; Salang said Persia too, by the two rivers. The clerk wrote: Salang, guard of the Daqin Monastery, aged forty-nine, ancestral home Persia; to be sent back to his own place. Salang stood watching him write; his mouth opened, and he did not argue.

It was Jingfu's turn. The clerk asked what country he was. Jingfu said he was born in Chang'an, grew up in Chang'an; his Pa was a soldier in the Shence Army called Sesame Cake, surnamed An, who died in Huaixi in the Zhenyuan years; who his Ma was he didn't know; he came to the monastery at five. The clerk looked up at him and said: surnamed An—a man of An? Jingfu said his Pa was descended from men of An, several generations in Chang'an; how many generations he didn't know. The clerk said: An is An; the father's line is An; you're a man of An. He wrote: Jingfu, leader of the Daqin Monastery, aged fifty, a man of An; to be sent back to his own place. Jingfu watched him write; he wanted to say his Pa was a soldier in the Shence Army, the Son of Heaven's soldier, who died in the Son of Heaven's war; he thought, and did not say it.

It was Lingbao's turn. He Siniang held him up; he stood before the table. He had left the register in the fourth year of Dahe and was a registered householder of Yining Ward, Chang'an county, the household register saying Yi Lingbao; but that day all the Daqin Monastery's people had come, and so he came too; the Office of Receptions' clerk leafed through the register sent from the Jingzhao prefecture, found his name, and said: Yi Lingbao, a registered householder—what are you here for? Lingbao said he was the monastery's man; the monastery's people had all come; he came; he was with them. The clerk said: you're a registered householder; registered householders don't belong to the Office of Receptions. He turned a page, looked again, and said: the household register says father Yisi, of Balkh in Tokharistan; he asked Lingbao: your Pa was a man of Balkh? Lingbao said yes. The clerk asked: and your Ma? Lingbao said his Ma was surnamed Pei, a Chang'an woman, daughter of a minor clerk of the Jingzhao prefecture. The clerk said: then what country are you?

Lingbao stood there. He could not see that clerk, could not see that register, could not see that room; he listened to that clerk's voice, twenty-odd, young, impatient. He answered; answered as it was. He said his Pa was of Balkh in Tokharistan, his Ma was Tang, he was born in Yining Ward in Chang'an, had lived seventy-eight years, and had never gone outside the Guannei circuit.

The clerk said: then it's the father's line; the father's line is Tokharian; you're a Tokharian. He said the decree says foreigners are sent back to their own places; your Pa was of Balkh; your own place is Balkh. He took up his brush and wrote: Yi Lingbao, aged seventy-eight, of Balkh in Tokharistan; father Yisi; mother Tang; formerly a registered householder, now transferred; to be sent back to his own place. When he had written it, he said to He Siniang: what are you to him? He Siniang said she was his wife. The clerk asked what country she was; He Siniang said her surname was He; the He family had been three generations in the West Market; she was born in Chang'an, and her registration was Chang'an's. The clerk asked: and your Pa? He Siniang said her Pa was born in Chang'an too; her Pa's Pa came from the west, a man of He. The clerk leafed through the Jingzhao prefecture's register and said: the He family, three generations registered householders; the register says of Chang'an county; you're Tang; you're not sent; whether you go with him is your own affair. He Siniang said she'd go with him. Jingfu stood by listening: his Pa's surname was An, He Siniang's Pa's surname was He; An and He, both from the same place; one written down as Tang, one written down as a man of An; the difference was the He family's three generations as registered householders; his Pa was a soldier in the Shence Army who died in Huaixi, and left him no household.

Xiaoman and Ayi were asked last. Xiaoman was the one the Shi Second found outside the Jinguang Gate; at Headman Liu's she was recorded as He Siniang's adopted daughter; the clerk asked whether she was Tang or hu; He Siniang said she didn't know; when she was found her mother was dead under a willow, and there was no telling where she was from; the clerk said: then she follows the adoptive mother, the adoptive mother follows the husband, so she goes with them. Ayi was recorded at the ward headman's as an orphan girl taken in by the Daqin Monastery, mother dead, father unknown; the clerk asked whom the orphan girl followed; Jingfu said she followed the monastery; with the monastery gone, she followed him; the clerk said: the community is sent back; the orphan girl follows the community, to go wherever they go; and added two lines in the register. The clerk closed the register and called the next house.

That day they came out of the Office of Receptions, out of the Imperial City, into the street. Joseph walked in front, Elijah supporting the old brother on the stick, Salang and the Shi eldest carrying the one lying down, Jingfu behind, and Lingbao last, He Siniang holding him up. On the Zhuque Street Lingbao stopped; He Siniang asked what was wrong; he said he'd rest a moment.

He stood by the Zhuque Street; people came and went, carts and horses, donkeys, men with carrying poles, men crying wares; he could not see, and listened. Jingfu turned back, came to his side, and asked: Uncle Lingbao, are you tired? Lingbao said he wasn't tired. He said: just now inside, that clerk asked him what country he was, and he answered, and when he'd answered the clerk wrote Balkh. He said: just now inside, as that clerk raised his brush to write, he remembered the night Pa died, Pa asking is the road still open, and Brother saying it's open; he's travelled it once. He said: his country is not in Balkh, nor in Chang'an. Jingfu reached out and held his other arm. Lingbao put his hand on He Siniang's arm and said: let's go.

Rijin had been with them all those days. He was there the day of the inventory, there the day of the moving; the day they went to the Office of Receptions he waited at the Imperial City gate for them to come out. He was Tang, a registered householder of Yining Ward in Chang'an county, thirty-nine, returned to lay life; the Office of Receptions' register had no place for him.

One day in the tenth month, Lingbao had Rijin come to that little courtyard at the south end of the ward. That night the little courtyard was packed with people: the two old brothers lying in the kitchen; Joseph and Elijah in the room packing their baggage; Salang in the yard tying loads; He Siniang under the lamp sewing oilcloth bundles. Lingbao sat under the pomegranate tree, had Rijin sit across from him, and had He Siniang bring out a thing.

It was the lamp from the gate. The bronze lamp-dish half the size of a palm, its wick twisted from hemp, its bottom soaked black with oil, a bright mark worn on its rim where hand after hand had held it these decades. The day the monastery gate was sealed, Jingfu lifted it from the stone block, tipped out the dregs, wiped it clean, wrapped it in a cloth, and handed it to Lingbao.

Lingbao held the lamp-dish in his hands, felt that bright mark, and told Rijin: this lamp was burning in the Tianbao years; an old deacon lit it then; the year An Lushan broke into Chang'an it went out; in the second year of Zhide, when his Pa came back to Chang'an, he set it on the steps and lit it again; in the first year of Qianyuan Elder Yonan moved it to the stone block left of the gate, and since then it has stayed on the stone block; his Pa lit it; when Pa went, Brother lit it; Brother lit it for decades and went, and Jingfu lit it. He said: a hundred years; lit once at dusk; on windy nights someone squatted beside it shielding it with his hands; it hardly ever went out.

He said: the monastery is gone; the stone block is still there; the gate is sealed; the lamp can't be lit any more. He said they were going—he and Jingfu, Joseph and Elijah, Salang, all going, sent back to their own places; going far; on the road the wind is strong; the lamp can't go. He said: you are Tang; you stay; you're a registered householder; your Ma is here; your Pa is buried outside the Kaiyuan Gate; you stay; this lamp—you take it. He said he wasn't telling you to go and light it at the monastery gate; the gate is sealed; if you went and lit it there and were seen, it would be breaking the ban; he was telling you to take it home, find a stone at your own gate, put it on the stone, light it once at dusk; if someone of the ward passes and sees it and knows what it is, they'll know; if they don't know, it doesn't matter.

Rijin knelt before him, put out both hands, and received the lamp. He held it, held it very low, as if afraid it would break. He said that long ago he stood outside the gate, stood a good many years, and wouldn't come in; this lamp he watched every day; he stood beside it; it lit the threshold, and he stood outside the threshold. He said: Uncle Lingbao, he's taken it; he'll light it every day.

Jingfu beside them took out that wooden box; in it were the three bowls—Du De's, the Great Worthy Yisi's, his Master's—and he gave them to Rijin too. He said: the bowls aren't worth anything; we can't take them; you keep them; later, when your family eats, set them on the table; whether or not anyone eats from them, set them out.

They left the city on the twentieth of the tenth month. Those sent back went with a Sogdian caravan bound for Liangzhou. The caravan's headman was surnamed Cao, past fifty, who had traded silk and horses in the West Market all his life; this time he had shut his shop to go west; he said Chang'an was no place to stay any more; he was an old acquaintance of He Qi's; the year He Qi left, He Qi urged him to go, and he didn't; now he was going too. The Office of Receptions had made its roll, the Jingzhao prefecture had issued its dispatch, the Office of Gates of the Department of State had given the travel permit, and the permit listed one by one names, ages, native places: Yi Lingbao, aged seventy-eight, of Balkh in Tokharistan; wife, née He, aged fifty-four, following her husband; Jingfu, aged fifty, a man of An; adopted daughter Xiaoman, aged eight; orphan girl Ayi, aged about eleven; Joseph; Elijah; Salang; two old brothers; three donkeys, one cart; travelling with the Cao caravan west of Liangzhou; to be passed on inspection at the barriers and fords along the way. Past the Long mountains, Liangzhou, Ganzhou and Shazhou were all in Tibetan hands; the Tang prefectures and counties ended once you passed Jingzhou; the permit reached only to the Tang border; further west was Headman Cao's business; Headman Cao said he'd travelled it who knew how many times; at the Tibetan barriers, pay the duty and they let you pass. Where their own place was, the permit did not say; what it said was their own place.

That day before light, the caravan waited on the open ground outside the Kaiyuan Gate: twenty-odd camels, a dozen-odd horses, a few carts, the loads all tied. Those going: Lingbao, He Siniang, Jingfu, Joseph, Elijah, Salang, Ayi, Xiaoman, and the two old brothers, one lying in the cart, one riding a donkey.

While it was still dark they stood morning devotion one last time in that little courtyard at the south end of the ward. There was no middle of the courtyard, no jujube tree, no well curb, no hall door; just under the pomegranate tree, crowded together standing. Jingfu stood in the middle, and when the sky greyed he looked back and counted. Lingbao, He Siniang, Joseph, Elijah, Salang, Ayi, Xiaoman, the two old brothers—one lying on a door board, carried out and set under the pomegranate tree; Rijin, Old Mother Kang, the Shi brothers; of the four from the four old believer households at the south end of the ward, three came that day. When he had counted, it was seventeen.

When he had counted, they all walked to the Kaiyuan Gate. Some of the ward's people came out to look, standing at their doors, some with folded hands, some wiping their eyes. Headman Liu stood under the ward gate, opened it, and watched them pass one by one; as each passed, he nodded once.

Outside the Kaiyuan Gate the caravan was waiting. Those seeing them off: Rijin holding up his Ma, the Shi brothers, people of those few south-end households; Lü Shao came too and stood at the back, in a suit of black, his dress for duty at the Secretariat; he said he'd taken leave today. Qushen did not come; Salang looked round the crowd for him and did not find him; later, in the shadow under the gateway, he saw a man in a grey Daoist robe standing with both hands folded in his sleeves, looking this way; Salang nodded to him, and the man nodded too.

On the other side of the gateway, against the wall, there was a donkey too, and on the donkey an old man in a green robe, a little hunchbacked; he did not get down, nor come this way, but sat on the donkey watching from afar. Jingfu saw him and knew him; he did not go over; he stood where he was, clasped his hands, and bowed toward him. The old man on the donkey raised his hand too, raised it to his breast, lowered it, turned the donkey's head, and went back into the city.

Headman Cao called from the front: we're off. The camels rose one after another, hind legs first, then forelegs; the loads swayed a few times and steadied. Lingbao was helped up into a cart, He Siniang beside him, Xiaoman on his other side; Ayi rode a little camel; Joseph and Elijah rode donkeys; Salang walked, leading the first camel by the halter. Jingfu was last; he stood before those seeing them off and told Rijin: go home; your Ma can't stand much longer. Rijin said he'd watch them go. Jingfu said: then watch.

He turned and walked toward the caravan, and a few steps on he broke wind. Very loud. On that open ground outside the Kaiyuan Gate, those seeing off, those going, camels, horses—all of them heard it. Jingfu stopped, red in the face, turned back, and said: it was me.

Xiaoman laughed first; she was eight, and laughed out loud, covering her mouth. Ayi laughed after her. Salang, holding the halter, turned back, tears still on his face, and his mouth split wide. Joseph on his donkey was taken aback a moment, shook his head, and laughed too, and laughing, wiped his eyes with his sleeve. Rijin held up his Ma; Old Mother Kang was eighty-one, her eyes dim; she heard, and asked Rijin what it was; Rijin said: the leader; Old Mother Kang said he was like this as a child at morning devotion; the Great Worthy Jingjing was still alive then; the Great Worthy went on singing at the front, and when it broke up called him to his room to drink a bowl of hot water. As she said it she laughed too, laughed till she coughed, and Rijin patted her back. Lü Shao stood at the back, his head bowed, his shoulders shaking.

Jingfu himself did not laugh. He stood a moment, looked back once at the Kaiyuan Gate, looked once at the man in the grey Daoist robe under the gateway, looked once at the little crowd seeing them off—Rijin, Old Mother Kang, the Shi brothers, Lü Shao. Then he turned, went to the side of Lingbao's cart, took hold of the cart's shaft, and went west with the caravan.

Lingbao sat in the cart; he could see nothing; he had heard that sound, heard the people behind laughing, and he laughed too. He said to He Siniang: if Brother were here he'd call him to drink a bowl of hot water and eat a piece of ginger. He Siniang said: Brother isn't here any more. Lingbao said: no, Brother isn't here any more; he is.
