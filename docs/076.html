Volume Eleven　Huichang

Chapter 76　Their Own Countries

The inspectorate came to make the inventory on the second day after the stele was buried.

The one who came was that young assistant, the one who had come in the fourth month to check ages. He brought four clerks, two aides-de-camp and a dozen-odd minor clerks; Chang'an County sent men too, and the ward headman followed behind. The assistant set a table in the gallery before the hall, the clerks spread out the registers, and the minor clerks split into parties—one to the hall, one to the scripture room, one to the back courtyard, one to the west wing—and counted room by room. Each thing counted was called out, and each thing called out the clerks wrote down.

Jingfu stood beside the table.

"The hall—how many bays?"

"Five."

"Galleries?"

"Twelve bays. The west wing six, the back courtyard four. One well. One jujube tree, two scholar trees. One scripture room, three scripture cabinets. One stove, two gruel pots, one of them cracked and mended."

"What's in the scripture cabinets?"

"Scriptures. In Syriac and in Tang. Some brought from the Patriarchate, some translated at the monastery, some copied at the monastery."

The assistant sent a clerk to count. The clerk went into the scripture room and came out more than an hour later, his face covered in dust.

"Assistant, they can't be counted. The cabinets are full of writing, some written across, some written down. The ones written across look like crawling insects; I can't read one."

The assistant turned his head. "Director, how are these scriptures to be disposed of?"

"The dispatch says the scriptures, images, articles and stele the Director is to dispose of as he judges fit," said Jingfu. "I mean to take the scriptures with us. The scriptures are the monastery's. The monastery's confiscated, but scriptures aren't money, aren't fields, aren't bronze; they'd be no use to the government. If I take them, I won't sell them, won't burn them; one day if someone wants to recite them, there'll be somewhere to recite."

The assistant thought, and said to the clerk, "Record: scrolls of scripture, a quantity; disposed of by the Director as he judges fit; to go with him."

When they counted the hall, there was the glass bowl on the table.

A Sasanian bowl, cut-faced, with circular facets. In the first year of Qianyuan the Son of Heaven had granted it to Master Yisi, and it had been used for the offering once in seven eighty-odd years.

"What's this?"

"A bowl. Used for the offering bread. Granted by the Son of Heaven."

The assistant picked it up and looked at it against the light from the door. Ring upon ring of facets on its wall, the light passing through them and throwing rings of shadow on the table. He looked down at those shadows.

"Granted by the Son of Heaven. Granted to whom?"

"To Master Yi of the monastery. Master Yi served under Lord Guo the Director; his name is on the stele. In the battle at the Xiangji Monastery he took three arrows."

"And the stele?"

"Buried. Yesterday the county's Shaofu came; it's recorded in his register."

"I've heard."

The assistant set the bowl back on the table.

"An article of imperial gift. Record: one glass bowl; disposed of by the Director as he judges fit."

He said nothing else.

Behind the hall, beside the scripture cabinet, was a small wooden box with three bowls in it: one of coarse earthenware with a notch in its rim, and two more ordinary earthenware bowls. The assistant picked up the notched one and turned it over to look at its foot.

"And these three?"

"They're three men's bowls. One was Master Yi's brother-in-arms in Hebei in his day, one was Master Yi, one was Master Jingjing. All three are dead. The bowls were kept."

The assistant put the bowl back in the box.

"Worth nothing. Not recorded."

When the counting was done the sun was slanting west. The assistant put his mark in the register and handed the brush to Jingfu, and Jingfu put his mark too.

"The monastery's buildings are confiscated. From today the monastery gate is sealed by the ward, and the monastery's members are to move out within three days," said the assistant. "Tang men go back to their native places to be registered. Foreigners—once the Bureau of Receptions has examined them and drawn up a list—are sent back to their own countries. The dispatch says foreigners may travel with caravans going west; the inspectorate has already informed the Metropolitan Prefecture. The Metropolitan Prefecture issues a dispatch, the Office of Gates of the Department of State issues travel permits, and once you've found a caravan and reported it, you can go."

He stood up, took two steps, and stopped again, looking at the empty ground in the middle of the courtyard.

"Director, in the fourth month when I came, you took off your robe, and I told you to put it back on. That I remember," he said. "Today when I came, you said everything as it is. That I'll remember too."

He left with his men.

Within three days the monastery's people had moved to the south end of the ward.

Lingbao and He Siniang's little yard—two rooms, a well, a pomegranate tree—squeezed in Joseph and Elijah, and the two bedridden old monks, Elder Thomas and Elder Simeon. The two old men lay in the kitchen; He Siniang spread straw on the kitchen floor, and felt on the straw. Salang and the Shi brothers went to live at the Kangs'. The Kangs' three low rooms Mother Kang had lived in alone ten years, and Rijin half a year since he came back; now three more squeezed in.

Jingfu, with Ayi and Liu'er, had nowhere to go. Headman Liu met him at the ward gate.

"Director, the woodshed at my house is empty. It's winter, and there's not much wood. If you don't mind it, move in," said Headman Liu. "I won't take money. Only, if people in the ward ask, I'll say it's lent to distant relatives."

"I won't say we're distant relatives," said Jingfu. "If people ask, I'll say Headman Liu lent it."

Headman Liu looked at him and sighed.

"As you please."

The scriptures were divided among several places.

The Syriac scriptures Joseph and Elijah took charge of; they were going west and would take them back to the Patriarchate. The Tang scriptures—Jingjing's translations, and the eleven newly copied rolls Qushen had brought back—Jingfu took charge of. The few bark-skin cases in the scripture room, brought from the Patriarchate in Elder Alopen's day, with the oldest rolls, Lingbao asked for.

"I can't see; keeping them, I can't read them," said Lingbao. "But these few were brought by Elder Alopen from the Patriarchate. I want to take them back to the Patriarchate."

Samaan's nine ledgers, Luo Haoxin's sheet of paper, Jingjing's slip with the character shi, to give, Official Pei's little book—Lingbao put them all in an oiled-cloth packet, had He Siniang sew it shut, and carried it on his own back.

The glass bowl Jingfu took. He wrapped it in several layers of an old cloth and put it at the very centre of his bundle. As for the three bowls, he thought about them a night, and the next day went to tell Lingbao.

"I want to leave the three bowls with Rijin."

"The bowls are worth nothing and heavy to carry," said Lingbao. "Leaving them is good too."

The old board from Xizhou Jingfu gave to no one. On the morning of the inventory he stood the morning as always and went into the scripture room—it was the last time he went into the scripture room. He read a passage, took up the charcoal, and wrote on the board four characters: liang wei chu zhi, as he judges fit. When he had written them he did not wipe them off, but came out with the board under his arm.

The Bureau of Receptions held its examination in the ninth month.

After the edict of the eighth month, the monks and nuns of the monasteries kept in the realm no longer came under the Commissioners of Merit, but were reassigned to the Bureau of Receptions. The Bureau of Receptions was the office under the Ministry of Rites that managed the audiences of foreigners. People in the West Market laughed: the monks of Chang'an were more than half Tang, and now they were under the Bureau of Receptions, the Tang had become foreigners too.

The Daqin Monastery's people went on the seventh of the ninth month. Jingfu led them; Lingbao, Joseph, Elijah, Salang all went; of the two old monks, Simeon went on his stick and Thomas was carried. The Bureau of Receptions was inside the Imperial City, in a side courtyard beside the offices of the Ministry of Rites. The courtyard was packed: people of the Magi, of the fire-temples, of every hu temple of the West Market—Persians, Sogdians, Tokharians, a few Uighurs too—all crouched in the gallery waiting for their names to be called.

The Bureau's minor clerk sat behind a table, twenty or so, with a few pimples on his face, a register spread before him. When the Daqin Monastery was called, Jingfu led them in to stand before the table.

The clerk did not raise his head. "One at a time. Name, age, native place, what country."

Joseph reported first. "Joseph, fifty-eight, a man of Merv. Came by sea in the fourth year of Yuanhe with the Patriarchate's Elder David."

"What country is Merv?"

"It's Persia. Now it's ruled by the Arabs."

The clerk wrote: The Daqin monk Joseph, age fifty-eight, of Merv in Persia; to be sent back to his own country. Elijah the same.

Salang's turn.

"What country?"

"My grandfather came from the Patriarchate, named Sargis, a guard of the monastery. My Pa was born in Chang'an, and so was I."

"What country is the Patriarchate?"

"Persia too, by the Two Rivers."

The clerk wrote: Salang, guard of the Daqin Monastery, age forty-nine, ancestral country Persia; to be sent back to his own country. Salang stood watching him write, opened his mouth, and did not argue.

Jingfu's turn.

"What country?"

"I was born in Chang'an and grew up in Chang'an. My Pa was a soldier in the Shence Army called Sesame Cake, surnamed An, who died in Huaixi in the Zhenyuan years. Who my Ma was, I don't know. I came to the monastery at five."

The clerk raised his head. "Surname An. A man of An?"

"My Pa was descended from men of An, in Chang'an for several generations—how many, I don't know."

"If the father's line is An, you're a man of An."

He wrote: Jingfu, Director of the Daqin Monastery, age fifty, a man of An; to be sent back to his own country.

Jingfu watched him write. He wanted to say that his Pa was the Son of Heaven's soldier, who died in the Son of Heaven's war. He thought about it, and did not say it.

Lingbao's turn. He Siniang helped him to the table.

The clerk turned the pages of the register sent from the Metropolitan Prefecture, found his name, and frowned.

"Yi Lingbao, registered householder. Left the monk register as early as the fourth year of Dahe. What are you here for?"

"I'm the monastery's. The monastery's people have all come, and I've come with them."

"Registered householders don't come under the Bureau of Receptions." The clerk turned another page. "The household register says: father Yisi, of Balkh in Tokharistan. Your father was a man of Balkh?"

"Yes."

"And your mother?"

"My mother was surnamed Pei, of Chang'an, daughter of a minor clerk of the Metropolitan Prefecture."

"Then what country are you?"

Lingbao stood there. He could not see that clerk, could not see that register, could not see that room; he could hear only a young, impatient voice.

"My father was a man of Balkh in Tokharistan; my mother was Tang," he said. "I was born in the Yining Ward of Chang'an, and in seventy-eight years I've never been outside the Guannei Circuit."

"Then it's the father's line. The father's line is Tokharian, so you're Tokharian." The clerk lifted his brush. "The edict says foreigners are to be sent back to their own countries. Your father was a man of Balkh, so your own country is Balkh."

He wrote: Yi Lingbao, age seventy-eight, of Balkh in Tokharistan, father Yisi, mother Tang; formerly a registered householder, now reassigned; to be sent back to his own country.

When he had written it he looked up at He Siniang. "What are you to him?"

"His wife."

"What country?"

"My surname is He. The He family has been in the West Market three generations. I was born in Chang'an, and my register is Chang'an's."

"And your father?"

"My father was born in Chang'an too. My grandfather came from the west, a man of He."

The clerk turned the pages of the Metropolitan Prefecture's register. "The He family, registered householders three generations; the register says people of Chang'an County. You're Tang; you're not sent. Whether you go with him is your own affair."

"I'll go with him," said He Siniang.

The last asked about were Liu'er and Ayi.

"Liu'er was found, outside the Jinguang Gate. Her mother died under a willow; no telling where she was from," said He Siniang. "At the ward headman's she's recorded as my adopted daughter."

"Then she goes with her adoptive mother. The adoptive mother goes with her husband, so she goes along."

"And Ayi?"

"At the ward headman's she's recorded as an orphan girl taken in by the Daqin Monastery, mother dead, father unknown," said Jingfu. "She goes with the monastery. The monastery's gone, so she goes with me."

"The monastery's members sent back; the orphan girl to go with the members, wherever she will." The clerk added two lines to the register and closed it. "Next."

That day they came out of the Bureau of Receptions, out of the Imperial City, and into the street. Joseph walked in front, Elijah holding Simeon's arm, Salang and the elder Shi brother carrying Thomas, Jingfu behind, and Lingbao last, on He Siniang's arm.

When they reached the Zhuque Street, Lingbao stopped.

"What is it?" asked He Siniang.

"Let me rest a moment."

The street was full of people coming and going—carts and horses, donkeys, porters, hawkers. He couldn't see; he stood there listening. Jingfu turned back and came to his side.

"Uncle Lingbao, you're tired."

"I'm not tired," said Lingbao. "Back there, when that clerk asked me what country I was, as he lifted his brush to write, I remembered Pa's last night. Pa asked: is the road still open? Brother said: it's open; I've walked it once."

Jingfu waited for him to go on.

"He wrote that I'm a man of Balkh. I won't argue with him either," said Lingbao. "Fu'er, my country isn't in Balkh, and it isn't in Chang'an."

Jingfu reached out and took his other arm.

"Let's go," said Lingbao.

Rijin was with them all those days. He was there the day of the inventory and the day of the moving. On the day they went to the Bureau of Receptions he waited at the Imperial City gate for them to come out. He was Tang, a registered householder of the Yining Ward of Chang'an County, thirty-nine, returned to lay life; the Bureau's register did not have him.

One night in the tenth month Lingbao called Rijin to the little yard at the south end of the ward.

The little yard was full of people. In the kitchen lay the two old monks; in the room Joseph and Elijah were packing; Salang was tying packs in the yard; He Siniang was sewing oiled-cloth packets under the lamp. Lingbao sat under the pomegranate tree and had Rijin sit across from him.

"Siniang, bring that out."

He Siniang brought a cloth bundle out of the room and untied it. It was the lamp from the gate: a bronze dish half a palm across, the wick twisted of hemp, the bottom of the dish blackened by oil, a bright band worn on its rim—the place where hand after hand had held it these decades. On the day the monastery gate was sealed, Jingfu had taken it down from the stone block, tipped out the dregs, wiped it clean, wrapped it, and given it to Lingbao.

Lingbao held the lamp dish and felt that bright band.

"This lamp, lit from my Pa's time till today, has hardly ever gone out," he said. "The monastery's gone. The stone block's still there, but the gate is sealed, and the lamp can't be lit. We're going, and the wind is strong on the road; the lamp can't be taken."

He held the lamp dish out.

"Rijin, you're Tang; you stay. Your Ma is here, your Pa is buried outside the Kaiyuan Gate. Take this lamp."

"Uncle Lingbao…"

"I'm not telling you to go and light it at the monastery gate. The gate's sealed; if you lit it there and were seen, it would be breaking the ban. Take it home, find a stone by your own gate, set it on it, and light it once at dusk," said Lingbao. "If someone in the ward passes and sees it and knows what it is, then they'll know. If they don't know, it doesn't matter either."

Rijin knelt before him, held out both hands, and took the lamp, holding it very low.

"In my day I stood outside the gate many years and wouldn't come in," he said. "This lamp I looked at every day. It lit the threshold, and I stood outside the threshold. Uncle Lingbao, I've taken it. I'll light it every day."

Jingfu, beside them, brought out the small wooden box and gave it to him too.

"Three bowls. Worth nothing, and they can't be taken. You keep them. When your household eats, set them on the table—whether anyone eats from them or not, set them out."

They left the city on the twentieth of the tenth month.

Those being sent back went with a Sogdian caravan bound for Liangzhou. The caravan's headman was surnamed Cao, past fifty, who had traded silk and horses in the West Market all his life and had shut up his shop this time to go west.

"Chang'an's no place to stay any more," said Headman Cao. "The year He Qi left he urged me to go too, and I didn't. Now I'm going as well."

The Bureau of Receptions drew up the list, the Metropolitan Prefecture issued its dispatch, the Office of Gates of the Department of State issued the travel permit. The permit listed name, age and native place one by one: Yi Lingbao, age seventy-eight, of Balkh in Tokharistan; wife, Mistress He, age fifty-four, accompanying her husband. Jingfu, age fifty, a man of An; adopted daughter Liu'er, age eight; orphan girl Ayi, age about eleven. Joseph, Elijah, Salang, two old monks. Three donkeys, one cart. With the Cao caravan to west of Liangzhou; the passes and fords on the way to examine and let them pass.

Beyond the Long Mountains, Liangzhou, Ganzhou and Shazhou were all in Tibetan hands; once out of Jingzhou there were no more Tang prefectures or counties. The permit reached only as far as the Tang borders; beyond that, further west, was Headman Cao's affair.

"The Tibetan checkpoints—I've been through them I don't know how many times," said Headman Cao. "Pay the tax, and they let you through."

Where their own country was, the permit did not say. What it said was their own country.

That day, before light, they stood the morning one last time in that little yard at the south end of the ward.

There was no middle of a courtyard, no jujube tree, no well curb, no hall door; they crowded together under the pomegranate tree. Jingfu stood in the middle, and when the sky greyed looked back and counted: Lingbao, He Siniang, Joseph, Elijah, Salang, Ayi, Liu'er, the two old monks—Thomas lying on a door plank, carried out and set under the pomegranate tree—Rijin, Mother Kang, the Shi brothers, and from the four old believing households at the south end of the ward, three had come that day.

Seventeen.

When he had counted, they all set off for the Kaiyuan Gate. People of the ward came out to watch, standing at their doors, some with hands crossed, some wiping their eyes. Headman Liu stood under the ward gate, opened the ward gate, and watched them pass one by one; as each one passed, his lips moved, as if he were counting.

Outside the Kaiyuan Gate the caravan was waiting—twenty-odd camels, a dozen-odd horses, a few carts, the packs all tied. The people seeing them off stood to one side: Rijin holding his Ma's arm, the Shi brothers, those few households from the south end of the ward. Lü Shao had come too, standing at the back in a black robe, the dress he wore on duty at the Secretariat.

"I've taken leave today," he said.

Qushen did not come. Salang searched the crowd and couldn't find him; then, in the shadow under the gate passage, he saw a man in a grey Daoist robe, hands tucked in his sleeves, gazing this way. Salang laid one hand on his breast, and the two hands in that man's sleeves moved.

On the other side of the gate passage, against the wall, there was also a donkey. On the donkey sat an old man in a green robe, a little stooped. He did not get down from the donkey, nor come this way; he only sat on the donkey, gazing from afar. Jingfu knew him. He did not go over, but stood where he was, crossed his hands, and bowed toward him. The old man on the donkey raised his hand to his breast and lowered it, turned the donkey, and went back into the city.

Headman Cao shouted from the front: "Moving!"

The camels rose one after another, back legs first, then front legs; the packs swayed a few times and settled. Lingbao was helped onto a cart, He Siniang sitting beside him and Liu'er on his other side. Ayi rode a small camel. Joseph and Elijah rode donkeys, Simeon rode one, Thomas lay in the back of the cart. Salang went on foot, leading the first camel by its halter.

Jingfu was last. He stood before those seeing them off.

"Rijin, go home. Your Ma can't stand much longer."

"I'll watch you go."

"Then watch."

He turned and walked toward the caravan. He had gone a few steps when his belly gave a gurgle and something dropped. Not now, he cried inwardly. Before light He Siniang had boiled a pot of bean gruel—it was cold on the road, she said, they should eat something solid—and he had drunk two big bowls, and now it had come calling.

He slowed his steps, clamped his legs together, held his breath, and tried to keep it back. He held it one step, held it two steps, his face burning with the effort, and at the third step, could hold it no more.

Out it came—no small sound, and with a tail to it. On that open ground outside the Kaiyuan Gate, those seeing them off, those leaving, the camels, the horses, all heard it. The first camel had been chewing hay; its mouth stopped, and its ears swivelled round.

Jingfu stopped and turned his head back, red in the face.

"That was me."

Liu'er laughed first. She was eight, and laughed out loud, hands over her mouth. Ayi laughed after her. Salang, holding the halter, turned his head, tears still on his face, and his mouth split into a grin. Joseph, on his donkey, stared a moment, shook his head, and laughed too, and laughing, wiped his eyes with his sleeve.

Mother Kang, eighty-one, her eyes dim, tugged at Rijin's sleeve. "What was it?"

"It was the Director."

"When he was small he did the same at the morning," said Mother Kang. "Master Jingjing was still alive then. The Master went on singing in front, and when it broke up, called him into his room to drink a bowl of hot water."

As she said it she laughed too, laughed until she coughed, and Rijin patted her back. Lü Shao, at the back, bowed his head, his shoulders shaking.

Jingfu stood there, his face still red, scratched his head, and laughed himself.

"In Tang there's an elegant name for this. It's called chu xugong—making an empty bow," he said. "The Master taught me. The Master said that when he was small in Lingzhou he let one go at table, and Uncle Duan from Lord Guo's camp slapped his thigh and laughed at him, and said men of letters call it making an empty bow: a trip for nothing—all sound and no goods."

Those seeing them off laughed again.

"That phrase I've kept forty-odd years and never once had occasion to use," said Jingfu. "I never thought that today, in front of everyone, I'd use it."

Laughing, he looked back once: the Kaiyuan Gate, the man in the grey Daoist robe under the gate passage, the little group seeing them off—Rijin, Mother Kang, the Shi brothers, Lü Shao. Then he turned, went to the side of Lingbao's cart, took hold of its shaft, and went west with the caravan.

Lingbao, sitting in the cart, could see nothing. He had heard the sound, heard the people behind laughing, and he laughed too.

"If Brother were here, he'd send him to drink a bowl of hot water, and eat a piece of ginger," he said to He Siniang.

"Brother isn't here."

"No, Brother isn't here," said Lingbao. "He is."
