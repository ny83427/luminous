Volume Five　Chang'an

Chapter 25　Weeping and Laughing

The old foundation of the main hall was dug out in the tenth month.

They had been repairing for most of a year, and all they had repaired were the things round the edges. The roofs of the side rooms were patched, the courtyard wall was built up, a row of sheds was put up in the back courtyard for the monk-soldiers to live in. The main hall had not been touched. That half-burned hall—its eastern pillars still stood, its western side had collapsed into a heap of brick and tile, charred wood and ash and earth, piled higher than a man. Sabrisho said the hall must be pulled down clean and rebuilt, not patched on the old frame. Narsai said that before it was pulled down, they must pray first.

They prayed three days. On the fourth, they began pulling down.

Twenty-four monk-soldiers, with several dozen people from the ward come to help, pulled it down over more than half a month. The charred timbers were carried out one by one, the bricks and tiles carried out basket by basket, the ash and earth hauled out cart by cart. Sargis could shoulder a beam by himself; he heaved it up onto his shoulder and walked off steady as you like, and the men beside him clicked their tongues. Ishaq stood to one side directing—to the left, to the right, slowly, slower still—and did not carry a single timber himself. Old Duan told him off, and Ishaq said: this flesh of mine can't carry timber, but it can carry your dinners.

When they had pulled it down to the bottom, the old foundation was laid bare.

The old foundation was built of stone, bluestone, block by block, very evenly laid. There were black marks of fire on the stones; several had cracked, and one had broken into three pieces. Babai squatted down and wiped the dust from one stone with his sleeve, and as he wiped, his hand stopped.

On the side of the stone characters were carved. They were very small and clogged with dust, and he picked them out bit by bit with his fingernail.

Twelfth year of Zhenguan.

Babai squatted there and for a long time did not get up.

That autumn the Son of Heaven issued a decree.

The decree said that in Lingwu and the four other commanderies the Luminous monasteries were to be re-established.

The decree was for Lingwu, for Shuofang; it said nothing of Chang'an. But after the decree came down, no one ever came to question the monastery in the Yining Ward again. Before, people had often come: the ward headman had come, petty clerks of the capital prefecture had come, once even two men of the Imperial Guard, who stood at the gate looking in a long while and asked who had given leave to repair this monastery. Official Pei had gone to speak for it several times; each time he spoke it was all right for a while, and after a while someone came again.

After the decree came down, no one came.

Yisi's purple robe was still locked in the cupboard. The Patriarchate's letter had not come back.

The laying of the foundation was set for the twenty-second of the eleventh month.

That day was overcast. From morning the sky was grey, grey and very low, as if it were pressing on the tops of the ward walls. The wind was not strong, but it was cold, cold enough to make people hunch their necks.

More people came than Yisi had expected.

Everyone from the monastery came. The seven teachers, the twenty-four monk-soldiers, Xu Jing, Old Duan. Official Pei came, not in his official robe but in a plain one, standing in the crowd. A dozen and more of the old soldiers who had been in charge on the day of the rice at Qujiang came, and Hou Siyuan and Cheng Chongyi came too. Many people came from the ward—some who had drunk the gruel, some who had worn those washed old clothes, some who had dug pits with him south of the city. The old man from south of the city came too, with his two sons, and stood at the courtyard gate, not coming further in, craning to look.

And there were some whom Yisi knew, and yet did not quite know.

They were people of the monastery from the old days.

In the Tianbao years, when the monastery was at its height, several hundred people would come on a Lord's Day. There were Persians, Sogdians, Tokharians, and Tang people too. Then came the war, the city fell, and they fled, died, scattered. In this last year and more, little by little, some had come back. Some had heard there was gruel at the monastery gate and came for gruel, and as they drank it recognised Yisi; some had heard it from elsewhere, heard that the monastery in the Yining Ward had people again, had a lamp again, and asked their way here.

Twenty-odd had come.

All were old. The youngest was past fifty. Some leaned on sticks, some had sons or grandsons holding them up, and one old woman had been brought pushed in a wheelbarrow.

They stood to one side of the courtyard, crowded together, saying little, their eyes all on the empty ground where the main hall had been.

On the empty ground, the old foundation had been cleared.

The cracked stones had been carried away, the broken stones carried away, and the rest were still where they had been. In the northeast corner of the old foundation a pit had been dug, and the bottom of the pit laid with a layer of rammed earth. Beside the pit lay a new stone.

The new stone had been brought from the Zhongnan Mountains, bluestone, the same as the stones of the old foundation. The mason had dressed it square and true, ground one face smooth, and on the smooth face carved a cross. The four ends of the cross were rounded, like four leaves.

The old stone, the one carved Twelfth year of Zhenguan, had not been carried away. It lay beside the pit, next to the new stone.

Yisi stood at the edge of the pit.

He wore his grey robe. The scar by his left eyebrow had turned white, and under the grey sky it looked whiter still.

Narsai prayed first. His voice in prayer was very hard, knocking out the words one by one. Then Sabrisho, then Haddad, then Babai. Ishaq prayed shortest; when he had finished he said "Mm" to himself, as if nodding to himself.

Yonan did not pray. He stood at the very back of the seven, his two hands folded before him.

Then Sargis and three other monk-soldiers bent down, lifted the new stone, carried it step by step to the edge of the pit, and slowly lowered it in.

The stone came down on the rammed earth with a thud, very dull.

Gabriel opened the scroll.

What he read was the Book of Ezra. He read a sentence in Syriac and expounded it in Tang. His Tang had improved a good deal that year, but he still liked to use hard words, and having used them, explained them himself.

"And when the builders laid the foundation of the temple of the LORD," Gabriel read, "they set the priests in their apparel with trumpets, and the Levites the sons of Asaph with cymbals, to praise the LORD, after the ordinance of David king of Israel."

There were no trumpets here, and no cymbals. The monk-soldiers stood in a row, leaning on their staves.

"And they sang together by course in praising and giving thanks unto the LORD; because he is good, for his mercy endureth for ever toward Israel."

A few people from the ward began to sing along. What they sang was the monastery's hymn, in Tang, handed down from Alopen's time, the words old and the tune old too. They did not sing together—some fast, some slow, some forgetting the words halfway and humming along with their mouths open.

Gabriel read on.

"And all the people shouted with a great shout, when they praised the LORD, because the foundation of the house of the LORD was laid."

He paused, and read again:

"But many of the priests and Levites and chief of the fathers, who were ancient men, that had seen the first house, when the foundation of this house was laid before their eyes, wept with a loud voice."

As he read this sentence, on the other side of the courtyard, among those twenty-odd old people, someone wept aloud.

The one who wept was an old man.

He was past seventy, very small, so badly bent that standing he was hardly taller than sitting. He was a Tokharian, and Yisi knew him. In the Tianbao years he had come every Lord's Day, and when he came he sat in the very front row, in the third seat from the left. His ears were bad, and in front he could hear.

Now he stood in the crowd, both hands over his face, weeping so that his shoulders heaved.

When he wept, those beside him wept too.

The old woman who had come in the wheelbarrow leaned out of it and stretched out her hand, as if to touch the new stone. She could not reach it, and her hand trembled in the air. The one pushing the barrow was her grandson, a boy of ten-odd, who did not know why she was weeping, and in alarm squatted down to ask her; she did not answer, only wept.

One, two, a whole swathe.

Those twenty-odd old people stood together and wept as one. Some wept aloud, some made no sound and only let the tears flow, the tears running down the wrinkles of their faces, running to their chins, turned cold in the wind.

On the other side of the courtyard, the people of the ward stood stunned.

They watched the old people weep and did not know what to do. Some of the young ones had only begun coming to the monastery to listen after the rice at Qujiang; they had never seen the old hall and did not know what it had been like. All they knew was that here there had been a heap of charred wood and a heap of brick and tile, and now it had been cleared, and a new stone had been set.

They were glad.

One young man, the one who had helped Yisi ladle gruel at the monastery gate, looked at the stone and broke into a grin. When he grinned, those beside him grinned too. Someone clapped. Hou Siyuan gave a whistle and got a slap on the back of the head from Cheng Chongyi, and Hou Siyuan, clutching his head, went on laughing.

The weeping and the laughter mingled together.

Gabriel was still reading.

He read more slowly. His voice was half drowned by the weeping and the laughter, and those standing at the back could not hear it well.

"And many shouted aloud for joy."

"So that the people could not discern the noise of the shout of joy from the noise of the weeping of the people: for the people shouted with a loud shout, and the noise was heard afar off."

He finished reading, closed the scroll, and expounded no further.

Yisi stood at the edge of the pit and listened.

He listened a while, and could not tell them apart. In the weeping there was laughter, and in the laughter weeping too. The old Tokharian, weeping, suddenly laughed once—probably at something he remembered—and when he had laughed went on weeping. The young man who ladled gruel, laughing, saw an old man beside him weeping, and his laughter stopped; his mouth was still open in a grin, but his eyes had gone red.

Old Duan stood a little way behind Yisi. He did not weep, and did not laugh; the scar on his face was drawn tight. After standing a while he raised his hand and wiped his face once with his sleeve.

Xu Jing, beside him, glanced at him.

"Windy," said Old Duan.

Xu Jing said: "There's no wind."

Old Duan paid him no attention.

When the old Tokharian had finished weeping, he came slowly over.

He came very slowly, step by step, up to the new stone, and stopped. He bent his head and looked a while at the cross, then turned his head and looked a while at the old stone beside it, at the characters on its side. He could not read Tang characters. He only looked.

Then he raised his head and looked towards the seven teachers.

He saw Yonan.

He looked a long time, looked until his eyes narrowed. Yonan stood there and did not move.

The old man came two steps forward, right up to Yonan, and turned up his face to look at that beardless, young face.

"Bishop," said the old man.

He said it in Tokharian. Yisi understood some of it.

Yonan bent his head and looked at him.

"The bishop has come back," said the old man.

Yonan said: "I'm not bishop any more."

He said it in Tang. The old man did not understand Tang, thought he had said something else, seized his hand, held it, and began to weep again. Yonan did not draw his hand away. His hands were an old man's hands, and the old man's hands were an old man's too; the two pairs of old hands clasped together, and you could not tell which was older.

Jingjing stood beside Yisi, watching all the while.

Yisi looked down at him. Jingjing's eyes were black, and under the grey sky so black they shone.

The old woman in the wheelbarrow had her grandson push her over too, afterwards.

She did not look at the stone, nor at Yonan. She looked at Jingjing.

She called Jingjing over to the barrow, took his hand, turned it over and looked at his palm, then raised her head and looked at his face. Her eyes had gone cloudy, and she had to bring them very close to see. She brought them close and looked a while.

"You're the one," said the old woman, "that Priest Yi carried home that winter."

Jingjing said: "Yes."

"You were this small then." The old woman measured with her two hands, smaller than a cat. "Your ma held you, in this hall, sitting at the very back. When you cried, your ma carried you out and walked under the locust tree, walked a while, and you'd stop crying."

Jingjing said nothing.

He knew whom she meant by his ma. That Sogdian woman, whose hands were very soft and very warm, like a cloth that had lain in the sun. The year he was six, she died.

He stood there, the old woman holding his hand. Yonan stood not far off, his back to them. Yonan's shoulders did not move at all.

"You've grown up," said the old woman.

"Mm."

"Grown up fine," said the old woman. "Like your ma."

Jingjing said: "Mm."

The old woman let go of his hand and had her grandson push the barrow away. Jingjing stood where he was and watched that wheelbarrow creak its way out of the courtyard gate.

He put his hand inside his coat and touched the knotted cord. He did not take it out, but there inside his coat turned one knot.

They dispersed very late.

When they had gone the courtyard was empty. On the ground was a mass of footprints, muddy, confused, one layer over another. The new stone lay in the pit, the old one beside it.

The sky grew darker.

Towards evening it began to snow.

The snow came from the west, first a little at a time, as if someone up in the sky were sifting flour, then heavier, flake by flake, falling very slowly, very lightly, without a sound.

Yisi stood alone by the old foundation.

He watched the snow fall on the new stone. Fall on the carved cross, on the four leaves of the cross. The snow fell on it, and at first melted, melted into drops of water, and soaked the stone, and bluestone when it is wet goes black. Then more fell than could melt, and it gathered, a thin layer, white.

On the old stone beside it the snow fell too. Fell on the characters Twelfth year of Zhenguan, filled them, and the characters could not be seen.

Jingjing came out of the side room with a broom in his hand. He went to the edge of the pit to sweep the snow off the stone.

Yisi said: "Let it fall."

Jingjing stopped. He looked at his father, looked at the two stones, put down the broom, and leaned it against a wooden post beside him.

He came and stood beside Yisi, side by side.

The two of them stood watching the snow come down. It fell on the new stone and on the old stone too, fell a layer, melted a layer, fell another layer. Over in the back courtyard, Yonan was drawing water at the well; the windlass creaked and creaked, creaked a while and stopped a while.

It grew dark.

Yisi did not go to sweep it.
