Volume Six　The Shuofang Army

Chapter 28　Ba'aluo

In the autumn of the first year of Shangyuan, a band of young men in white appeared at the monastery in the Yining Ward.

There were eleven of them. The eldest was twenty-four or five, the youngest seventeen. Some were Tang, some Sogdian, and one was the son a Uighur horse dealer had left behind in Chang'an, who spoke better Tang than Uighur. They had sewn the white clothes themselves, of hemp cloth, undyed, washed until it was stiff, so that it rustled when they walked.

Every day before light they went to the well behind the monastery, drew a bucket of well water, and poured it over their heads. When one was done, they drew another bucket and poured it over the next. When all were done, they knelt dripping by the well and chanted scripture until daybreak.

People of the ward who rose early to carry water often came upon them. The first few times they got a fright; later they grew used to it, and walked past them with their buckets, saying: "Washing again."

That year Jingjing was fourteen. He got up once to watch, stood by the well watching them pour bucket after bucket over their heads. Before he had finished watching, his own stomach had begun to ache.

They called themselves the Pure Ones.

Their leader was called Ba'aluo.

What his surname had been, few at the monastery remembered later. All they knew was that he was the son of a tanner in the West Market and had scraped hides with his father since he was small, and his hands smelled of saltpetre that would not wash off.

In the spring of the first year of Qianyuan, when Chang'an was in famine and the monastery gave out relief in the city, fifty to a group, sitting and waiting, he was one of those six thousand. His father had starved to death that winter, died at home, and he could not carry him, and left him in the house nine days.

Later, when the monastery gathered the dead, no one was willing to touch those rotted corpses. He went. He tied a cloth over his mouth and carried them one by one, carried them outside the city, carried them for more than a month. Someone counted and said he had carried more than seventy. He did not count himself.

In that month and more, Gabriel told stories every night beside the gruel shed. One night it was about a man called Paul. This man had made it his business to seize those who believed in the Lord, and seized many, and then on the road a light struck him down, and he was blind three days, and when he opened his eyes again he became the most diligent preacher under heaven.

The tanner's son heard it, and sat by the fire all night. The next day he went to Gabriel and said he wanted to be baptised and take that name.

Gabriel said: that name is too big.

He said: I'll grow into it slowly.

The name Paulos, spoken in Syriac, sounded to Tang ears like Ba'aluo. So he was called Ba'aluo.

Ba'aluo was the best believer in the monastery. No one person said so; everyone said so.

He could not read, and Babai taught him; he learned slowly, and when he had learned a character he wrote it a hundred times on the ground with a twig. His memory was good; a story Gabriel had told he could hear once and tell from beginning to end without missing a word. He had two robes, and in winter gave one to a blind man in the ward. He ate only half of every meal and wrapped up the other half to take to the children begging at the monastery gate. When anyone at the monastery was sick, he sat up with them. When he sat up he did not sleep, but knelt by the sick man's bed all night, and the next day, his knees bruised, went to scrape hides just the same.

Ishaq said: that boy, his heart is warm.

Sabrisho said: too warm.

Ishaq asked: what's wrong with too warm?

Sabrisho did not say. He spoke little, and one sentence of his was one sentence. This one he had said half of; the other half he kept back.

That year more and more followed Ba'aluo.

First three or four, all of whom had carried the dead with him. Then seven or eight. Then eleven. Together they poured cold water over themselves, together they chanted, together they ate no meat. Later, they would not eat at the same table as those who had not been baptised.

Later still, they said the elders of the monastery were not pure enough either.

They said the elders bowed when they met officials, bowed when they met Buddhist monks too, took money from benefactors and chanted prayers for the benefactors' safety in return. What difference was there, they said, between that and the people in the ward who worshipped the Kitchen God, or the fox spirits?

Ishaq heard it and was not offended; he took two dry flatbreads in his coat and went to find them, and pressed one on each. Ba'aluo took the bread, broke it in two, gave half to the man beside him, and laid the other half on the ground, saying: "Elder, today we are fasting."

Ishaq looked at that half flatbread on the ground, stood a while, bent and picked it up, blew off the dirt, and ate it himself.

In the ninth month, something happened.

There was an old believer at the monastery, past seventy, a Tang woman whose husband had been dead twenty years. On the stove in her house she had always kept a Kitchen God, of clay, the size of a palm, blackened by smoke. It had been there while her husband was alive, and when she was baptised she did not take it down. Not that she did not believe; she could not bear to. Her husband had made it.

The Pure Ones went to her house.

They did not abuse her. Ba'aluo knelt before her, held her hand, and told her how Moses came down from the mountain and saw the people worshipping the golden calf, and ground the golden calf to powder and strewed it upon the water and made them drink of it. He told it very well, told it until his own tears came down.

When he had finished, he stood up, went to the stove, picked up the Kitchen God, and threw it on the ground.

A thing of clay breaks at one throw.

The old woman did not weep aloud. She squatted down and picked up the pieces one by one, picked them up and held them in the palms of her hands. The Pure Ones left, and she was still squatting there picking them up.

It was Jingjing who told Yisi. Jingjing had gone that day to take her gruel, and had seen it.

Yisi called Ba'aluo to his room.

The room was as it always was. A low table, two mats, a shield, a sword and a mace hanging on the wall. On the cupboard stood three bowls: one Yisi's, one Jingjing's, and one with a chipped rim, holding a pinch of earth.

Ba'aluo stood at the door in his wet white clothes, his hair still dripping.

"Sit," said Yisi.

Ba'aluo did not sit. He said: "Master Yi wants to speak about the Kitchen God."

"Yes."

"The Kitchen God is an idol," said Ba'aluo. "It is written in the scripture: Thou shalt not make unto thee any graven image; thou shalt not bow down thyself to them."

"It is written in the scripture," said Yisi.

"Then where was I wrong?"

Yisi thought and said: "Her husband made it."

Ba'aluo looked at him as if he had not understood.

Yisi said again: "You can talk to her. Talk to her a hundred times, until she takes it down herself. You can't smash it for her."

Ba'aluo lowered his head. After a while he raised it again, his eyes very bright.

"Master Yi," he said, "Elijah on Mount Carmel slew the prophets of Baal, four hundred and fifty men. He didn't talk to them a hundred times."

"Who told you about Elijah?"

"The elder Narsai."

Yisi said nothing.

"Master Yi has killed men," said Ba'aluo. His voice was not loud, not hot; he said it word by word, as if asking something he had thought about a long time. "I've heard men back from the camp say Master Yi killed men at Changshan, and at Xiangji Temple too."

"I have."

"Did you kill them for the Lord?"

The room was very quiet. Outside the window someone was drawing water at the well, the windlass creaking and creaking.

Yisi looked at him, looked a long time.

"When I kill a man," he said, "I don't think of the Lord. If I thought of him, I couldn't do it."

Ba'aluo stood there, opened his mouth, and nothing came out.

"You pour cold water over yourselves—I leave that alone," said Yisi. "You fast, you won't eat at the same table as other people—I leave that alone too. You smash things in people's houses—that I don't leave alone. Tomorrow you go to the old woman's house and pay her for the Kitchen God."

"It's not worth anything."

"Then you can afford it," said Yisi.

Ba'aluo stood a while, made Yisi a bow, turned and went. As he crossed the courtyard he held his back very straight, and his white clothes rustled.

The next day he really did go and pay. The old woman would not take it.

That night Narsai came.

He stood at Yisi's door and did not come in. No lamp was lit in the courtyard, and his face was in shadow; only his high brow ridges could be seen.

"The Elijah story," he said. "I told it."

"I know."

"What I told was the words of the scripture," said Narsai. "I didn't add one word."

"I know," said Yisi.

Narsai stood a while longer. He seemed to want to say something more, but did not, and turned and went.

Narsai stood at the door a while, his lips moving, as if to ask something. But in the end he did not ask it aloud, and Yisi did not hear it.

On the twentieth of the ninth month, the fire temple in the Liquan Ward held its festival for the god.

The fire-worshippers in Chang'an were mostly Sogdian merchants. Their temples were in the Liquan Ward, the Buzheng Ward and a few other places, their gates shut on ordinary days; inside burned a basin of fire that never went out, day or night, with a man set apart to tend it, called the fire-keeper. Every year on the festival day the temple gates stood wide open, and the merchants slaughtered pigs and sheep, played the pipa, blew flutes, beat drums, drank and danced, and kept it up until midnight. The temple master would also, before everyone, take a knife and drive it into his own belly and out through his back; and when it had gone through, he pulled the knife out, spat a mouthful of water, and there was nothing on his belly at all. The people of the city all loved to go and watch, and when they had watched, said: the hu have fine conjuring tricks.

That afternoon Yisi was not at the monastery.

He was at Guo Ziyi's residence. That year Guo Ziyi was in the capital without command of troops, idle at home every day. Guo Ziyi idle looked much the same as Guo Ziyi at war: in the morning millet gruel, pickles, a steamed bun, and when he had eaten, a slow walk round the courtyard, looking at whatever he walked past. Yisi walked with him most of the day, and the two of them said hardly a word.

When the sun was past its height, Xu Jing came running in.

He was running so hard his head was all sweat, his right hand with two missing fingers flailing in the air, and it was a long while before he could get the words out:

"The Pure Ones… the Liquan Ward… someone's dead."

When Yisi got there, several hundred people were crowded round the gate of the fire temple.

In the crowd were hu and Tang, the ward headman, the ward police, and children watching the fun from their fathers' shoulders. Everyone was shouting, shouting every sort of thing. Yisi heard hu speech, heard Tang speech, heard murdering bald-pated hu, and heard let's see what they say this time.

He pushed his way in.

The temple courtyard was wet. There was water all over the ground, and soaking in it were overturned buckets, carrying poles, trampled fruit, a red dancing boot. In the middle of the courtyard stood a stone platform, on the platform a bronze basin, and in the bronze basin a fire was burning.

The fire had not gone out.

Afterwards Yisi got it straight. That afternoon the Pure Ones, each carrying two buckets of well water on a pole, had walked from the Yining Ward to the Liquan Ward, the better part of an hour. All the way they had sung psalms, in Syriac, not quite in tune. They reached the temple gate just as the temple master was driving the knife into his belly; the crowd were all watching the knife, and no one watched them. So they walked straight into the courtyard with their buckets.

On the road Ba'aluo had told them: Elijah on Mount Carmel had men pour twelve barrels of water on his own altar, and then called upon God, and fire came down from heaven and licked up all the water. Today we will pour water on their altar, and see whether their god sends down fire.

What the scripture says is that Elijah poured the water on his own altar. But that day on the road, no one remembered.

The first bucket went on, half the fire in the bronze basin went out, and a great cloud of white smoke rose up. The people in the courtyard were stunned a moment, and then it erupted.

The fire-keeper was an old man.

He was past sixty, thin, short, his beard white, in an old white robe, a white cloth over his mouth. He had tended the fire in this temple forty years and never left it a single day. When the first bucket went on he was standing nearest, and was drenched. When the second of the Pure Ones charged up with his buckets, he did not dodge, but spread his arms and stood before the fire basin.

The youngest of the Pure Ones, seventeen, a butcher's son, had a carrying pole in his hands. He said afterwards he had only meant to push the old man aside.

The pole struck the old man on the temple.

The old man pitched forward against the edge of the stone platform. His sleeve touched the half of the fire that had not gone out, and caught. The people of the temple threw themselves on him, dragged him away, and beat out the fire on his sleeve with their hands.

When they dragged him away, the old man was already not moving.

The rest was all confusion.

The hu threw themselves on the Pure Ones and beat them. The Pure Ones did not hit back. People in the ward spoke of this afterwards: those white-clad followers of the hu teaching, when they were beaten, not one of them hit back; they knelt in the water clutching their heads and still singing. The ward headman and the ward police at last pulled them apart, and all eleven Pure Ones were tied up, tied in a string, squatting by the temple gate.

Yisi went up to them.

Ba'aluo's face was all blood, one eye swollen shut. With the other eye he saw Yisi, and grinned, and his teeth were bloody too.

"Master Yi," he said. "The fire didn't go out."

Yisi did not answer him.

The youngest boy squatted at the very end. His trousers were wet—whether with water or something else, there was no telling. His two hands were tied before him, his eyes fixed on his hands, and he was saying over and over: "I didn't mean to hit his head. I didn't mean to hit his head."

Ba'aluo turned his head and said to him: "You did nothing wrong."

The boy raised his head and looked at Ba'aluo, and then at Yisi.

Yisi turned round and walked to the middle of the courtyard.

The old man lay at the foot of the stone platform, covered with a white cloth. One corner of the cloth was soaked and stuck to his face.

Yisi went over and squatted down. He put out his hand to lift the soaked corner and straighten it for the old man.

"Don't touch him!"

A hu shouted behind him. He shouted in Sogdian, and Yisi understood. The man rushed up and knocked his hand away.

"The dead mustn't be touched," the man said. "You don't understand. You don't understand anything."

Yisi's hand stopped in mid-air.

He remembered: the fire-worshippers had their own rules; the dead were unclean, and only men set apart for it could touch them, and having touched them, must wash many times over. He had known it in Balkh. He had forgotten.

He drew his hand back.

He knelt down on the wet ground, three paces from the old man. The water on the ground soaked through his knees at once.

The people in the courtyard all watched him. Someone cursed. Someone threw a piece of fruit peel at him; it hit his shoulder and rolled into the water. He did not move.

He knelt a long time, knelt until it was dark. The fire in the bronze basin burned before him all the while. In front of him, the people of the temple fed the basin twice more.

Three days later a memorial was sent into the palace.

The man who submitted it was a censor; Yisi did not know him. The memorial said: The hu monk Yisi, having received the favour of the purple, knows no gratitude, but has let his followers enter a temple with weapons and beat to death the old hu who kept it, so that the capital is shaken. The hu teaching was never a law of the Middle Kingdom; if today they dare to kill a man of the fire temple, what will they not dare tomorrow? It is requested that the purple robe granted to Yisi be taken back and all the titles he holds be stripped from him together; that the Daqin monastery in the Yining Ward be at once abolished and destroyed, and its monks ordered back to their native places.

Appended to the memorial was a complaint from monks of the Ximing Monastery, saying that for half a year these white-clad followers of the hu teaching had repeatedly reviled the monks outside their gate, calling the Buddha an idol and the monks slaves of an idol.

When the news reached the monastery, the seven teachers argued all night in the hall.

Haddad said they must go and find people—find Official Pei, find Lord Guo, find everyone they could find. Sabrisho said finding anyone was no use; it was our people who killed him. Ishaq said: even so, we must find them. Babai sat in a corner checking the scrolls one by one, as if the monastery were to be sealed tomorrow. Gabriel said not a word. He had baptised Ba'aluo, and the name had come from his story.

Narsai said nothing either. He sat furthest from the lamp.

Yonan asked Yisi: what does Guo Ziyi say?

Yisi said he had not yet gone to ask Guo Ziyi.

What he did not say was that he did not mean to ask him. That year Guo Ziyi was idle at home, and the men at court watching him outnumbered the men watching the rebels. Let Guo Ziyi say one word for a hu monk, and in other men's mouths it would not be one word.

On the morning of the fourth day, a minor clerk in a blue robe came to the monastery gate with a document.

The document was very short. Tomorrow at the hour of chen, to attend at the Ministry of Rites in the Department of State Affairs, to be questioned.

Yisi took the document, read it once, folded it, and put it inside his coat.

That night he ate with Jingjing. On the table a bowl of broth, a dish of flatbread, a dish of ginger. Neither of them spoke. When they had eaten, Jingjing went to wash the bowls, and Yisi sat at the table a while, then stood up and went to the wall.

He unfastened the mace from his belt and hung it back on its peg on the wall.
