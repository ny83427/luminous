Luminous

Appendix 4　The Stele, IV: The People of the Stele

Besides Yisi there are many people on the stele: from Alopen, who came to Chang'an in the Zhenguan years, to Jingjing who composed the text and Lü Xiuyan who wrote it out; from Taizong to Dezong, seven emperors, to Fang Xuanling, Gao Lishi and Guo Ziyi; and the names of some seventy monks on the sides. Many of the characters in this book come from that list. This appendix sorts them by station, giving for each the lines of the inscription, the main facts of the life, and the scholarly discussion, at more or less length as the sources allow. For Yisi, see Appendix 3.

1. Figures of the Luminous Religion

1.1 Alopen (阿羅本, Aluoben)

On the stele: "In the kingdom of Daqin there was a man of high virtue named Alopen. Reading the omens of the azure clouds, he carried the true scriptures; watching the winds and the pitch-pipes, he hastened through hardship and danger. In the ninth year of Zhenguan he arrived in Chang'an." "Alopen, a man of great virtue from the kingdom of Daqin, has brought scriptures and images from afar and presented them at the capital." "And honoured Alopen as Great Lord of the Law, Guardian of the State."

Main facts

  • Arrived in Chang'an in the ninth year of Zhenguan (635): the first Christian missionary to China for whom there is a clear record.
  • Taizong sent Fang Xuanling to receive him in the outskirts, had his scriptures translated in the library and his Way inquired into in the palace (for the rank of his reception, see Appendix 5).
  • In the seventh month of the twelfth year of Zhenguan (638) Taizong issued an edict to build a monastery in the Yining Ward of Chang'an and ordain twenty-one monks.
  • Under Gaozong (649–683) he was honoured as "Great Lord of the Law, Guardian of the State," and Luminous monasteries were set up in the prefectures.
  • Date of death unknown.

Sources

Besides the stele, there are two independent records:

  1. The Tang Huiyao, chapter 49, gives the edict of 638 and calls him "the Persian monk Alopen," unlike the stele's "man of great virtue from Daqin." Antonino Forte holds that the Tang Huiyao preserves the edict as it was, and that the stele has altered it to "Daqin," the name used after the fourth year of Tianbao (745).
  2. The colophon to the Dunhuang manuscript Zunjing (Book of the Honoured, P.3847, acquired by Pelliot): it records that Alopen came to China in the ninth year of Zhenguan and memorialised the throne "in his own tongue," which Fang Xuanling and Wei Zheng had translated and presented; it also says that Jingjing later translated thirty scriptures.

The name

The original form of "Aluoben" has never been settled. The main reconstructions are:

  • Rabban (Syriac "our teacher," an honorific for monks and teachers); those who hold this view think "Aluoben" is a title, not a personal name;
  • Abraham;
  • others, such as Ruben or Yahballaha.

English works usually write Alopen or Olopen, a relic of early Wade–Giles-style spelling.

Discussion

  • Did Alopen lead a mission? The stele mentions him alone, but the ordination of twenty-one monks in 638 shows that by then there was a community of some size, most of whom were perhaps Persian and Central Asian clergy who came with him or followed him.
  • What was his "own tongue"? The Handbook of Christianity in China, edited by the Belgian scholar Nicolas Standaert, suggests Sogdian rather than Persian, since Sogdian was the lingua franca of Central Asia at the time.
  • Deeg calls Alopen a "legendary" figure, reminding readers that the stele's portrait of him is something constructed. When the stele says he came "reading the omens of the azure clouds" and "watching the winds and the pitch-pipes," it uses the trope of envoys from the Western Regions who came when the wind was right, from the Records of the Ten Continents attributed to Dongfang Shuo: a literary commonplace, not an itinerary.
  • The Dunhuang texts Xuting mishisuo jing (the Jesus-Messiah Sutra) and Yishen lun (Discourse on the One God) were formerly held to be translations from Alopen's time (c. 635–650), but in recent years some scholars have questioned their date and authenticity.

1.2 Luohan (羅含)

On the stele: "Then there were the chief monk Luohan and the Great Virtue Jilie, both of noble lineage from the land of metal, eminent monks beyond the world, who together drew taut the hidden net again and bound up the cord that had broken." "An edict summoned the monk Luohan, the monk Pulun and others, seventeen in all, with the Great Virtue Jihe, to perform rites of merit in the Xingqing Palace."

Main facts

  • He appears twice on the stele: from the end of Xiantian (712–713) to the beginning of Kaiyuan, rescuing the Luminous Religion under attack together with Jilie; and in the third year of Tianbao (744), leading the clergy in rites of merit in the Xingqing Palace with Jihe.
  • Called "chief monk," he was probably the head of the Luminous church in Chang'an at the time.
  • The two appearances are some thirty years apart; if they are the same man, he spent a long time in China.

Disputes over his name and identity

  • Some scholars reconstruct "Luohan" as Abraham.
  • Earlier scholars (such as Haneda Tōru) read the "Aluohan" of the epitaph Aluohan qiuming, found at Luoyang in the late Qing (its subject died in 710 and styled himself "great chieftain of Persia" and "commissioner for pacifying the foreign peoples of Fulin"), as Abraham, connected him with Luohan, and even held him to be a Luminous believer.
  • In 1996 Antonino Forte published "On the So-called Abraham from Persia: A Case of Mistaken Identity," arguing forcefully that Aluohan was not Abraham and must not be confused with the Luminous Luohan, and that Aluohan's original name was more likely Iranian. This view is now widely accepted.

1.3 Jilie (及烈)

On the stele: "the Great Virtue Jilie" (see above).

Main facts

  • Together with Luohan he is called "of noble lineage from the land of metal, an eminent monk beyond the world"—a high monk of aristocratic Western birth.
  • The transmitted histories contain two records generally taken to refer to the same man:
  • Second year of Kaiyuan (714): the palace censor Liu Ze memorialised against Zhou Qingli, Commissioner for Maritime Trade in Lingnan, who with "the Persian monk Jilie" and others had been making strange and ingenious devices to present to the emperor (New Book of Tang, biography of Liu Ze; Cefu Yuangui);
  • Twentieth year of Kaiyuan (732): the king of Persia sent the chieftain Pannami with "the monk of great virtue Jilie" to court (Cefu Yuangui, ch. 975); the court gave the chieftain a military post and the monk a purple robe and silk, and sent them home. If this record is right, Jilie received the purple robe before Yisi did.
  • So Jilie was active in Lingnan (Guangzhou), connected with the sea trade, and also came to court as a member of a Persian embassy—a man who moved between religion, trade and diplomacy.
  • Reconstructions of his original name include Gabriel and Gīwargīs (George); none is settled.

1.4 Pulun (普論)

On the stele: "An edict summoned the monk Luohan, the monk Pulun and others, seventeen in all…"

Main facts: one of the Luminous monks who performed rites of merit in the Xingqing Palace in the third year of Tianbao (744). Nothing more is known.

1.5 Jihe (佶和)

On the stele: "In the third year, there was a monk of Daqin named Jihe, who looked to the stars and turned toward our civilisation, and gazed at the sun and came to pay homage to the Most High."

Main facts

  • Came from "Daqin" in the third year of Tianbao (744) and was called "Great Virtue"; Xuanzong ordered Luohan, Pulun and others to perform rites of merit with him in the Xingqing Palace.
  • "Looked to the stars and turned toward our civilisation, gazed at the sun and came to pay homage" is a stock phrase for foreigners submitting to the empire, and cannot be taken to mean that he really came from Byzantium (Daqin); he was more likely a senior cleric from Persia or Central Asia.
  • Reconstructions of his name include Gīwargīs (George) and Gabriel. Some scholars suggest he was an archbishop sent by the patriarch of the Church of the East, connected with the already established "province of China" (Beth Ṣīnāyē).

1.6 Jingjing (景淨)

On the stele: "Composed by Jingjing, monk of the Daqin monastery." In Syriac: "Adam, priest and chorepiscopus, and papash of Sinistan."

Main facts

  • Author of the inscription; his church name was Adam (Ādām).
  • The Syriac term pāpaš is rare; literally close to "father, pope," it is generally taken to mean that he was head of the province of China, that is, its metropolitan. The Church of the East had already established a metropolitan province of China under the patriarch Sliba-zkha (714–728).
  • He was a master of Chinese; the inscription mixes parallel and plain prose and draws freely on Confucian, Daoist and Buddhist allusion—the highest level of Chinese writing in the Tang Luminous Religion.
  • The colophon of the Zunjing says Jingjing "translated the above thirty works," that is, thirty of the scriptures listed in the Dunhuang Zunjing were his translations. Scholars differ on whether the Zhixuan anle jing (Sutra of Aiming at the Mystery and Peaceful Joy), the Xuanyuan zhiben jing and others are by him.
  • Collaboration with Buddhism: according to the Tang monk Yuanzhao's Zhenyuan xinding shijiao mulu (Catalogue of Buddhist Teachings Newly Fixed in the Zhenyuan Era), chapter 17, around the second year of Zhenyuan (786) Jingjing worked with the Buddhist monk Prajñā, from Kapiśa in northern India (which the Tang sometimes loosely called Jibin), on a translation in seven chapters of the Mahāyāna Sutra on the Six Pāramitās, made from a "Hu" text. Dezong judged that since one of them knew no Sanskrit and the other knew neither the Hu language nor Chinese, the translation could not be relied on, and issued an edict that "Jingjing should transmit the teaching of Mishihe, and the sons of Śākya should spread the Buddhist sutras," requiring each religion to keep to its own; the translation was discarded.
  • A 2025 article in the Journal of Central Asian History discusses why Jingjing translated a Buddhist sutra in 788, explaining his collaboration with Prajñā from the Tokharistan background.

Relation to Yisi: whether he was Yisi's son is disputed; see Appendix 3, section 1.

1.7 Ningshu (寧恕), that is, Hnanisho

On the stele: "At that time the Lord of the Law, the monk Ningshu, oversaw the Luminous congregations of the East." In Syriac: "In the days of the Father of fathers, Mar Hnanisho, Catholicos and Patriarch."

Main facts

  • He is the patriarch of the Church of the East Ḥnanīšōʿ II (r. 773–780), seated at Baghdad (or Seleucia-Ctesiphon).
  • The stele was raised early in 781, when Hnanisho had already died and his successor Timothy I (r. 780–823) had taken office. The church in Chang'an did not yet know, so the stele still names Hnanisho. This detail shows how slowly news travelled between Chang'an and Baghdad—about a year.
  • Timothy I mentions in his letters sending bishops to the "Turks" and to "China," an important source for the eastward spread of the Church of the East.

1.8 Yeli (業利)

On the stele: "Assisting in supervising: the monk Yeli, Acting Chief Minister of the Court of Imperial Sacrifices, granted the purple robe, head of the monastery."

Main facts

  • His titles—"Acting Chief Minister of the Court of Imperial Sacrifices" (that minister was of the third grade, in charge of ritual music and sacrifices; "acting" marks it as honorary), "granted the purple robe," "head of the monastery"—place him second only to Yisi.
  • He was "assisting in supervising," that is, he helped oversee the raising of the stele.
  • In the Syriac there is a "Gabriel, priest and archdeacon, head of the church of Kumdan and Sarag (Chang'an and Luoyang)"; "head of the church" corresponds to "head of the monastery," and Yeli is generally identified with this Gabriel.
  • That Yeli too received the purple robe shows that Yisi was not alone: a group of leading Luminous clergy received honours from the Tang court.

1.9 Xingtong (行通)

On the stele: "Supervising the erection of the stele: the monk Xingtong."

Main facts: the monk in charge of raising the stele. In the Syriac he corresponds to "Sabranisho (Ṣabrānīšōʿ), priest" (so matched in the 2016 edition of Eccles and Lieu).

1.10 Mar Sargis (Mār Sargīs)

In Syriac: "Mar Sargis, priest and chorepiscopus."

Main facts: a chorepiscopus like Yisi; his Chinese name cannot be identified. Sargis (Sergius) is a common saint's name in the Church of the East.

1.11 Lingbao (靈寶)

Chinese name on the side of the stele: "the monk Lingbao." In Syriac: "Adam, deacon, son of Yazdbozid the chorepiscopus."

Main facts: Yisi's son, a deacon. He comes first in the list of monks on the side.

1.12 Milis (Mīlīs)

In Syriac: "son of the late priest Milis… from Balkh, a city of Tokharistan."

Main facts: Yisi's father, a priest of Balkh, already dead when the stele was raised. The Chinese text does not mention him. Whether he ever came to China the stele does not say; the phrasing "the late priest Milis, from Balkh" suggests rather that he stayed in Balkh.

1.13 Yaolun (曜輪)

Side of the stele: the Taishō reads "□□輪"; by comparison with the SERICA edition, "the Great Virtue Yaolun." In Syriac: "Yohannan (Yōḥannān, John), bishop."

Main facts: the only person called "bishop" in the list on the side. He was bishop of the church in Chang'an to which Yisi belonged, and must have ranked above him; yet the Chinese inscription does not mention him at all, presumably because it was written with Yisi as its patron.

1.14 The elder Yejumo (老宿耶俱摩)

Side of the stele: "the elder Yejumo."

Main facts: the only person in the list called not "monk" but laosu, "venerable elder," an old monk of great age and virtue. "Yejumo" is a transliteration, usually reconstructed as Yaʿqōḇ (Jacob).

2. The Tang emperors

The inscription runs from Taizong to Dezong: seven emperors in all (not counting Zhongzong and Ruizong; Wu Zetian appears only indirectly, through the "Shengli years"). Each has a passage of prose and a passage of verse. This is the skeleton of the whole stele: "the Way is not spread abroad without a sage; the sage is not great without the Way."

Emperor Reign On the stele Facts and discussion
Taizong, the Cultured 627–649 Receives Alopen, issues the edict to build a monastery, has his portrait painted on its wall The edict in the Tang Huiyao confirms the building of the monastery and ordination of monks; the reception in the outskirts is known only from the stele. See Appendix 5
Gaozong, the Great 649–683 Sets up Luminous monasteries in the prefectures, honours Alopen as Great Lord of the Law, Guardian of the State Under Gaozong the Persian king Peroz lived in exile in Chang'an, and in the second year of Yifeng (677) asked to found a Persian monastery in the Liquan Ward (whether it was Luminous or Zoroastrian is disputed). How many monasteries "in every prefecture" there really were cannot be established
(Wu Zetian) 690–705 "In the Shengli years the sons of Śākya used their strength and raised their voices in Eastern Zhou" In the Shengli years (698–700) Wu Zetian favoured Buddhism, and Buddhists in Luoyang attacked the Luminous Religion. The stele, tactfully, names the reign period and not the ruler
(between Ruizong and Xuanzong) 712–713 "At the end of Xiantian, lesser scholars laughed aloud and slandered us in Western Hao" Xiantian was the reign period of the handover from Ruizong to Xuanzong; the "lesser scholars" are usually taken to be the Daoists
Xuanzong, Perfect in the Way 712–756 Sends five princes to the monastery; sends portraits of the five sage emperors; gives a hundred bolts of silk; in the third year of Tianbao orders Luohan and others to perform rites with Jihe in the Xingqing Palace; writes the monastery's name-board himself In the fourth year of Tianbao (745) an edict renamed the Persian monasteries of the two capitals Daqin monasteries (Tang Huiyao)—the origin of the name "Daqin" on the stele, though the stele does not mention this edict
Suzong, Cultured and Bright 756–762 Rebuilds Luminous monasteries in Lingwu and four other commanderies; sends Yisi to follow Guo Ziyi Took the throne at Lingwu in the seventh month of 756; the main phase of the war against An Lushan and Shi Siming fell in his reign
Daizong, Cultured and Martial 762–779 At the time of the Nativity each year, sends incense and dishes from his table "The time of the Nativity" may be Daizong's birthday or Christmas; both readings stand
The Emperor of Jianzhong, Sage, Divine, Cultured and Martial (Dezong) 779–805 The inscription closes with his praises In Dezong's first years there was talk of reforming the clergy; in the Zhenyuan years he rejected the Buddhist sutra translated by Jingjing and Prajñā, laying down that "Jingjing should transmit the teaching of Mishihe, and the sons of Śākya should spread the Buddhist sutras"

Some additions

Taizong's portrait: the stele says Taizong ordered the officials to copy his portrait onto the monastery wall. Under Xuanzong portraits of the "five sages" were also sent to be set up in the monastery. To enshrine the emperor's portrait in a foreign religion's monastery was on the one hand a mark of imperial favour toward that religion, and on the other made the monastery a place for displaying imperial power. For the Luminous Religion it was an important guarantee of legal standing in the Tang.

"Lingwu and four other commanderies": which five, the stele does not say. Lingwu was where Suzong took the throne; the others were perhaps within the Shuofang army's territory, or key places in the war against the rebels.

3. Court officials and the calligrapher

3.1 Fang Xuanling (579–648)

On the stele: "The emperor sent his chief minister, Duke Fang Xuanling, with the full honour guard to the western outskirts, to receive him as a guest and bring him in."

Main facts

  • The great chief minister of Taizong's reign; from the third year of Zhenguan (629) he was Left Vice-Director of the Department of State Affairs and directed the government for twenty years. He and Du Ruhui were spoken of together as "Fang and Du."
  • The stele records that he was sent to receive Alopen in the western outskirts; the colophon of the Zunjing says that Alopen memorialised "in his own tongue," and that Fang Xuanling and Wei Zheng had the memorial translated and presented.
  • Fang Xuanling was not connected only with the Luminous Religion. In the third year of Zhenguan, when the Indian monk Prabhākaramitra opened a translation bureau at the Daxingshan Monastery, Taizong also ordered Fang Xuanling and Du Zhenglun to "assist in checking"; when Xuanzang returned in the nineteenth year of Zhenguan, Fang Xuanling, as regent of the western capital, sent officials to meet him. As chief minister, Fang Xuanling was evidently often charged with receiving foreign religious figures and with translation affairs.

3.2 Wei Zheng (580–643)

On the stele: not mentioned. He appears only in the colophon of the Zunjing: "Fang Xuanling and Wei Zheng had the memorial translated and presented."

Main facts: a famous minister of the Zhenguan reign, known for his blunt remonstrance; in 635 he was Director of the Chancellery. That he and Fang Xuanling "had the memorial translated and presented" for Alopen probably means they formally presented the translated memorial to the emperor, not that they themselves knew foreign languages.

3.3 The Prince of Ning and four other princes

On the stele: "The Emperor Xuanzong, Perfect in the Way, ordered the Prince of Ning and four other princes to go in person to the house of blessing and set up an altar there."

Main facts

  • Usually taken to mean Xuanzong's brothers (including a cousin): Li Xian, Prince of Ning; Li Hui (Chengyi), Prince of Shen; Li Fan, Prince of Qi; Li Ye, Prince of Xue; and Li Shouli, Prince of Bin. Before his accession Xuanzong had lived with his brothers in the "Residence of the Five Princes" in the Longqing Ward, later enlarged into the Xingqing Palace; the histories make much of the affection between Xuanzong and his brothers.
  • "Ning" is the Prince of Ning, named by his fief.
  • Note: The Prince of Shen died in the twelfth year of Kaiyuan (724) and the Prince of Qi in the fourteenth (726). If all five came, it must have been before 724, very likely soon after the slander of the Luminous Religion at the end of Xiantian—which fits neatly with the preceding line, "the beam of the Law, bent for a while, rose higher; the stone of the Way, tilted for a time, stood upright again."
  • There is one more piece of circumstantial evidence for a link between Li Xian, Prince of Ning, and the Luminous Religion: the monk Chongyi who treated his illness (see Appendix 3) is thought by some scholars to have been a Luminous monk.

3.4 Gao Lishi (684–762)

On the stele: "At the beginning of Tianbao he ordered the General-in-Chief Gao Lishi to take the portraits of the five sage emperors to the monastery and set them up there, and gave a hundred bolts of silk."

Main facts: Xuanzong's most trusted eunuch, who in the Tianbao years rose to be General-in-Chief of the Cavalry. That he personally escorted the portraits of the five former emperors to the Luminous monastery shows how high the honour was.

3.5 Guo Ziyi (697–781)

On the stele: "When Lord Guo Ziyi, President of the Secretariat and Prince of Fenyang, first took command of the army in Shuofang, Suzong had Yisi go with him on campaign."

Main facts

  • The foremost figure in putting down the An Lushan rebellion. Military governor of Shuofang in 755; recovered the two capitals as deputy commander-in-chief in 757; made President of the Secretariat in 758, Prince of Fenyang in 762.
  • When the stele was raised (early 781) Guo Ziyi was still alive; he died in the sixth month of that year. Jingjing calls him "Lord Guo" and lists his current titles—great deference to a living elder statesman.
  • For his relation to Yisi see Appendix 3. This is the only line of the inscription that mentions him, but nearly all of Yisi's honours flowed from that relationship.

3.6 Lü Xiuyan (呂秀巖)

On the stele: "Written by Lü Xiuyan, Gentleman for Court Discussion, formerly Administrator of Granaries in Taizhou."

Main facts

  • He wrote out the inscription in ink for carving, and also the title on the head. "Gentleman for Court Discussion" was a civil prestige rank of the sixth grade, upper class; "formerly Administrator of Granaries in Taizhou" means he had been an assistant official in the prefecture of Taizhou (around today's Linhai in Zhejiang). Not a high post; he was probably a man of letters in Chang'an known for his calligraphy.
  • The regular script of the inscription is upright and neat, in the manner of Ouyang Xun and Yu Shinan, and has long been admired by calligraphers.
  • Since the Qing some have claimed that Lü Xiuyan was Lü Dongbin (Lü Yan, styled Dongbin), the Daoist immortal, and all sorts of legends about Lü Dongbin and the Luminous Religion have grown from this. The two do not match in period or station, and scholars reject it.

4. The monks on the sides and foot of the stele

The two sides of the stele bear the names of some seventy monks, each with Chinese name and Syriac church name side by side. This list is the most direct material we have on the Luminous community of the Tang.

4.2 What the list shows

  1. The Chinese names mostly have no literal relation to the church names. The Chinese names use auspicious characters—treasure, blessing, virtue, harmony, light, reverence—and are religious names made afresh in the Chinese manner, as Buddhist monks took dharma names. A few, like "Fulin" (拂菻, the Eastern Roman Empire), have a geographical flavour.
  2. The church names are mostly biblical, with some purely Iranian names (Mahdād-Gušnasp, for instance, is a Persian name with a Zoroastrian colour). This shows that most of the community were of Iranian origin. Deeg concludes from this that the Tang Luminous Religion was essentially a religion of Iranian expatriates, with very few Chinese believers.
  3. The community had a complete hierarchy: bishop, chorepiscopus, archdeacon, priest, deacon and monk were all present, showing that the church in Chang'an was quite well organised.
  4. Numbers: about seventy. Twenty-one monks were ordained in 638; a hundred and forty years later there were some seventy Luminous clergy in and around Chang'an—growth, but nothing to compare with Buddhism.

4.3 Other names worth noting

  • Jingtong: there is a "monk Jingtong" on the side of the stele. The Dunhuang Zunjing also lists a "Dharma King Jingtong" among its "Dharma Kings," but that is a saint's name, and whether the two are connected cannot be known.
  • Xuanlan: there is a "monk Xuanlan" on the side, a name with a Daoist flavour (Laozi: "cleanse the dark mirror," 滌除玄覽).
