Appendix 3　The Stele, III: Yisi

The person described at greatest length on the stele is the great benefactor Yisi. Yisi's native city and his father's name in this book, his following Guo Ziyi against the rebels, his giving away of his wealth, his repairing of monasteries, his feeding of the hungry and healing of the sick—all come from the two hundred or so characters on the stele and the few lines of Syriac at its foot. His elder brother, his family affairs, and most of what happens to him in the army are invented. This appendix takes the stele's text about Yisi apart line by line, to see how scholars have read it, what can be pinned down, and what is only conjecture. Wherever the inference is this book's author's own, it is marked "Note."

1. General: who Yisi was, and how we know

1.1 There is only one source

Yisi appears in none of the transmitted Chinese histories—neither of the two Books of Tang, nor the Zizhi Tongjian, the Cefu Yuangui or the Tang Huiyao. Everything we know of him comes from two pieces of writing:

  1. The last section of the Chinese preface (from "The great benefactor, Grand Master of the Golden Seal and Purple Ribbon" to "to make his noble deeds known"), composed by Jingjing and written out by Lü Xiuyan;
  2. The Syriac inscription at the foot of the stele, cut in Estrangela script, which states plainly that it was he who raised the stele.

The two are different in kind. The Chinese is a eulogy written for Tang readers, dense with allusion and careful in its parallelism. The Syriac is a record of identity written for the church itself, plain in language, but giving his native city, his father's name and his church rank, none of which the Chinese gives. Any study of Yisi must read the two together.

1.2 What the Syriac says

The Syriac at the foot of the stele (summarised from Moule, Saeki, Pelliot–Forte and Eccles & Lieu's 2016 SERICA edition) says in substance:

In the year one thousand and ninety-two of the Greeks (autumn 780 to autumn 781), Mar Yazdbozid, priest and chorepiscopus of Kumdan (Chang'an), the royal city, son of the late priest Milis, from Balkh, a city of Tokharistan, set up this tablet of stone. On it is written the way of our Saviour's salvation, and the preaching of our fathers to the kings of the Chinese.

It goes on to list:

Adam, deacon, son of Yazdbozid the chorepiscopus. Mar Sargis, priest and chorepiscopus. Sabranisho, priest. Gabriel, priest and archdeacon, head of the church of Kumdan and of Sarag (Chang'an and Luoyang).

From these lines five things can be established:

Item Content Remarks
Church name Yazdbōzīd A Middle Persian name, generally explained as "redeemed by God (yazd)." The Chinese name Yisi (伊斯) is probably a rendering of its first syllable, Yazd- (or Izd-).
Native city Balkh in Tokharistan, in northern Afghanistan today The "city of Rajagriha" of the Chinese text
Father Milis, a priest, already dead when the stele was raised So Yisi came from a clerical family, at least the second generation in holy orders
Rank when the stele was raised Priest and chorepiscopus of Chang'an A chorepiscopus was a senior cleric who helped a bishop govern a district, ranking below a bishop
Son Adam the deacon, named "Lingbao" in Chinese on the side of the stele Clergy of the Church of the East could marry, so Yisi had a son

One more thing is very clear from the Syriac, and the Chinese says not a word of it: Yisi himself raised the stele. The Chinese says only "we wish to carve a great stele, to make his noble deeds known," with no subject; the Syriac says plainly that it was Yazdbozid who set it up. This is crucial for understanding the nature of the whole eulogy (see section 15).

1.3 Yisi and Jingjing

This is an old question in the scholarship, and the easiest to get muddled. There are two "Adams" on the stele:

  • Jingjing: the author of the inscription, called in Syriac "Adam, priest, chorepiscopus, and papash of Sinistan," the highest-ranking cleric in the Chinese province at the time, generally thought equivalent to a metropolitan;
  • Lingbao: called in Syriac "Adam, deacon, son of Yazdbozid the chorepiscopus"—Yisi's son, only a deacon.

The two share a name but differ greatly in rank, and most scholars think they are not the same man. Some, however—Max Deeg, for example, in a 2020 article in Entangled Religions—call Yisi outright the father of Jingjing, as does Kawaguchi Kazuhiko of the Japanese society for the study of the Luminous Religion. This view rests on their sharing a name, living at the same time in the same city, and on the fact that Jingjing praises Yisi on the stele at such length, as if a son were writing his father's life. Those who disagree point out that the Syriac explicitly lists "Adam, son of Yisi" as a deacon and matches him to "Lingbao" in the Chinese on the side of the stele, while Jingjing's rank is far above his supposed father's, which is hard to credit.

Note: Neither reading can be ruled out entirely. But as far as the correspondences on the stele itself go, "Yisi's son = Lingbao (Adam the deacon)" is certain, while "Jingjing = Yisi's son" is a conjecture.

2. The passage in full

大施主金紫光祿大夫、同朔方節度副使、試殿中監、賜紫袈裟僧伊斯，和而好惠，聞道勤行。遠自王舍之城，聿來中夏。術高三代，藝博十全。始効節於丹庭，乃策名於王帳。中書令汾陽郡王郭公子儀，初總戎於朔方也，肅宗俾之從邁。雖見親於臥內，不自異於行間。為公爪牙，作軍耳目。能散祿賜，不積於家。獻臨恩之頗黎，布辭憩之金罽。或仍其舊寺，或重廣法堂，崇飾廊宇，如翬斯飛。更効景門，依仁施利。每歲集四寺僧徒，虔事精供，備諸五旬。餧者來而飯之，寒者來而衣之，病者療而起之，死者葬而安之。清節達娑，未聞斯美；白衣景士，今見其人。願刻洪碑，以揚休烈。

(For an English translation of the whole, see Appendix 2, section 8.) Below, the passage is divided into thirteen sections by sense and discussed line by line.

3. Section one: a string of titles

The great benefactor, Grand Master of the Golden Seal and Purple Ribbon, Associate Deputy Military Governor of Shuofang, Acting Director of the Palace Administration, granted the purple robe, the monk Yisi

These five titles belong to five different systems—the church, prestige rank, commissioned office, substantive office, and religious honour. They are the key to Yisi's social standing.

3.1 Great benefactor (大施主)

Shizhu, "donor," is a Buddhist term for the patron who supports a monastery with gifts. To be called "great benefactor" shows that Yisi was the chief funder of this stele and of the Luminous monastery in Chang'an. Jingjing borrows a ready-made Buddhist title, as the stele does throughout ("monk," "monastery," "Lord of the Law," "merit" are all the same).

3.2 Grand Master of the Golden Seal and Purple Ribbon (金紫光祿大夫)

A Tang civil prestige rank, first-class third grade, so named because its holder could wear purple and carry a gold fish-pouch. Prestige ranks marked grade only and carried no duties. The third grade was already high in the Tang: a chief minister's substantive office (such as President of the Secretariat) was itself only third grade.

Scholars generally note that during the An Lushan rebellion the court, to raise money for the army and reward merit, handed out prestige ranks and "blank warrants" in huge numbers, and titles lost much of their value. The Zizhi Tongjian records that in the Zhide years "all who answered the call to arms wore gold and purple," and quotes the gibe that "a general's warrant would buy just one drunk." So high as this title is, it does not necessarily show that Yisi had real power at court; it is more likely a wartime reward.

3.3 Associate Deputy Military Governor of Shuofang (同朔方節度副使)

A deputy military governor held a real post in the governor's headquarters, second only to the governor himself. The character tong (同) can be understood two ways:

  • as "equivalent to deputy governor," that is, given the same treatment as a regular deputy, like the terms "equal to regular" or "supernumerary"—a non-quota appointment;
  • as "concurrently," meaning that besides his prestige rank Yisi also served as deputy governor of Shuofang.

Most scholars lean to the first, and regard it as an honorary addition. Either way, this title ties Yisi directly to Guo Ziyi's Shuofang army, and echoes the later line "when Lord Guo Ziyi first took command of the army in Shuofang."

3.4 Acting Director of the Palace Administration (試殿中監)

The Director of the Palace Administration headed the Palace Administration, second-class third grade, which looked after the emperor's personal needs. Shi (試) means "acting," not a full appointment. After the rebellion there were very many such acting offices, mostly empty titles with no duties attached.

Note: The Palace Administration oversaw six services—Food, Medicine, Clothing, Horses, Lodging and Carriages—and the Medicine Service was specifically charged with "compounding the emperor's medicines and taking his pulse." To make a foreign monk skilled in medicine "Acting Director of the Palace Administration" was probably not a random choice, but connected to his being a physician. This fits the later line "his art surpassed the three generations; his skill was wholly sure."

3.5 Granted the purple robe (賜紫袈裟)

Purple was the highest colour of dress in the Tang. The custom of the emperor granting purple robes to monks began in the first year of Zaichu under Wu Zetian (690), when they were given to nine men including Falang and Xue Huaiyi, and thereafter became the court's standard honour for eminent monks. That Yisi, a Luminous priest, received a purple robe—and another Luminous monk on the stele, Yeli, likewise—shows that in the eyes of the Tang court Luminous clergy were fitted into the honours system for Buddhist clergy.

3.6 The monk Yisi (僧伊斯)

The people of the Tang called the clergy of the Luminous, Zoroastrian and Manichaean religions all seng, "monk," and the Luminous clergy called themselves so too. Yisi is a Chinese name, taken, as said above, from the first syllable of the Syriac name Yazdbōzīd.

Summary

In his 2013 article Deeg calls Yisi "a belligerent priest," and points out that Yisi held at once a religious identity and high political office. This string of titles is the evidence: a foreign cleric bearing a third-grade prestige rank, a third-grade substantive office, a deputy governorship and the purple robe all at once. There were plenty of men of foreign descent in high military posts in the Tang—Geshu Han, Li Guangbi, Pugu Huai'en—but ones who were also clergy were very rare.

4. Section two: character

He was gentle and loved to do kindness; having heard the Way, he practised it diligently.

  • Gentle and loved to do kindness: mild of temper and glad to give. "Kindness" (惠) is the keynote of the whole passage; the giving away of salary, the repairing of monasteries and the feeding of the hungry that follow all unfold from it.
  • Having heard the Way, he practised it diligently: from Laozi 41, "when the superior scholar hears the Way, he practises it diligently." With this allusion Jingjing hints that Yisi belongs among the "superior scholars," who can carry out the Way when they hear it. Notably, the next line of the same chapter of the Laozi is "when the lesser scholar hears the Way, he laughs aloud"—and earlier in the inscription, describing those who slandered the Luminous Religion at the end of Xiantian, the stele uses exactly "lesser scholars laughed aloud." Jingjing has set the two halves of one chapter on the side of praise and of blame within the same text, plainly on purpose.

5. Section three: native city and coming to China

From the distant city of Rajagriha he came to the Middle Kingdom.

5.1 Where is "the city of Rajagriha"?

Rājagṛha was the capital of Magadha in India, where the Buddha often stayed and preached. Early Western translators, Legge among them, took it that way and believed Yisi came from India.

The problem is solved by the Syriac: the inscription says plainly that Yisi came from "Balkh, a city of Tokharistan." And Xuanzang's Record of the Western Regions, in its first chapter, describing the kingdom of Balkh, says that the people there "all call it Little Rajagriha." Balkh had many Buddhist monasteries and was known as Little Rajagriha. On this basis Pelliot and others established that the stele's "city of Rajagriha" is Balkh, the Balkh of northern Afghanistan today. The identification is no longer disputed.

Jingjing wrote not "Balkh" but "the city of Rajagriha" to lend Yisi's homeland the lustre of a Buddhist holy place, and so that Tang readers would know at a glance that it lay out toward the Buddhist lands of the west. It is of a piece with the stele's general borrowing of Buddhist vocabulary.

5.2 Religion and politics in Balkh

Balkh was the centre of ancient Bactria, and in the seventh and eighth centuries a place where many religions lived side by side: there was the famous Buddhist monastery Nava Saṅghārāma (the "New Monastery"), there were Zoroastrians, and there was a bishopric of the Church of the East. The Church of the East had set up sees in Khorasan and Tokharistan very early, and the Christian community of Balkh survived the Arab conquest.

Politically, Balkh came by degrees under the control of the Umayyad Arab armies after 705; the Abbasid revolution of 747 was launched from Khorasan. In this period the states of Tokharistan repeatedly sent envoys to the Tang asking for help against the Arabs.

An article published in the Journal of Central Asian History in 2025 (on why Jingjing translated a Buddhist sutra in 788) approaches the subject from the Tokharistan background, and discusses Yisi in a section on "fellow countrymen in a strange land." It regards Yisi as both a religious figure and a holder of high political office in China, and observes that Deeg's article, though right to call Yisi "a belligerent priest," does not set out the larger political background of his emigration.

Note: When and why Yisi left Balkh, the stele does not say. Two possible backgrounds suggest themselves: first, the eastward movement of Central Asian Christians under the pressure of Arab conquest and conversion; second, that Yisi may have come to the Tang with an embassy from Tokharistan or elsewhere in Central Asia and stayed in Chang'an on the strength of his medicine. Both are only possibilities, without direct evidence.

5.3 "He came to the Middle Kingdom"

Yu (聿) is an opening particle common in the Book of Songs (as in "Mian": 聿來胥宇, "he came to survey the site"), with no meaning of its own. The line imitates the Songs in describing his arrival in China.

5.4 Yisi's age

The stele gives no information about his age. Reckoning from what is likely: he followed Guo Ziyi into the army in the reign of Suzong (756–762), already with a reputation as a physician; when the stele was raised in 781 he was chorepiscopus of Chang'an, and had a son who was already a deacon. So he was probably in his fifties or sixties at the time, and born somewhere around the 720s or 730s. This is a broad estimate; scholars have no firm view.

6. Section four: his skills

His art surpassed the three generations; his skill was broad and wholly sure.

These two lines are the key to Yisi's identity, and they have long been read two ways.

Reading one: medical terms (the main view)

  • Three generations: from the Book of Rites ("Quli," part 2): "If a physician's family has not practised for three generations, do not take his medicine." "His art surpassed the three generations" means his medicine was better than that of a third-generation physician—and hints that he came from a medical family.
  • Wholly sure (十全, "ten complete"): from the Rites of Zhou ("Officers of Heaven: the Chief Physician"): "At the end of the year his medical work is examined to fix his pay: ten cures out of ten is the highest grade; one failure in ten is next…" That is, physicians were assessed at year's end, and those who cured every patient were rated highest. "His skill was wholly sure" means he was a physician of the highest grade.

Both allusions come from medical contexts, and together they point very clearly. Early commentators such as Saeki and Moule had already noticed this; Tang Xiaofeng's 2024 article goes further, setting Yisi beside the Yuan-dynasty figures Isa and Mar Sargis as representatives of the "Luminous physicians" of the Tang and Yuan.

Reading two: skills in general

Some take "three generations" to mean the three dynasties of Xia, Shang and Zhou—that is, "his art surpassed high antiquity"—and "ten complete" to mean versatile, accomplished in everything. This reading makes Yisi an all-round talent (perhaps versed in astronomy, crafts and war as well).

Supporting evidence for the medical reading

  1. The medical tradition of the Church of the East: the Church of the East was renowned for medicine as early as the Sasanian period; the mainstay of the medical centre at Gundeshapur were physicians of that church, and the court-physician dynasties of the eighth- and ninth-century Abbasids (such as the Bukhtishuʿ family) were also its members. It was normal for its clergy to practise medicine.
  2. Other examples of Luminous believers practising medicine in the Tang: the biography of Li Xian, the "Yielding Emperor," in the Old Book of Tang records that a monk Chongyi treated Xuanzong's eldest brother, Li Xian, Prince of Ning, for illness; when the prince improved, Xuanzong was delighted and gave Chongyi a scarlet robe and a fish-pouch. Whether Chongyi was a Luminous monk is nowhere stated; many scholars guess so from his name and the naming habits of Luminous monks (compare "Chongjing" and "Chongde" on the side of the stele), but it is not settled.
  3. The echo in his titles: as noted above, the Palace Administration of which he was "Acting Director" oversaw the Medicine Service.
  4. The echo later in the passage: "trusted even in the bedchamber" shows he could go into Guo Ziyi's bedroom, which fits a physician exactly; and "the sick were healed and raised up" shows he practised medicine himself.

Note: Taken together, that Yisi was a physician is close to a consensus among scholars. His duties as "eyes and ears" and "claws and teeth" are not at odds with being a physician: in Tang armies the physician went in and out with the commander, had his ear, and often handled confidential errands as well.

7. Section five: the start of his career

He first served loyally at the vermilion court, and then enrolled his name in the royal tent.

  • Served loyally (效節): gave loyal service.
  • The vermilion court (丹庭): the red-painted steps and courtyard before the palace, standing for the court. "He first served loyally at the vermilion court" means he first served at court.
  • Enrolled his name (策名): from the Zuozhuan (Duke Xi, 23rd year), "to inscribe one's name and present one's pledge; to be of two minds is then a crime"—writing one's name on the bamboo slips as a sign of committing oneself as a subject.
  • The royal tent (王帳): the tent of a king.

The "first… then…" of these lines gives an order: first at court, then in the camp.

Whose is "the royal tent"?

This is one of the difficulties of the passage. There are three possibilities:

  1. Suzong's tent: in 756 Suzong took the throne at Lingwu, and the court was then in the army; "the royal tent" could mean the tent of the emperor in the field;
  2. The headquarters of the Prince of Guangping (later Daizong): when the two capitals were recovered in 757, Li Chu, Prince of Guangping, was commander-in-chief of all armies and Guo Ziyi his deputy, so "the royal tent" might mean service under this princely commander;
  3. Guo Ziyi's own headquarters: Guo Ziyi was made Prince of Fenyang in 762, and when Jingjing wrote (781) he called him a prince, so "the royal tent" could be a retrospective title.

Note: The next line says "Suzong had Yisi go with him on campaign"—Suzong sent him to Guo Ziyi. If "the royal tent" were Guo Ziyi's, the two lines would repeat each other; if it is read as "first served at court (under Xuanzong?), then was formally appointed under Suzong or in the commander-in-chief's headquarters, and was then sent by Suzong to follow Guo Ziyi," the sequence is clearer. The first and second readings suit the flow better, but the inscription is parallel prose and should not be pressed too hard.

When did he "serve at the vermilion court"?

If Yisi served at court before Suzong, that would be under Xuanzong. This fits his being a physician: there were foreign physicians at Xuanzong's court (such as Chongyi, above), and Yisi might well have served the court with his medicine. But this is only conjecture; the stele does not say what he did at court.

8. Section six: on campaign with Guo Ziyi

When Lord Guo Ziyi, President of the Secretariat and Prince of Fenyang, first took command of the army in Shuofang, Suzong had Yisi go with him on campaign.

8.1 Guo Ziyi's titles

  • President of the Secretariat: conferred on Guo Ziyi in the first year of Qianyuan (758);
  • Prince of Fenyang: conferred in the first year of Baoying (762).

Both are the titles Guo Ziyi held in 781, when the inscription was written, not those he held when Yisi followed him on campaign. Guo Ziyi died in the sixth month of the second year of Jianzhong (781), only a few months after the stele was raised, so he was still alive. Jingjing's solemn mention of him owes something to a wish to borrow the elder statesman's prestige.

8.2 The problem of "first took command in Shuofang"

There is a plain contradiction of dates here:

  • Guo Ziyi was first made military governor of Shuofang in the eleventh month of the fourteenth year of Tianbao (755), after An Lushan rose in revolt, and he was appointed by Xuanzong;
  • Suzong only took the throne at Lingwu in the seventh month of the first year of Zhide (756).

So when Guo Ziyi "first took command in Shuofang," Suzong was not yet emperor and could not have "had Yisi go with him." Scholars explain this in several ways:

  1. It means after the eighth month of 756: Guo Ziyi and Li Guangbi brought fifty thousand men from Hebei to Lingwu, and Suzong made Guo Ziyi Minister of War, Administrator of Lingwu and joint chief minister, still in command of the Shuofang army. From Suzong's point of view, this was Guo Ziyi's first command in Shuofang under the "new court." This is the usual reading.
  2. The inscription is loosely worded: Jingjing was writing more than twenty years later, and called the whole period of Guo Ziyi's command of the Shuofang army his "first command."
  3. "First" means simply "at that time": there is no need to pin it to his first appointment.

Note: The first explanation is the most reasonable. In the autumn of 756 Suzong was setting up a makeshift court at Lingwu, desperately short of capable men; it was a time to use whoever could be used. That a foreign monk who knew the languages of the Hu peoples, was skilled in medicine and familiar with the Western Regions should be sent by Suzong to the commander of the Shuofang army makes good sense.

8.3 Why Suzong sent Yisi

The stele says "had Yisi go with him" (俾之從邁): Yisi was assigned by the emperor, not recruited by Guo Ziyi himself. This is worth pondering.

Note: It was common for Tang emperors to place men they trusted beside their great generals (the eunuch army inspectors were the institutional form of it). Yisi, sent by Suzong, both "trusted even in the bedchamber" and "the army's eyes and ears," may have played a more complicated role beside Guo Ziyi than a mere army physician: Guo Ziyi's confidant, and possibly also one of Suzong's channels for knowing what went on at the front. This is only one possible reading; the inscription itself is all praise and would never let such a thing show.

8.4 Foreign troops in the rebellion

Another background worth noting: in the campaign of 757 to recover Chang'an and Luoyang, the Tang army included Uyghur troops, troops from the states of the Western Regions, and Arab troops. Some researchers suggest that Yisi, who knew Persian and the languages of Central Asia, may have served in Guo Ziyi's army as liaison and interpreter with these foreign forces. It is a reasonable guess, but neither the stele nor the histories record it directly.

9. Section seven: his role in the army

Though he was trusted even in the bedchamber, he did not set himself apart in the ranks. He served the Duke as claws and teeth, and the army as eyes and ears.

9.1 "Trusted even in the bedchamber"

Wonei (臥內) is the bedroom. The phrase has a source: the Shiji biography of the Lord of Xinling says that Lady Ru, being the King of Wei's favourite, "went in and out of the king's bedchamber," and so was able to steal the army tally. To go in and out of a commander's bedroom was to enjoy the closest trust.

As already said, this fits a physician: a doctor treating the commander would naturally go into his bedroom.

9.2 "He did not set himself apart in the ranks"

Hangjian (行間) means the ranks of the army, among the common soldiers. Though trusted by the commander, among the soldiers he did not show himself different from the rest. It is a stock phrase of praise for modesty and sharing hardship with the troops.

9.3 "Claws and teeth"

"Claws and teeth" was a term of praise in ancient Chinese, meaning an able military officer. It comes from the Book of Songs ("Qifu"): "Minister of War, we are the king's claws and teeth." To be "the Duke's claws and teeth" is to be Guo Ziyi's capable right hand.

9.4 "Eyes and ears"

"Eyes and ears" means spying, scouting, watching—intelligence work. To be "the army's eyes and ears" is to serve the whole army in that way, which might include scouting the enemy, learning the state of the army, and carrying messages.

9.5 Did Yisi fight in person?

This is disputed.

  • Deeg's "belligerent priest": in his 2013 article Deeg calls Yisi "a belligerent priest," holding that he really did take part in the war against the rebels as a soldier. Popular works such as the English Wikipedia say on this basis that Yisi "held a military command himself."
  • The cautious reading: "claws and teeth" and "eyes and ears" may be rhetoric, and need not mean charging the enemy. Yisi was more probably a staff officer, physician or liaison.

Deeg makes a still more important point: this passage is in essence self-praise. The American scholar Thomas Carlson, reviewing the volume in which the article appeared, sums up that Deeg reads the inscription as the self-advertisement of "a belligerent priest." Since Yisi paid for the stele, its portrait of him can hardly avoid embellishment.

Note: The two readings can be reconciled. "Claws and teeth" was the common old term for a trusted military officer; the deputy governors in Guo Ziyi's headquarters held military posts, and Yisi's title of "deputy military governor of Shuofang" shows he belonged, nominally, to the military system. Whether he went into battle himself, the stele does not say, and it cannot be proven.

10. Section eight: giving away his wealth

He could give away his salary and rewards, and did not hoard them at home. He offered up the glassware he received by grace, and spread out the gold-threaded carpets given him on retiring.

10.1 "He could give away his salary and rewards, and did not hoard them at home"

He gave his salary and rewards to others, and kept no savings at home. This answers to the earlier description of the Luminous rule, "they amass no goods, and show that they have given all away": the first half of the preface says that Luminous believers "amass no goods," and the second half gives Yisi as a living example.

10.2 "He offered up the glassware he received by grace, and spread out the gold-threaded carpets given him on retiring"

These are the hardest lines in the passage.

  • 頗黎 (poli): glass, or crystal; a transliteration of Sanskrit sphaṭika. Glassware in the Tang mostly came from the west and was very precious.
  • 金罽 (jinji): carpets or woollens woven with gold thread. Ji is woollen cloth.
  • 臨恩 (lin'en) and 辭憩 (ciqi): literally "facing grace" and "taking leave to rest"; what exactly they refer to has long been disputed.

The main explanations:

Explanation "glassware … by grace" "carpets … on retiring" Main proponents
1. Imperial gifts glassware received when favoured by the emperor carpets received on retiring Most early English translations, Moule, Saeki
2. Gifts to the monastery he gave glassware to the monastery he laid carpets in the monastery Most Chinese commentaries combine this sense
3. Names of objects "lin'en" and "ciqi" are the names of objects or places the same A minority view, with little evidence

On the first explanation, the lines mean: Yisi gave to the church the glassware and gold-threaded carpets the emperor had given him on various occasions. The two verbs, "offered" and "spread," one meaning to present and the other to lay out, both have the monastery in view.

Note: If ciqi means "retiring," it implies that Yisi later left military service and returned to the church in Chang'an. This fits his being "chorepiscopus of Chang'an" in the Syriac when the stele was raised, and gives a thread for his later life: army → retirement → church office in Chang'an → paying for the repair of monasteries and the raising of the stele.

11. Section nine: repairing monasteries

Some old monasteries he restored as they were; some halls of the Law he enlarged anew. He raised and adorned their colonnades and roofs, until they were like a pheasant in flight.

  • Some old monasteries he restored: some he kept and repaired;
  • Some halls of the Law he enlarged anew: some halls of worship he rebuilt larger;
  • He raised and adorned their colonnades and roofs: he made the galleries and buildings higher and finer;
  • Like a pheasant in flight: from the Book of Songs ("Si Gan"), "like a bird spreading its wings, like a pheasant in flight," a stock phrase praising buildings with upswept eaves.

Background

During the rebellion Chang'an and Luoyang each fell twice, and many monasteries were damaged. Earlier the inscription says Suzong "rebuilt Luminous monasteries in Lingwu and four other commanderies," and "rebuilt" implies that the existing monasteries had been damaged or abandoned. Yisi's repairs were presumably part of rebuilding the network of Luminous monasteries after the war.

Which monasteries he repaired, the stele does not say.

12. Section ten: the fifty days of provision each year

He served the Luminous gate still more, and gave benefits in accordance with benevolence. Every year he gathered the monks of four monasteries, and served them reverently and provided for them carefully for fifty days.

12.1 "He served the Luminous gate still more, and gave benefits in accordance with benevolence"

He devoted himself still further to the Luminous Religion, and gave to others out of kindness.

12.2 Which four monasteries?

This is a concrete and important question. Possible candidates:

  • the Daqin monastery in the Yining Ward of Chang'an (built in 638, the first Luminous monastery in China);
  • the Persian monastery in the Xiushan Ward of Luoyang (recorded in the New Records of the Two Capitals, Song Minqiu's Gazetteer of Henan, and elsewhere; a Luminous pillar unearthed in Luoyang in 2006 also mentions a Daqin monastery in Luoyang);
  • Luminous monasteries at Lingwu and elsewhere (one of the "five commanderies" where Suzong "rebuilt" them);
  • the Daqin monastery at Zhouzhi (in today's Zhouzhi county, one of the proposed sites where the stele was found).

Scholars have reached no conclusion. Lin Wushu, in his study of the numbers of Luminous monasteries on the stele, inclines to think phrases like "monasteries filled a hundred cities" are rhetorical exaggeration, and that the real number was small. On that view "four monasteries" is a relatively concrete figure, probably referring to Luminous monasteries in and around Chang'an.

Note: That he could gather the monks of four monasteries in one place every year shows that Yisi was at the organisational and financial heart of the church in Chang'an, as befits his rank of chorepiscopus.

12.3 "Served them reverently and provided for them carefully for fifty days"

To serve them devoutly and provide for them with care; wuxun (五旬) is fifty days. There are two main views of what the fifty days were:

  1. The great fast: the great fast of the Church of the East (Ṣawmā Rabbā) lasted fifty days and was the most important fast of the year. To provide for the monks during it fits the church's rites.
  2. Pentecost season: the fifty days from Easter to Pentecost, a season of celebration.

Both have their reasons; the first suits "served reverently and provided" (provision in a fast) a little better, the second matches "fifty days" more directly.

13. Section eleven: four good works

The hungry came and were fed; the cold came and were clothed; the sick were healed and raised up; the dead were buried and laid to rest.

These four lines are the most quoted in the whole inscription.

13.1 The biblical background

The four works closely match the words of Jesus in Matthew 25:35–36:

For I was an hungred, and ye gave me meat: I was thirsty, and ye gave me drink: I was a stranger, and ye took me in: Naked, and ye clothed me: I was sick, and ye visited me: I was in prison, and ye came unto me.

"Burying the dead" is not on Matthew's list, but in the Jewish and Christian tradition it had long counted as a good work (Tobit in the Apocrypha, for instance, is famous for burying the dead), and when the Western church later drew up the "seven corporal works of mercy," the seventh was burying the dead.

13.2 The Chinese background

At the same time, the parallel phrasing of these four lines is wholly in the habit of Tang Chinese. Tang Buddhist monasteries ran "fields of compassion" (beitian yangbing fang), which took in the poor, the sick, orphans and the old—charitable institutions run jointly by the government and the Buddhist monasteries. A Tang reader would naturally think of the Buddhist idea of fields of merit on reading these lines.

13.3 The connection with Yisi as physician

"The sick were healed and raised up" is the only one of the four that involves a special skill; "healed and raised up" means cured so that they could get up from their beds. This confirms the earlier "his art surpassed the three generations; his skill was wholly sure": Yisi very probably treated the sick himself.

Note: What is good about these four lines is their plainness: one line, one deed, no ornament. In an inscription so ornate and parallel, it is a rare plain passage.

14. Section twelve: the highest praise

Among the pure and upright tarsa, such goodness was never heard of; among the white-robed Luminous scholars, now we see the man.

14.1 Tarsa

Dasuo (達娑) transliterates Middle Iranian tarsā, originally "one who fears (God)," used in Middle Persian and Sogdian for Christians. Eccles and Lieu's SERICA edition explains it as the Central Asian term for Christians and notes that the inscription pairs it with "Luminous scholars."

"The pure and upright tarsa" are Christians (or monks) of clean and steadfast life.

14.2 White-robed Luminous scholars

How to understand "white-robed" is the key to this couplet. There are two views:

  • White vestments: the clergy of the Church of the East wore white, in contrast to the "black robes" of Buddhist monks. On this view, "white-robed Luminous scholars" are Luminous clergy in white—exactly Yisi's station.
  • Laymen: in Buddhist usage "white-robed" means a layman living at home (in contrast to the "black-robed" who have left home). On this reading, "a white-robed Luminous scholar" is a lay Luminous believer who does what even those who have left home cannot.

Note: Yisi was a priest and chorepiscopus, not a layman, so the first reading is the more fitting. Yet Jingjing may have meant the double sense of "white-robed": Luminous clergy did not leave home as Buddhist monks did, and could marry and have children, so in Tang eyes they were close to "living at home." It is worth adding that Tang Manichaean clergy were known for "white robes and white caps" (the Song monk Zhipan's Fozu tongji says of the Manichaeans that "their followers wear white robes and white caps"); if the Luminous clergy also favoured white, Tang people might not have told them apart.

14.3 The couplet as a whole

The first line, "among the pure and upright tarsa, such goodness was never heard of," says that even among clean-living Christians such virtue was unheard of; the second, "among the white-robed Luminous scholars, now we see the man," says that among the white-robed Luminous clergy, today such a man is seen. The first line lowers, the second raises: it is the highest praise in the passage.

15. Section thirteen: the wish to raise a stele

We wish to carve a great stele, to make his noble deeds known.

Hongbei (洪碑) is a great stele; xiulie (休烈) are fine achievements. The line means: we wish to carve this great stele, to proclaim his fine achievements.

15.1 Whose wish?

The Chinese has no subject. From the sense, it can be taken to mean that the congregation wished to raise a stele for Yisi; but the Syriac says plainly that Yisi raised the stele himself. Set side by side, they produce an interesting situation: Yisi paid for the stele, and the inscription lavishly praises him in the third person.

15.2 The nature of the inscription

This leads to a new understanding of the whole stele. Deeg holds that this passage is Yisi's self-praise and should be understood in the political setting of 780–781. Dezong came to the throne in 779, and in his first years there was talk at court of reforming the Buddhist and Daoist clergy (Peng Yan, a vice-director in the Ministry of Justice, for example, memorialised that monks and Daoists should be made to pay taxes or return to lay life). An article in Studies in Church History, "Silk Road Christians and the Translation of Culture in Tang China," cites Deeg's view that some passages of the inscription may answer the questions raised in these memorials, though the emperor's response could not yet be foreseen.

In other words, while the stele proclaims that the Luminous Religion had been generously treated by every emperor for a hundred and forty years, it also reminds the new emperor that Luminous clergy had worked to put down the An Lushan rebellion, and that Yisi was living proof of it. So Yisi's long string of titles and deeds are not only a personal glory, but the "political capital" of the whole Luminous community under the new reign.

Note: This reading turns the inscription from a purely religious memorial into a document with a political purpose. It does not deny the facts the inscription records, but it reminds the reader that the inscription is the party's own account, inevitably selective and embellished, and that Yisi's image has been carefully shaped.

16. Summary: how much of Yisi's life can be established

Putting the analysis above together, the outline of Yisi's life is roughly as follows. The left column is what the stele states or what follows directly from it; the right column is reasonable conjecture, to be treated with caution.

Stage Stated on the stele, or directly inferable Reasonable conjecture (no direct evidence)
Origins A man of Balkh; his father Milis was a priest; his own name Yazdbōzīd From a medical family ("surpassed the three generations")
Coming to China Came from Balkh to China Probably under Xuanzong; either moving east because of the Arab conquest, or arriving with a Central Asian embassy
Early years First served at court ("served loyally at the vermilion court") Served the palace as a physician
In the army Sent by Suzong to follow Guo Ziyi; Guo Ziyi's confidant, serving as "claws and teeth" and "eyes and ears" From around autumn 756; perhaps also physician, liaison and interpreter
Titles Grand Master of the Golden Seal and Purple Ribbon, Associate Deputy Military Governor of Shuofang, Acting Director of the Palace Administration, granted the purple robe Mostly honorary wartime rewards, not necessarily with real power
Retirement Received gifts on "retiring" Left military service after the rebellion was put down
Later years Chorepiscopus of Chang'an; gave away his wealth to repair monasteries; every year provided for the monks of four monasteries for fifty days; fed the hungry, clothed the cold, healed the sick, buried the dead The financial and organisational heart of the Luminous church in Chang'an
The stele Paid for and raised the stele in 781; had a son, Lingbao (Adam the deacon) Jingjing may also have been his son (disputed)
End Unknown Unknown

Some overall judgements

  1. Yisi was a real person. The native city, father's name, church rank and son's name in the Syriac are all very specific and do not look invented.
  2. Yisi was very probably a physician. Five clues point the same way: "three generations," "wholly sure," "the bedchamber," "Palace Administration," and "healed and raised up."
  3. Yisi's military role may be exaggerated. "Claws and teeth" and "eyes and ears" are stock phrases of praise; his actual duties may have been closer to those of a staff officer, physician or liaison.
  4. Yisi's titles should be discounted. Titles were handed out freely during the rebellion, and the words "associate" and "acting" mark them as honorary.
  5. The inscription is Yisi's account of himself. He paid for the stele and it praises him; the passage should be seen as a narrative shaped by the man himself, not an impartial third-party record.
  6. Yisi represents one way the Luminous Religion survived in the Tang: attaching itself to the court and the military elite, trading medicine and talent for protection, and repaying the church with wealth. The family of Li Su studied by Rong Xinjiang (Persians who entered the Directorate of Astronomy through their skill in astronomy and calendar-making) is another example of the same pattern.
