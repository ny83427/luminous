Volume Seven　A-Lian

Chapter 44　Hnanisho

The name Ningshu was a Tang translation.

His own name was Syriac, and sounded something like Ḥnanishoʿ, meaning "the mercy of Jesus." The monastery in Chang'an, translating his name, took the sound of its latter part and wrote it 寧恕, Ningshu. It took the sound, but the characters were Tang characters. 寧恕: rather forgive.

He himself never knew this. To the day of his death he never saw a single Tang character.

In the spring of the twelfth year of Dali, the letter from Chang'an reached Baghdad.

Baghdad was a new city, built by the Caliph al-Mansur; it took four years to build, and was built in a circle. Its walls were round, three of them, one inside another; in the middle of the city were the Caliph's palace and the mosque; on four sides four gates, and four great streets running from the gates straight to the middle, straight as a pulled cord. Outside the city, along the Tigris, markets, workshops, wharves and houses grew up patch by patch, until they were larger than the city itself.

The Patriarchate was in a compound on the east bank of the river, moved there only the year before last. The compound was new, the walls new, even the well newly dug, its water tasting of earth. The people of the Patriarchate had been there two years, and still some went out to buy things and took the wrong road, and came back only after a great circuit outside the walls.

The Patriarch Hnanisho had taken the wrong road too. He was past seventy that year, his eyes bad, his legs bad. When he had first moved there, he once went out alone to the river, walked half the day, and came to the door of a mosque, from which he was very courteously led back.

After that, he seldom went out.

The letter was read to him by a young secretary.

Letters from Chang'an went first to the secretary as a rule; the secretary opened them, made a copy, and then read them to the Patriarch. This secretary was twenty or so, wrote Syriac well and read letters clearly, but he had never travelled far; where Chang'an was he had seen only on a map, an ink dot at the far eastern edge of the parchment.

The letter was written by Yisi. The letters were big and clumsy, only a few words to a line, and in several places scraped, the parchment worn so thin the light came through. The secretary read very slowly, and at some words had to hold it up to the window to make them out.

Hnanisho sat in an old wooden chair, listening.

The letter said Yonan had married. In the back courtyard of the monastery in Chang'an, on the sixteenth day of the fifth month.

Hnanisho nodded.

The letter said that on the third day after the wedding, Yonan died. Narsai killed him. Under the locust tree at the monastery gate, by the well, with a little knife for trimming pens. Yonan did not try to get away.

Hnanisho's head did not nod again.

The secretary paused and glanced at him. Hnanisho raised his hand and told him to read on.

The letter told of the county, of those of the same kind who offend against one another are each judged by the law of their own people; Yisi had spelled out those Tang words by sound, and the secretary read them stumblingly, as if with pebbles in his mouth. The letter told of the trial, of how Yisi had judged it. Deposed, barred from the lectern, not cast out of the church, to gather the dead, to take the orphans, to live.

The last line was: "What the Patriarch allowed was not wrong. The wrong was in Chang'an. Chang'an is in my charge. How it is to be dealt with, I will abide by the Patriarch."

When the secretary had finished reading, he put the letter down.

For a long time there was no sound in the room. Outside the window was the river, and on the river boats, and the boatmen calling, one call long, one short.

"Before he died," said Hnanisho, "what did Yonan say? Is it in the letter?"

The secretary took up the letter again, searched a while, and found it.

"It is," he said. "But…"

"Read it."

"This sentence the priest Yisi has spelled out by the sound of the Tang," said the secretary. "I can sound it out, but I don't know what it means."

"Read it."

So the secretary read it. He read it letter by letter, and what came out was a string of sounds no one could understand. Nine sounds, broken in two: four, then five. Bu yao shang ta. Ta shi zhu de yang. He read very stiffly, very slowly, as Yonan in Ctesiphon that first time had said, my name is Yonan.

Hnanisho listened to it with his eyes shut.

"Read it again."

The secretary read it again.

Hnanisho sat there, and after a while said: "It sounds like a prayer."

The secretary asked: "Shall I write to Chang'an and ask what it means?"

Hnanisho thought, and shook his head.

"No need to ask," he said.

That afternoon he had the secretary find the copy of the letter he had written to Yonan two years before.

The secretary searched a long time in the cupboard, and found it. Hnanisho had him read it. The secretary read as far as the sentence: "God has regard to man's weakness. For he knoweth our frame; he remembereth that we are dust."

"Stop," said Hnanisho.

The secretary stopped.

Hnanisho sat in the old wooden chair, his two hands resting on his knees, the backs covered with spots. He looked out of the window, looked a long time.

"I had regard to one man's weakness," he said.

The secretary waited for him to go on.

He did not go on.

That night he dictated an answer, and the secretary wrote it. The answer was not long. He said Yisi had judged rightly. He said that for every dead man Narsai gathered, the monastery in Chang'an must pray for them, and pray for Narsai too. He said: peace to the sister A-Lian.

At the end, he had the secretary add one line.

"The East is in my charge."

When the secretary had written it, he handed the letter to him. He could no longer see letters well; he held it close under the lamp, looked a long time, and signed his name at the end. He signed very slowly. When he had signed, he put down the pen and said: "This one, send by the fastest caravan."

The secretary said: "The fastest will still take the better part of a year."

Hnanisho said: "Then the better part of a year."

Here something more must be said of Baghdad.

In those years the Church of the East under the rule of the Caliph was lawful. The church had a patriarch, the patriarch had a seal, and the seal was acknowledged by the Caliph. Its clergy could preach, could baptise, could build churches—only not higher than the mosques, and they must not ring their bells too loud in the streets. In the Caliph's palace there were several physicians who held to the Church of the East; they were good physicians, and the Caliph could not do without them. One physician, called Abu Quraysh, was the man most relied on by the Caliph al-Mahdi, and went into the palace more often than the vizier.

So in Baghdad, no one came to burn the church, and no one came to plunder it.

The pressure came from elsewhere.

One was the government. The government did not burn the church, but the government wanted money. The poll tax had to be paid year by year, the patriarch had to be acknowledged by the Caliph, and acknowledgement too had to be smoothed. Who to smooth it with, how much, all needed someone to run about.

The other was the church itself.

The Church of the East was large, spreading from the Two Rivers all the way to Khurasan, to India, to Chang'an. A large territory meant many metropolitans, and many metropolitans meant many who wanted to be patriarch. The metropolitan of Elam, the metropolitan of Merv, the several bishops round Mosul, each had his own people, his own ways in. A dozen and more years before, the Archdeacon Shapur had died in those ways. Who the murderer was, no one to this day could say; and those who could say, did not.

Hnanisho had become patriarch in the eighth year of Dali. Why he was chosen, people at the Patriarchate said in private: because he was old. Old, so he would not be patriarch long; not long, and every party still had hope.

Hnanisho himself probably knew it. In seven years as patriarch he moved no one, and promoted no one. Every day he looked at letters, listened to letters, signed his name. Every letter from the East he had the secretary read twice.

At the start of spring in the first year of Jianzhong, another letter came from Chang'an.

It was Yisi's again. The letters still big and clumsy. The letter said the monastery in Chang'an wanted to raise a stele, to carve on stone when Alopen came, which emperor issued the edict, and what the church had done in the East in these hundred and more years—carved in Tang characters, and in Syriac letters too. It asked the Patriarch's decision. At the end of the letter were the signatures of the three elders, and a line of small writing added by Samaan, saying that if the name of the reigning patriarch was to be written on the stele, the Patriarch's leave must first be asked.

By then Hnanisho's eyes could hardly see at all.

When the secretary had read it, Hnanisho asked: "Stone?"

"Stone," said the secretary. "The people of Chang'an say the Tang trust stone."

Hnanisho thought a while.

"My name is to be written on the stele?"

"By the rule, it must be."

Hnanisho smiled. The secretary had served him four years and had seldom seen him smile.

"Write it," he said. "While I lived I never went to the East. Let my name go there once; that's good."

He dictated an answer. The secretary wrote it, and when it was written, guided his hand to sign his name at the end, and below his name to write one word meaning granted. That stroke went crooked, crooked right out of the line. Hnanisho felt the paper and asked whether it had gone crooked, and the secretary said it had not.

The answer went in the spring, with a caravan going to Khurasan, out by the east gate.

Two months later, Hnanisho died.

He died in the night, with no one beside him. The next morning the secretary went with the letters and knocked, and no one answered. He pushed the door and went in, and Hnanisho was sitting in that old wooden chair, his head fallen to one side, a blanket across his knees, as if asleep.

By his hand the secretary found a sheet of paper. Nothing was written on it; only a few marks scored with a fingernail, across, down, every which way. The secretary looked a long time and could not make out what it was.

That sheet was afterwards put away in the cupboard, with the letters from Chang'an.

As soon as Hnanisho died, the Patriarchate was in turmoil.

Faster than ever before. Before Hnanisho was even buried, Ephrem, metropolitan of Elam, had already set out for Baghdad, with several bishops in his train. From Merv too people came. Each party lodged in the city with its own kin, its own acquaintances, came to the Patriarchate by day to mourn, and at night shut the doors and counted votes.

They counted and counted, and came out with one man: Timothy.

Timothy was bishop of Beth Bgash, a place in the mountains east of Mosul, small and poor, which in the eyes of the parties counted as no party at all. He was fifty-two that year, three years younger than Yisi in Chang'an. The two never met in their lives, and did not know who the other was.

Timothy could talk. He spoke Syriac, spoke Arabic, spoke Greek, had read Aristotle, could debate with the scholars of the Caliph's palace, and when the debate was over they invited him to dinner. He was a friend of the physician Abu Quraysh. One word from Abu Quraysh before the Caliph was worth more than ten bishops quarrelling a month at the Patriarchate.

In the days of the election, one thing happened that was told at the Patriarchate for many years.

When Timothy came to Baghdad, he brought several heavy sacks. The sacks were tied at the mouth with hemp rope, took two men to carry, and when they were set down on the ground went thud. Some of the bishops saw them and made inquiries in private, and Timothy's men said these were the bishop's; when the matter was settled, naturally they would be put to use.

So those bishops inquired no further.

The matter was settled. Timothy was elected, consecrated, and took the patriarch's seat. Those bishops came to him and spoke of the sacks. Timothy had the sacks carried out and, before their eyes, untied the hemp ropes.

In the sacks were stones. Stones from the riverbed, big and small, round, flat, still muddy.

The bishops' faces went livid.

Timothy said: "The office of priest is not bought and sold for money."

He said it very calmly, as if preaching.

Ephrem would not accept it.

He said this election did not count; the electors had been fooled by stones, pressed by a physician, bought by men of the Caliph's palace. He took several bishops and held a council of his own, which declared Timothy's patriarchate void. Timothy's side held a council too, which declared Ephrem's council void.

Each side excommunicated the other once.

The Muslims of Baghdad watched these bearded old men quarrelling back and forth on the east bank of the river and found it very entertaining. Someone in the Caliph's palace told it to al-Mahdi as a joke, and when al-Mahdi had heard it he asked one thing: their patriarch's seal—whose hands is it in?

It was in Timothy's hands.

One night, Timothy was alone in the Patriarchate's library.

The library had been built when Hnanisho moved there; it was not large, three walls lined with wooden shelves, and on the shelves were heaped parchment rolls and paper rolls. There were the instructions of past patriarchs, letters from metropolitans of every region, old scriptures brought over from Ctesiphon, some more than a hundred years old, the parchment gone black, the edges curling, crumbling at a touch.

Timothy could not sleep. That day another letter had come from Ephrem's side, in very ugly words. He had read it, not answered it, and had it put away.

He drew a roll at random from the shelf and opened it by the lamp.

It was the life of Nestorius.

Nestorius had lived more than three hundred years before.

He was a monk of Antioch, a fine preacher, and was later summoned by the emperor to Constantinople to be patriarch there. He was patriarch three years. He said that Mary was the mother of Christ, and should not be called the mother of God. These words angered Cyril, patriarch of Alexandria. The two of them quarrelled, quarrelled all the way to the emperor, and the emperor called the bishops of the world to a council at Ephesus.

Cyril arrived first. The bishops from Antioch were on the road and had not yet come. Cyril waited two weeks, waited no longer, opened the council, and deposed Nestorius. By the time the bishops of Antioch arrived, the council was over.

Nestorius was sent back to his monastery at Antioch. Later he was exiled, first to Petra, then to the Great Oasis in Egypt. The oasis was in the middle of the desert, sand on every side. The desert nomads raided it several times; once they carried him off and then let him go, and he was injured, and one hand was never much use after.

In the oasis he wrote a book in his own defence. There is a passage in it whose sense is: let Nestorius be anathema, so long as what they confess of God is right; if I am accursed, no matter.

He died in the desert. When he died he was about seventy.

He never in his life went to the East.

When Timothy had read it, he laid the roll on his knees and sat a long time.

He thought: the church of the East, the people of the West call it the Nestorian sect. Have called it so three hundred years. But Nestorius himself never went to Persia, never to the Two Rivers, never to Khurasan, still less to Chang'an. He was patriarch three years in Constantinople, wrote a book in the desert, and died.

How did this church survive?

It was the bishops under the Persian kings who, generation after generation, dealt with the king, dealt with the king's officials, dealt with the fire priests, and later dealt with the caliphs. Some bishops were killed, some imprisoned, some bowed their heads, some gave gifts. Their names the people of the West do not remember, not one.

But the church survived.

Nestorius was right. Timothy believed he was right. But a man who was right had been driven into the desert by a man who arrived two weeks before.

Timothy thought: choose the men who can keep the church alive.

He rolled up the life of Nestorius and put it back on the shelf.

He did not go back to bed at once.

He searched the shelves a while longer and came to the compartment for letters from the East. On top were the letters from Chang'an, a pile, tied with a fine cord. He untied the cord and looked at them one by one.

One had big, clumsy letters, scraped in several places, the parchment worn so thin the light came through.

He read it through by the lamp. At deposed, barred from the lectern, not cast out of the church, to gather the dead, to take the orphans, to live, he paused. At the wrong was in Chang'an; Chang'an is in my charge, he paused again.

In the letter there was a line spelled out by the sound of Tang speech. Nine sounds, broken in two. He did not know them, and read them once, very stiffly.

And read them again.

Under the letter lay a sheet—the copy of Hnanisho's answer. Its last line was in the secretary's hand: "The East is in my charge."

Under that lay another sheet with nothing written on it, only a few marks scored by a fingernail.

Timothy looked at those marks a long time. He too could not make out what they were.

He folded the letters back as they had been, tied them, and put them back in their compartment.

Then he went back to his own room, lit the lamp, spread out a sheet of paper, and took up his pen.

The first letter he wrote was to Ephrem of Elam.
