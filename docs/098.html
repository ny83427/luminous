Appendix 10　Football in Tang and Song China

Chapter 7 describes a football match between the Shuofang and Hedong armies outside the walls of Jiumen. The first draft of that chapter was called The Eye of Elegance (风流眼), and drew on the commonest later picture of "ancient Chinese football": a single tall goal with a hole in the net, the two sides kicking across it, and the ball never allowed to touch the ground. But that picture comes mostly from the Song dynasty. The story takes place in the fifteenth year of Tianbao, at the very end of the High Tang. How the Tang played, how the Song played, and when exactly the term "eye of elegance" appeared, deserve sorting out on their own. Chapter 7 was later rewritten on this basis and renamed Football in the Camp; the grounds for the rewriting are in sections 6 and 7 below.

Two words first. Cu (蹴) is to kick; ju (鞠) is a ball. Before the Qin the game was called taju; from the Han it was generally called cuju; from the Tang it was also called cuqiu, and by a shift of sound zhuqiu. Note too that when the people of the Tang said daqiu, "striking the ball," they usually meant polo, played on horseback with a stick—a different thing from cuju, played with the feet. Chapter 23 of this book, The Polo Mallet, is about polo.

1. Before the Tang: a stuffed ball, and two sides attacking each other

Cuju first appears in the Warring States period. The Stratagems of the Warring States ("Qi") says that among the people of Linzi, the capital of Qi, "there were none who did not blow the yu pipes, play the zither, strike the zhu, pluck the lute, fight cocks, race dogs, play liubo, and kick the ball."

In the Han, football was closely tied to the army. The bibliographic treatise of the Book of Han lists a work called Cuju in twenty-five chapters, which Ban Gu filed among the military books under "military skills"; sadly it was lost after the Tang. The Shiji says that when Huo Qubing was beyond the frontier and his soldiers were short of food, he still "dug out a pitch and kicked the ball."

What a Han pitch looked like is shown by Li You's Inscription on the Ball Court (Jucheng ming) of the Eastern Han. It is only eight lines:

圓鞠方牆，仿象陰陽。法月衡對，二六相當。建長立平，其例有常。不以親疏，不有阿私。端心平意，莫怨其非。鞠政猶然，況乎執機。

A round ball and a square wall, in the image of yin and yang. Modelled on the moon, facing pair by pair, two and six set against each other. Choose a captain, appoint an umpire; their rules are fixed. Make no distinction of near and far; show no favour. Keep the heart upright and the mind even; do not resent being judged wrong. If this is so in the governance of the ball, how much more in the governance of the state.

Several things can be read from it. "A round ball and a square wall": the pitch was square, with walls around it, and so was called a "ball city." "Modelled on the moon, facing pair by pair": the goals imitated the shape of the moon and faced each other in pairs, and were called "ball chambers." "Two and six set against each other" can be read two ways: two teams of six, or six goals on each side; some read it as twelve to a team. It remains unsettled. "Choose a captain, appoint an umpire": a captain (zhang) was chosen and an umpire (ping) set up. "Make no distinction… show no favour" is what is required of the umpire; "keep the heart upright… do not resent being judged wrong" is what is required of the players: accept the ruling, and do not blame others if you lose.

How positions were divided, how a side was arrayed, how fouls were punished, how long a game lasted—the inscription does not say, and no other Han or Tang source records it.

At this time the ball was stuffed. The early Tang scholar Yan Shigu, annotating the Book of Han, says the ju was "made of leather, filled with something solid"; Sima Zhen, in his Suoyin commentary on the Shiji, also says it was "made of hide, filled with hair." A stuffed ball is heavy and cannot be kicked high, so the goal would not be set high either. So scholars generally think that football before the Tang was two teams attacking each other directly: a goal at each end, the players dribbling, passing and tackling, trying to get the ball into the other side's goal. This is closest to modern football, with bodily contact as well as dribbling and passing along the ground.

2. The Tang: an inflated ball and a tall goal

The great change in Tang football was that the ball went from stuffed to inflated.

The Chuxue ji, compiled under Xuanzong, puts it plainly: "In ancient times it was made of matted hair; now it is made of leather, with a bladder inside, blown up, closed, and kicked." The "bladder" was that of an ox or pig, inflated to make an inner lining, with leather sewn around it. In the late Tang someone made a joke on the surname of the poet Pi Rixiu (pi means hide), writing: "Six pointed pieces of hide stitched into a ball, scalded in the fire and kneaded in water; as long as it is full of empty air, it invites kicks and punches without end" (one version has "eight pieces"). That is how such balls were made. The Chuxue ji was compiled in the Kaiyuan years, so by Yisi's time the army was already playing with an inflated ball.

A light ball can be kicked high and far. Wang Wei, writing of football at the Cold Food festival, says "the kicked ball flies again and again above the birds"; the Youyang zazu says a woman playing football in front of the Fugan Monastery often kicked it "half as high as the pagoda."

The goal changed too. The Yuan scholar Ma Duanlin's Wenxian tongkao says: "Cuqiu probably began in the Tang. Two tall bamboo poles, several zhang high, were set up, with a net strung across their tops for a goal, and the ball was sent over." Two tall bamboo poles with a net between them, and the ball had to "cross" through the goal: this is the usual basis for the later view that the tall goal began in the Tang. But note that the Wenxian tongkao is a Yuan work and this is a later account; the Tang itself has left no record so specific.

Some scholars infer from this that since an inflated ball could be kicked high and far, keeping the old arrangement of a goal at each end would have needed a very large pitch, and so in the Tang the goal was gradually moved to the middle of the field, the two teams each on one side of the net, scoring by kicking the ball through a hole in it. With a net between the teams there was no bodily contact, and the game was less combative than in the Han.

The Tang also had a way of playing without any goal, called baida, "plain kicking." Baida was about kicking high and doing tricks; it could be played alone or by several people taking turns. Wang Yong's Rhapsody on the Palace Women Kicking the Ball describes palace women at football: "the ball never leaves the foot, the foot never leaves the ball." The Jutan lu records a girl of seventeen or eighteen with her hair in three loops, standing in wooden clogs under a locust tree on the north street of the Shengye Ward in Chang'an; when the young soldiers playing football sent the ball her way, she "caught it and sent it back, straight up several zhang," and more and more people gathered to watch. The story shows in passing that "young soldiers" playing football was nothing unusual in the Tang.

Football was also a seasonal custom in the Tang, especially around the Cold Food and Clear Bright festivals; examples from poetry are given at the end of this section.

In sum, Tang football had at least three forms: the Han way, with a goal at each end and direct attack (whether it was still popular in the army after the High Tang the sources are too thin to say; one can only guess); the tall bamboo poles with a net and shooting through it; and baida without a goal. Which was dominant in the Tang, scholars disagree. Only two things are certain: the ball was inflated, and the tall goal had appeared by the Tang.

One more point: the rule that "the ball may not touch the ground" is, as far as clear records go, found only in the Song. In Tang baida keeping the ball off the ground was the skill, but whether the ball touching the ground counted as a loss in a game across the net, Tang sources do not say.

Lines about football in Tang poetry are few; the most often quoted are these. Wang Wei, "On the Cold Food Festival East of the City": "The kicked ball flies again and again above the birds; the swings rise one after another out of the drooping willows." Du Fu, "Two Poems on the Clear Bright Festival": "Ten years of football, the young ones led far away; ten thousand li, and the swings the same custom." Bai Juyi, "Ten Couplets on the Cold Food Festival at the Luo Bridge": "The ball is kicked, no dust arises; the water-splashing rain has just cleared." Wei Zhuang, "Clear Bright Festival in Chang'an": "The palace eunuchs first hand out the new fire of Clear Bright; the chief ministers at leisure share out the baida money." The one that speaks directly of football in the army is Wei Yingwu's "Written in the North Tower after the Cold Food Festival":

園林過新節，風花亂高閣。遙聞擊鼓聲，蹴鞠軍中樂。

The gardens have passed the new festival; wind and blossom swirl round the high tower. Far off I hear the beating of drums: football, the army's pleasure.

The Cold Food festival just past, the poet in his tower hears drums far off: the camp is playing football. Two things can be learned from this poem: football was a common thing in Tang armies, and there were drums when they played. Wei Yingwu was one of Xuanzong's palace guards in the Tianbao years and later rose to be prefect of Suzhou; the date of this poem is unknown, and it is later than the fifteenth year of Tianbao. Chapter 7 borrows its last three characters, 軍中樂, "the army's pleasure," for the chapter's Chinese title, and uses the whole poem as the chapter's epigraph.

3. The Song: zhuqiu and baida

The Song was when football flourished most, and its forms became fixed, falling into two broad kinds: zhuqiu, with a goal, and baida.

Zhuqiu was a contest with a goal. Meng Yuanlao's Dongjing menghua lu (Dreams of the Splendour of the Eastern Capital), of the Northern Song, records a game at the emperor's birthday feast: a goal was set up before the hall for the occasion, "about three zhang high, decked with coloured silks, leaving a gate of about a foot." The captain of the Left Army was Su Shu, in a red brocade jacket; the captain of the Right Army was Meng Xuan, in blue brocade; the musicians played flutes and beat drums beside them. The winning side was rewarded with silver bowls and brocades; on the losing side, the captain was whipped and had white powder daubed on his face.

The Southern Song writer Chen Yuanliang's Shilin guangji has a picture of the goal: two posts thirty-two chi high, with a net hung between their tops, and in the middle of the net a round hole two chi eight cun across. This hole was called the fengliu yan, "the eye of elegance," and only a ball that passed through it counted. The later Cuju pu (Manual of Football) says the hole was only one chi two cun, which made it harder still.

Each side in Song zhuqiu had fixed positions. The Wulin jiushi (Old Affairs of Wulin) records the starting line-ups of the Southern Song palace teams; each of the Left and Right Armies had positions called qiutou (captain), qiaoqiu (feeder), zhengxie (main wing), touxie (head wing), zuo ganwang and you ganwang (left and right net-post), and sanli (rover). After the kick-off the ball was passed foot to foot among one side's players and finally to the captain, who shot for the eye. If the ball was not caught and fell to the ground, or a shot went out, the other side scored a point. So the art of Song zhuqiu was for the whole team to keep the ball in the air together, until one man shot true.

Baida was also very popular in the Song, played by anything from one to ten people: one player was called "one-man ground," two "two-man ground," three "the official ground," and there were many other names. Head, shoulders, chest, back, knees and feet could all be used, and control of the ball was what mattered. When the Water Margin describes Gao Qiu playing football—"the ball stuck to him as if with fish-glue"—it is this skill it means.

The Song also had football clubs, the most famous called the Qiyun She ("Cloud-High Club"), also the Yuan She ("Round Club"). Wu Zimu's Mengliang lu (Record of Dreams of Liang), of the Southern Song, says the football clubs of Lin'an were "not for officials, but pastimes for the sons of the rich, young men of elegance, and idlers."

4. Tang and Song compared

Tang Song
Ball Inflated ball already in use (Chuxue ji), of eight or six pieces of hide Inflated, more finely made; twelve pieces mentioned; dozens of named kinds
Goal Goal of tall bamboo poles with a net first appears in the Tang (later account in Wenxian tongkao) Goal in the middle of the field, about three zhang high, with the "eye of elegance" in the net (Dongjing menghua lu, Shilin guangji)
Contest The Han way of a goal at each end and direct attack may have survived; play across a net coming in Zhuqiu chiefly shooting across the net; no bodily contact between teams
Ball touching the ground In baida keeping it up is the skill; no explicit rule for contests Zhuqiu rule explicit: if the ball falls or goes out, the other side scores
Baida Already played, chiefly high kicks and tricks, by palace women and common women alike Very popular; one-, two-, three-man grounds, etc.; control of the ball
Organisation A seasonal pastime, played in the army, the palace and the wards Clubs like the Qiyun She; fixed positions

In a word: the Tang was the period of transition in which football turned from "attack" toward "skill," and the Song fixed that turn.

5. The term "eye of elegance"

Clear records of the fengliu yan come from the Song and Yuan (Shilin guangji, Cuju pu and others), and mean the round hole in the goal net through which one shot. The term has not been found in Tang sources.

Why it was called fengliu, the old books do not explain. In Song usage fengliu meant dashing, outstanding, gifted, and football was a pastime of "young men of elegance" (fengliu zidi). The Cuju pu praises the art as "first house of all elegance." So the fengliu yan was perhaps "the eye where one shines": the ball goes through there, and the man who kicked it wins glory. This is a guess, not a conclusion.

6. The form in Chapter 7: the early Tang game of two goals

The first draft of Chapter 7 described the Song-style game of shooting across a net. Since the story takes place in the fifteenth year of Tianbao, and the game is an impromptu one between soldiers of two armies, the revision changed it to the two-goal game handed down from the Han: a ball chamber at each end, both teams attacking and defending on the same ground, with passing along the ground and through the air, and bodily contact—closest to modern football. Whether this game was still popular in High Tang armies the sources are too thin to say; the book's version is an inference, not a conclusion.

The details follow the sources where there are sources, and are supplied by the novel where there are none:

The position names are borrowed from the Song. In Song zhuqiu they were the division of labour for play across the net; not one name for positions in the Han–Tang two-goal game has survived. To give readers a concrete picture, the novel borrows the Song names, has them called in the book "the old names they use for football in Chang'an," and redistributes them for the two-goal game:

Position Original sense in Song zhuqiu Role in Chapter 7
Captain (qiutou) Captain; the one who shoots for the eye The striker at the front, usually the side's "captain"
Feeder (qiaoqiu) Feeds the ball to the captain Behind the captain, setting up his shots
Main wing, head wing (zhengxie, touxie) Chief passers in midfield Midfield, winning the ball and running up and down
Left and right net-post (zuo/you ganwang) Stationed by the posts of the net Defenders by the two willow posts of their own chamber
Rover (sanli) Free on the field, covering gaps In front of his own goal covering gaps; may use his hands within three paces of the goal—today's goalkeeper

Whether these names existed before the Song there is at present no evidence; their use in the book is a borrowing.

7. The chapter title

The first draft of Chapter 7 was called The Eye of Elegance, for two reasons: Du De scores the winning goal in this chapter and in the next dies covering the army's retreat, so fengliu, "elegance," meant both the hole and the man; and the word sounds well. But the "eye of elegance" is not recorded until the Song and Yuan, and refers to the hole in the net of the game played across it; once the game was changed to two goals, the name no longer meant anything.

The revision took the last three characters of Wei Yingwu's "Written in the North Tower after the Cold Food Festival," 軍中樂, "the army's pleasure," as the title, and replaced the epigraph with the whole poem—the only chapter in the book whose epigraph is not from the Luminous stele. First, the poem is about exactly this: football in the camp just after the Cold Food festival, the drums heard from afar, matching the chapter's season, setting, and the drum that marks each point. Second, the Changshan garrison spent the Cold Food festival on the walls; this game is a festival made up afterwards, and under the one word "pleasure" lie forty-odd days of siege.

(In the English edition the chapter is titled Football in the Camp, which says plainly what the chapter is; the poem stands at its head as the epigraph.)
