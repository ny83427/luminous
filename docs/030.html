Volume Six　The Shuofang Army

Chapter 30　Du Huan

In the second month of the second year of Shangyuan, Jingjing was to travel west again.

This time it was to report. Half a year had passed since the affair in the Liquan Ward, and Yisi had written a long letter to the Patriarchate; he wrote for seven days, and when it was written, revised it for seven more. The letter told of the Pure Ones, of the old fire-keeper, of the questioning at the Ministry of Rites, of how many people the monastery had now—how many Tang, how many hu—how many dan of gruel it gave out in a year, how many dead it had buried. At the end he wrote: the affairs of Chang'an cannot be made clear in a letter; I am sending someone to tell it in person.

Who to send, the monastery debated a long time. The seven teachers were all past fifty, two already past sixty, and could not bear the winds of the Pamirs again. Yisi himself could not get away; his name was on the rolls of the Shuofang army, and though he was in Chang'an, he counted as being in camp.

In the end it was settled on Jingjing.

That year he was fifteen.

With him went an elder named Marcus.

Marcus was an old Chang'an hand. He had come to Chang'an in the Kaiyuan years and lived thirty years at the monastery in the Yining Ward, longer than Yisi. He was a Persian, small and a little bent, and spoke Tang with the accent of the West Market, slicker than many Tang people. He was sixty-four. He said he wanted to see the Two Rivers once more in this life, and when he had seen them, whether he came back or not was as the Lord willed.

When Jingjing heard this, his heart gave a lurch. He thought of the elder Ahad.

There were three old believers too. One was a felt-maker, one a camel driver, and one had travelled to Fulin when young and traded in medicines, and though his legs were bad now, he knew every well on the road. All three were hu, had lived half their lives in Chang'an, and all had one thought: to go back and see it once before they died.

The five of them travelled with a Sogdian caravan. The caravan had more than eighty camels, carrying silk west to trade for silver.

The day they left the city, Yisi saw them as far as the Kaiyuan Gate.

Five years before, Jingjing had travelled west once already. But that time it had not been from here; it had been from Lingzhou. Then Chang'an had been in rebel hands. Yisi had not seen him past the mouth of the lane, but stood at the courtyard gate and raised his hand and let it fall.

This time Yisi led his horse for him out of the city gate, and went on two li west with him.

Jingjing was already taller than Yisi. He had not noticed it himself; only when Yisi stood beside him did he look down and see that the crown of his father's head had white hair in it, cut very short, in stubbly tufts, like a harvested wheatfield.

"The letter," said Yisi.

Jingjing patted his chest. The letter lay against his chest, with his grandfather's wooden cross.

"When you see your uncle," said Yisi, "tell him I'm well."

"Are you well?"

Yisi glanced at him.

"I'm well," said Yisi.

Jingjing wanted to say: that place in your side still hurts in cloudy weather; when you turn over at night, I can hear it. He did not say it.

"Whatever you see on the road, write it down," said Yisi.

"Master Cen said," said Jingjing, "don't just write ought; write what you see."

"Then write what you see."

Yisi put the reins into his hands. His hand rested on the back of Jingjing's for a moment, very brief, like a leaf falling on it and the wind blowing it away.

"Don't drink cold water," he said.

Jingjing's eyes went hot all at once. He held it back hard, nodded, and swung up onto his horse.

When he had gone a long way, he looked back. Yisi was still standing at the roadside, under a willow. The willow had just begun to show a little green, and from far off it looked like a haze of smoke.

Yisi did not wave. Jingjing did not wave either.

On this road, Jingjing wrote a great deal.

He wrote of the Long Mountains. The snow on the Long Mountains had not yet melted, and the caravan waited three days at the pass for the snow to open a way. The elder Marcus shivered with cold, wrapped himself in felt with only his nose showing, and said: "Lord, I've slept thirty years on a heated bed in Chang'an. Why must you make me remember it here?"

He wrote of Liangzhou. There were far fewer soldiers in Liangzhou than five years before, and the men on the walls were thin and scattered. The caravan leader spent two days in the city gathering news and came back with a grim face, saying the Tibetans in the mountains to the south were getting closer and closer: the year before last they only stole cattle and sheep, and now they dared burn the military farms outside the walls. The caravan did not linger in Liangzhou, and left early the next morning.

He wrote of that earthen mound outside the Jade Gate. Five years before, Uncle Duan had taken an arrow there, the head stuck in his arm, and Uncle Xu had dug it out for him. He hadn't cried out. Jingjing stood at the foot of the mound a while. The mound was just as it had been; the wind passed over it and raised a film of yellow sand. Uncle Duan was gone. He had died at Xiangzhou the spring before last, and when he died had asked a question, and no one had answered it.

Jingjing wrote on the paper: "Outside the Jade Gate. The mound is still there. Uncle Duan isn't."

When he had written it, he looked at it a long time, and added another line after it: "Uncle Duan slept with one eye open. I tried. I couldn't sleep."

In the fourth month they reached Xizhou.

The church north of the city was still there. Someone had pulled up the grass in the courtyard. The wooden cross on the east wall of the sanctuary had been wiped spotless. The woman who cooked was still there, a little older; seeing Jingjing she stared a long while before she knew him, then seized his hand and said: you've grown so tall, you've grown so tall.

Of her husband there was still no word.

That night the old believers of the church stewed a pot of mutton for them. At supper Jingjing sat at the door of the sanctuary looking at the courtyard. He remembered this place. Here he had taught the seven elders to speak Tang, and the elder Ishaq had said mo pa as "grind and crawl," and everyone had laughed for three days. Here, one night, he had heard the character lotus.

He touched the knotted cord inside his coat, and did not take it out.

Past the Pamirs, it was summer.

The wind on the Pamirs was as strong as ever. Jingjing rode with a cloth tied over his mouth and nose, only his two eyes showing. Five years before, he had followed Uncle Duan's back and inched his way down the mountain step by step. This time there was no Uncle Duan's back ahead. He looked for himself.

Beyond the pass there were still mountains upon mountains, and behind the mountains more mountains.

Coming down, he was terribly thirsty, and at a river of snowmelt he lay down and drank several great mouthfuls. The water was so icy his teeth ached to the roots. The elder Marcus shouted at him from behind, but it was already too late.

That night he squatted outside the tent for most of the night.

The next morning the old medicine trader who had been to Fulin brewed him a bowl of ginger broth, very strong, so hot it made his eyes stream. The old trader watched him drink it, shaking his head, and said: "If your pa knew."

Jingjing said: "Don't tell him."

The old trader said: "I won't tell him. Will you write it in your own journal?"

Jingjing thought and said: "I will."

He really did. What he wrote was: "Snowmelt river below the Pamirs. Drank it. Squatted half the night. Pa said so."

In the seventh month they reached Balkh.

The city was as small as ever, the mud houses as low. The gate of the church south of the city stood open, and the mulberry tree in the courtyard was still there. Under the tree sat an old man, badly bent, wrapped in an old fur robe, a knotted cord clutched in his hand.

Jingjing stopped at the church gate.

The old man heard footsteps, raised his head and squinted a long while. His eyes had gone cloudy, and he could not see people clearly. Jingjing went over, squatted down before him, and said: "Elder."

The elder Ahad put out his hand and touched his face, touched his shoulders, touched down his arms to his hands. Touched them once, and again.

"Grown so tall," he said. He said it in Tang, more slowly than before; some words he had to think a long time to remember.

The old deacon who mended shoes was gone. He had died the winter before last, the elder said, very peacefully; the evening before he had mended a shoe, and the next morning he did not get up. The church now had a young priest from Nishapur who led the services for the elder. The elder's legs were no good any more; on the day of the seventh-day offering he still expounded the scripture, sitting down.

Jingjing went with the elder to the open ground behind the church.

His grandfather's and grandmother's graves were clean, not a blade of grass. Before the stones stood a little earthen bowl with clear water in it. Beside the two graves there was a new one, its earth already settled, a cross carved on its stone and a name carved under the cross.

"It's him," said the elder. "He said he wanted to be buried beside the priest Milis. Said he'd mended shoes all his life, and every shoe of the priest Milis's he'd mended."

Jingjing knelt before the three graves and said the evening praises through once. The elder could not stand; he sat on a stone and said them with him. Halfway through the elder's voice stopped; Jingjing looked back, and the elder was sitting there clutching the knotted cord, his eyes shut, his lips still moving.

"Elder," asked Jingjing, "did you pull all this grass?"

"I can't pull it any more," said the elder. "There's a boy in the city comes to hear the scripture on offering days, and when he's heard it, I have him pull it. Each time, I give him a flatbread."

He laughed, his beard twitching up and down.

That night Jingjing wrote in his journal: "Grandfather, Grandmother, the old grandad who mended shoes. The elder Ahad watches over them for Pa. I came and saw them for Pa again."

They reached the Two Rivers at the end of the tenth month.

Half a day's journey short of Ctesiphon there was a ferry. Here the Tigris made a bend; the river was wide and slow, and rows and rows of boats lay moored along the bank, some going upstream, some down. The boats going downstream, at the end of their journey, came to Basra on the coast, and from Basra you could take a great ship all the way to India, all the way to Guangzhou.

The caravan rested at the ferry. Jingjing dismounted and went to the river for water. As he drew it he was muttering to himself, in Tang. He did not notice it himself. All along this road he had often done this: when he was alone, he talked to himself in Tang, as if afraid of forgetting it.

Someone behind him spoke.

"You're from Chang'an?"

It was Tang. A Chang'an accent, with a little of the country twang of east of the capital.

Jingjing spun round so fast he nearly dropped the bucket in the river.

The man who had spoken was about forty, in an Arab's long robe, white cloth wound round his head, his face burned a dark red by the sun. But his brows and eyes were a Tang man's brows and eyes.

He looked at Jingjing, and his lips trembled.

"You're from Chang'an?" he asked again.

"I am," said Jingjing. "The Yining Ward."

The man squatted down all at once, squatted on the muddy riverbank, and covered his face with both hands.

It was a good while before he took his hands away. His eyes were red, but he was smiling.

"Eleven years I haven't heard Chang'an speech," he said. "Eleven years. I thought if I heard Chang'an speech I'd cry. I didn't. Look, I didn't cry."

His surname was Du, his name Du Huan, of the capital district. In the tenth year of Tianbao he had gone west with Gao Xianzhi's army, suffered a great defeat at Talas, been taken prisoner by the Arabs, and been carried west all the way to here. He had lived eleven years among the Arabs, longest in a city called Aqula, done many things and seen many people. Now there was a merchant ship willing to take him, and he was going to sail from Basra and go back by sea, back to Guangzhou.

"The ship sails the day after tomorrow," he said.

That afternoon the two of them sat by the river and talked half the day.

Du Huan asked about Chang'an. So Jingjing told him. Told him An Lushan had rebelled, Chang'an had been lost, Chang'an had been won back again. Told him the Retired Emperor had come back from Shu and now lived in the palace, and the Son of Heaven was the Retired Emperor's son and had been Son of Heaven five or six years already.

Du Huan listened, asking question after question. Towards the end he stopped asking, and only listened.

"When I left," he said, "there was only one Son of Heaven."

Jingjing did not know how to answer that, so he did not answer.

Du Huan asked him too: what's a Chang'an boy like you doing running about out here?

Jingjing told him of the Daqin monastery. Told him of his father, his uncle, the Patriarchate, his report.

When Du Huan heard it his eyes lit up.

"The Daqin law," he said. "I know it. I've seen your people in Aqula. In the cities there, they worship every kind of thing—the Arab law most of all, but there's the Daqin law too, and the Xunxun law."

He took a roll of paper from inside his robe, worn furry at the edges from turning, covered close with writing.

"I've been writing it down all along the way," he said. "Whatever I saw, I wrote. I've an uncle in the family who loves books; I thought when I got back I'd give it to him to read."

He turned a few pages, found a place, and read it to Jingjing:

"The people of Daqin are skilled at healing eyes and the flux; some see a sickness before it shows itself; some open the skull and draw out worms."

When he had read it he looked at Jingjing and asked very seriously: "You people of Daqin—can you really open the skull and draw out worms?"

Jingjing was taken aback, and could not help laughing.

"I can't," he said. "I can't even cure my own flux. All I can do is drink ginger broth."

Du Huan laughed too. He laughed till he rocked back and forth, laughed till he nearly rolled into the river. When he had finished laughing, he wiped his eyes with his sleeve, and his eyes were red again.

When the sun was about to set, Du Huan asked him for something.

"That journal of yours," he said. "Could you let me look at it?"

Jingjing handed him the journal. Du Huan turned a few pages, turning very slowly. When he came to the line Uncle Duan slept with one eye open. I tried. I couldn't sleep, he stopped and looked at it a long time.

"Well written," he said. "You've written what you saw."

He gave the journal back to Jingjing, opened his own roll, took out a brush, dipped it in ink, and wrote a line on the last page. When he had written it, he showed it to Jingjing.

It said: Winter, twentieth year of Tianbao. At a ferry on the Tigris, met a young monk of the Daqin monastery in the Yining Ward of Chang'an, aged fifteen, very good Tang speech, a fine drinker of ginger broth.

Jingjing looked at the line and blushed. After looking a while, he said in a small voice: "Master Du, it hasn't been Tianbao for a long time."

Du Huan said: "I know."

He looked at the line, and did not change it.

"On this roll of mine," he said, "it's still Tianbao."

"I'll make a note of you," said Du Huan.

Jingjing thought of Uncle Duan. Five years before, on the road to Ctesiphon, Uncle Duan had squatted beside him and said: on that paper of yours, could you make a note for me?

He opened his own journal, took out his brush too, and wrote a line on the last page: "A ferry on the Tigris. Met Du Huan of the capital district. Eleven years without hearing Chang'an speech. He says he didn't cry. He's going home."

When he had written it, he showed it to Du Huan too.

Du Huan read it and said nothing. He looked at the page a long time, then closed the journal and handed it back to Jingjing with both hands.

Early on the third day, Du Huan's ship sailed.

It was going downstream, with the current, and went very fast. Jingjing stood at the ferry watching it go further and further, smaller and smaller. Du Huan stood at the stern, looking at the shore all the while.

As the ship rounded the bend of the river, Du Huan raised his hand and waved.

Jingjing raised his hand too and waved. He waved and waved, until the ship had gone round and could not be seen, and his hand was still raised.

That evening they entered Ctesiphon.

That great archway in the city was still there. The Patriarchate was still by the river in the east of the city, its mud walls as low and thick as ever. Two men in grey robes stood at the gate with short staves in their hands. One of them, about thirty, broad-faced, big-eyed, stepped forward to stop them when he saw them coming, asked a few questions, looked at the wax seal on the letter, and only then stood aside.

Jingjing learned later that this man was called Baluma.

His uncle was still in that little room.

The room was just as small, the walls still white, the wooden cross still nailed to the east wall. The wooden table, the mats, the oil lamp. The lamp was lit.

His uncle sat behind the wooden table.

His hair had gone all white. Five years before it had been only half white; now there was not one black hair left. His face was longer, thinner, the cheekbones higher, the eye sockets deeper, and those brown eyes were still exactly like his father's.

His right sleeve was still empty, knotted at the elbow. The knot was tied very tight, very neat.

Jingjing went up to the wooden table, knelt down, and made his bow.

His uncle looked at him. This time he did not look as long as the time before.

He spoke, still in the country speech of Balkh.

"Is the road still open?"

Jingjing raised his head.

"It's open," he said. "I've walked it twice."
