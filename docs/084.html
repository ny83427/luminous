Volume Twelve　Jade Gate

Chapter 84　Pure Wind

Crossing the Tianshan took twenty-three days. The Uighur caravan went by a road through the mountains, first up along a river; the valley was narrow, rock walls on both sides, ice hanging on the rock walls, still hanging in the eighth month; the river water was white and roared deafeningly. After seven days they came out of the valley onto a high place; the high place had no trees, only grass, the grass yellow, the wind very strong; the Uighurs said this was the mountain's top; past here, it went down. Going down, the grass grew greener every day and the trees more numerous every day—pines, black-green, growing patch after patch on the slopes—and below the slopes was a great plain, and on the plain a river, and by the river tents, white tents, one after another, scattered over the grass, and by the tents sheep, horses, cattle.

Ayi on her horse looked at the plain, at the tents; she reined in her horse and sat on its back and would not go on. Jingfu led the donkey up beside her—Xiaoman riding on the donkey's back—and Xiaoman asked why Sister Ayi had stopped. Ayi said her Ma had told her the steppe was green; her Ma hadn't lied to her.

Ayi's Ma's people were a small tribe of forty-odd tents, summering by the river and moving in winter to the valleys in the south. The tribe's chief was a man of middle age, his face very broad, his eyes very narrow; he heard from the caravan's headman how Ayi came, had Ayi stand before him, looked at her face, and sent someone to fetch an old woman.

The old woman was Ayi's Ma's aunt, past seventy, half blind, brought held up by someone. She stood before Ayi, reached out, felt Ayi's face, felt her forehead, felt her eyebrows, felt her chin, and when she had felt them said something in Uighur; Ayi heard, and wept. Jingfu asked Ayi later what the old woman had said; Ayi said she said: those are her eyebrows—her Ma's eyebrows.

What to do with Jingfu and Xiaoman, the tribe's people didn't know. The chief asked the caravan's headman who this man was; the caravan's headman said: from over the Tang side, from some monastery over there, who brought these two girls. The chief looked at Jingfu, looked at his black robe, looked at his face, and asked whether he was Tang. Jingfu said he was born in Chang'an; Chang'an's roll wrote him as a man of An, to be sent back to his own place; he didn't go to An; he brought Ayi here. The chief asked again what teaching he was of. Jingfu said the teaching of Daqin; he believed in Aluohe, believed in Mishihe. The chief heard, said a few words to those beside him, and those beside him all looked at Jingfu, and their faces were not friendly.

Ayi told him later that what the chief said was: the Tang killed our Manichaean teachers, killed our people in Chang'an; a man come from the Tang side, and he doesn't believe in Mani either—what's he come for? That night Jingfu and Xiaoman had no tent to live in. Ayi was in her great-aunt's tent; Jingfu led the donkey and took Xiaoman to a lone willow at the edge of the tribe's camp and made a shelter there with felts, lit a fire, and the two of them crowded into the shelter. Xiaoman asked him: has Sister Ayi stopped wanting us? Jingfu said no; Sister Ayi is with her Ma's relatives; she'll come tomorrow.

Ayi came the next day. She came running, ran to the willow, knelt at the shelter's door, and said weeping that she'd told the chief: the leader is a good man; the leader took her in in Chang'an; when her Ma died, it was the leader who took her in; the leader told the ward headman the truth; it was the leader who brought her all the way here; if the chief won't let the leader stay, she'll live under the willow with the leader.

The chief came that evening. He stood under the willow, looked at the shelter, looked at the three squatting at the shelter's door, had someone bring an old tent and throw it on the ground, and said: live in it; when we move in winter, follow along; the tribe's rules, you keep; your teaching, recite it yourself; don't recite it in the tribe.

That winter Jingfu nearly died. In winter they moved to the valleys in the south; in the valleys the wind was a little less, but the snow was heavy; when it snowed, in one night it buried a man to the waist, and the tents' doors were blocked by snow and had to be dug out from inside. Jingfu didn't know how to pitch a tent, how to burn dung, how to milk a mare; no one in the tribe taught him; Ayi taught him; it was Ayi's first winter on the steppe too; she learned from her great-aunt, and having learned came back and taught him. Xiaoman was ten and learned faster than both of them; she went with the tribe's children to gather dung, brought it back, dried it, and stacked it by the tent, stacked it neat and square.

In the twelfth month Jingfu fell ill. First cold, then hot, burning till he was confused; he lay in the tent raving, raving in the speech of Chang'an—of morning devotion, of the gruel shed, of the lamp, of Master, of Uncle Lingbao, of the Kaiyuan Gate. Xiaoman watched over him, rubbed his forehead with snow, and when the snow grew warm changed it for another handful. Ayi went to beg her great-aunt, and her great-aunt said she had a remedy, from the Manichaean monastery: boil the water of a certain grass root; the root is under the snow and must be dug for; Ayi went and dug, dug a whole morning in the snow, found it, brought it back and boiled it, boiled it black and bitter, and poured it down Jingfu.

In Jingfu's bundle there was still a piece of ginger, brought out of Xizhou, dried, shrunk to a little lump, hard as stone. Xiaoman found it, scraped a little powder from it with a knife, put it in the pot of root-water, and boiled them together; the water that came out was bitter and hot both. Jingfu drank it, drank it three days, and the fever broke.

The day he woke he saw, through the smoke hole at the tent's top, light leaking in, white, snow-light. Xiaoman was asleep lying beside him, still gripping the knife she had scraped the ginger with. He lay looking at that bit of light and thought of Master: Master all his life feared cold water, all his life drank ginger broth; he all his life never feared cold water and hadn't drunk ginger broth more than a few times; now he had drunk it.

That winter in the valley some people's way of looking at him changed. In those days he was ill, from a neighbouring tribe wintering in the same valley an old man came every day to stand a while at the tent's door, not coming in, stood a while and left. When Jingfu was well, Ayi told him: that old man—what his surname was she didn't know; the tribe's people called him Old Sa; in his family's tent hung a cross; he was of the neighbouring tribe, wintering in the same valley as Ayi's tribe.

Old Sa came after the opening of spring. The snow had half melted; the river in the valley had thawed; blocks of ice floated down one by one, crashing on the stones with a crack, crack. Old Sa came, leading his grandson, a boy of ten or so, and stood at the door of Jingfu's tent. Old Sa was past sixty, a Uighur, his face dark, his beard white; he said a long string in Uighur, and Ayi beside them translated.

He said his family believed in Daqin; his Pa's grandfather came to believe, through Sogdian merchants; later the tribe's people all believed in Mani, but his family didn't; in his family's tent hung a cross; at the offering once in seven the family knelt under the cross and recited scripture, the Syriac the Sogdian merchants had taught—not understanding a word, just reciting by the sounds, recited so four generations. He said in their tribe there were three more households, the same; four households, twenty-odd souls, in this valley in winter and by the river in summer; at the offering once in seven they gathered in his family's tent and recited scripture, and when they came to one place they stopped; his Pa said that place ought to have bread; they had no bread, so they stopped. He said he'd heard you were of the Daqin Monastery in Chang'an; he'd like to ask you to come to his family's tent and lead the recitation at the offering once in seven.

Jingfu went with him. That day was the offering once in seven. Old Sa's family's tent was not large, and twenty-odd were crowded in it; on the tent's east side hung a cross, of wood, gone black, hung on the tent's wooden frame by a leather thong. Twenty-odd knelt on the felts; Old Sa led the recitation, in Syriac; Jingfu listened and could tell which passages they were—the psalms, the Lord's Prayer—but the sounds were crooked, badly crooked; some words came out with Uighur sounds; some words were missing half. They came to one place and stopped; everyone knelt and recited no more; those in the tent knelt, waiting.

Jingfu knew where that was. It was the place of the offering bread. He knelt there among those kneeling people, and thought of the hall in Chang'an, thought of the glass bowl, thought of Uncle Lingbao's hands shaking, thought of his own left hand holding the bottom of the bowl, the bowl not moving.

Today he was here; everyone stopped here; he stopped here too. From his robe he took a cake of bread. Xiaoman had baked it that morning; the wheat Ayi had traded for with the tribe's people; it was badly baked, burned on one side. He held the bread in his hands and spoke to those in the tent, and Ayi beside him translated sentence by sentence into Uighur.

He said he'd never had hands laid on him, wasn't even a deacon; he couldn't bless the bread. He said: this bread hasn't been blessed; it's food; Xiaoman baked it this morning; it's burned on one side. He said: in Chang'an there was a church, and in the church a priest called Lingbao; at the offering once in seven he blessed the bread; he recited the consecration very slowly, his hands shaking, and Jingfu held the bottom of the bowl. Uncle Lingbao has gone west now, to his Pa's homeland; he doesn't know whether he got there. He said: this bread, he'll break, and everyone eat it, and as you eat, remember that One—that One in the hall at Chang'an, in the hall at Xizhou, at Kashgar, at Balkh, in this tent. He said: and some day later, if someone who has had hands laid on him comes from the west, comes from the east, and blesses the bread, everyone will receive it again, and receiving it, know that today's piece was waiting for that day.

He broke the bread. He broke it into twenty-odd pieces and handed them out piece by piece—to Old Sa, to Old Sa's grandson, to the people kneeling in the tent; at the very end one piece was left, and he handed it to Xiaoman; Xiaoman said she baked it, she wouldn't eat it; he said: eat it; you baked it; you remember too.

Those in the tent ate it one by one. Old Sa ate it, and when he had eaten it said something in Uighur, which Ayi translated for Jingfu: he says—four generations, and the first time there's been something in the place where we stop.

That spring the grass by the river turned green again. The tribe moved back from the southern valleys to the river; on the day of the move Jingfu walked behind the last high-wheeled cart; he came out of the valley and saw that plain; last year when they came the grass was still green, and not many days later the tips had yellowed; now it was green again, green to the end of sight, greenest by the river; the river water was clear; the ice in the river had all melted, and the water flowed bright toward the east.

That evening, when the tents were pitched, Jingfu went alone to the river. From his bundle he took a clay dish, bought in the market at Xizhou, coarse clay, half the size of a palm, about the size of the one at the gate of the Chang'an monastery. He put a lump of mutton fat in the dish, given by Old Sa's family, white, congealed; he twisted a wick from a strand of wool, set it in the fat, struck the flint, and struck three times before it caught; the fat melted, the wick lit; the flame was small and yellow and wavered in the wind by the river.

He set that dish on a flat stone by the river. The stone was not high, about as high as the stone block at the gate of the Chang'an monastery. He squatted, set the dish steady, stood up, and looked at that point of fire. The wind blew from the river; the flame bent to one side, bent down, and stood up again; he squatted and cupped his two hands round it on the windward side, shielding it. Xiaoman came, Ayi came, Old Sa came leading his grandson, and stood behind him watching. It was the first dish of lamp he had lit since coming to the steppe.

On the seventeenth of the eighth month, when the grass tips had already yellowed, Jingfu told Old Sa: this is the day the stele was buried in Chang'an. That day Old Sa's family's tent was packed again—four households, twenty-odd souls, and Jingfu, Ayi and Xiaoman; Ayi's great-aunt had herself helped there too; she didn't believe; she said she'd come to look. Xiaoman and Ayi had baked a great stack of bread that morning; the wheat Ayi had traded for with a pair of silver earrings her great-aunt gave her.

The bread was still unblessed bread. Jingfu broke it, everyone ate, and when they had eaten Jingfu told them about Chang'an, and Ayi translated. He told them that in Chang'an there was a church in Yining Ward; at the church gate was a lamp that burned a hundred years; in the church was a stele with eighteen hundred characters on it, written by his Master; he told of the fifth year of Huichang, when the Son of Heaven sent down an edict that the church was to be torn down and the stele hauled away to pave a canal bank; a Daoist came with the county's document to haul it; the church's people fasted three days; on the evening of the third day a dispatch came from the inspectorate, and the dispatch said: the stele: the leader to dispose of as he judges fit; he told how that night the church's people, the ward's people, a man from a Daoist temple, a copyist from the Secretariat—a dozen-odd people, a dozen-odd spades—dug a pit and put the stele in it, while a blind old man sat under the jujube tree and recited the stele's text from beginning to end; he told how at midnight the wind rose, and he and a guard of the church squatted at the gate cupping their hands round that lamp, cupped it till light, and the lamp did not go out; he told how at daybreak it rained, and the rain smoothed away all the footprints; he told how the Daoist came with the county's Shaofu and asked the stele? and he said buried; the Shaofu asked buried where? and he said in the church; asked further, he wouldn't say; he doesn't lie, and he won't say.

When he got this far, someone in the tent laughed—Old Sa's grandson—and laughing asked Ayi: and the Daoist, after? Ayi asked Jingfu; Jingfu said the Daoist went away, and the county's men wrote in their register: buried, as he judged fit.

He finished. Those in the tent sat; some looked at him, some had their heads bowed; Ayi's great-aunt sat at the door narrowing her half-blind eyes, and asked Ayi: that stone—will it ever be dug up? Ayi translated for Jingfu. Jingfu said he didn't know. He said: that stone is in the earth, its characters whole; one day someone will dig it up and read it and know there was once such a church in Chang'an, and that's enough. Just then the tent's door curtain was lifted; the chief stood in the doorway. He had heard that in Old Sa's family's tent people of the neighbouring tribe and of his own tribe were packed in, and that one from the Tang side was inside telling them something. He stood in the doorway looking at those in the tent, looking at Jingfu, and said a long string; Ayi's face went white, and she translated for Jingfu: he says—he said before, your teaching, recite it yourself, don't recite it in the tribe; what are you telling here today?

Jingfu stood up. He said he was telling of Chang'an: in Chang'an there was a church; in the church there was a stele; today is the day the stele was buried; he was telling how that stele was buried. He said he'd told it, and told the truth; if the chief wants to hear it, he'll tell it again. He said: the chief said don't recite it in the tribe; this tent is Old Sa's, and Old Sa is of the neighbouring tribe; but he knows that today in this tent sit people of the chief's tribe—Ayi is, and her great-aunt too; he won't deny it; if the chief says this counts as in the tribe, he accepts it; he won't come again; if the chief wants to drive him away, he'll go.

Ayi's great-aunt, sitting at the door, spoke then; she said a few words in Uighur, very slowly, and the chief listened. Ayi told Jingfu afterwards that her great-aunt said: the Tang of Chang'an killed our Manichaean teachers; this man was sent out by the Tang of Chang'an; his church was torn down too; his stone was buried too; our people in Chang'an were killed; his people in Chang'an were driven out; what he told today was of his church; listening, it sounded to her like a telling of ours.

The chief stood in the doorway, stood a moment, let the door curtain fall, and left. Before he left he said one thing, which Ayi translated: he says—when you've finished telling, break up; don't tell till dark. Old Sa said: then from now on, every year on this day, let's all eat bread and tell it once. Jingfu said: good.

When it broke up that day, Jingfu came out of the tent; it was still light; autumn had come to the steppe, and it did not yet grow dark early. He stood at the tent's door looking at the grass; the grass had grown to a horse's belly, and yellowed, yellow all the way to the edge of the sky; at the edge of the sky were mountains, and on the mountaintops snow, white. From his robe he took that knotted cord, of wool, its knots told till they shone—Master's, Elder Ahad's, given him by Uncle Lingbao. He told it, one knot, one name.

Pa, Sesame Cake, who died in Huaixi. Master. Uncle Lingbao. Granny Siniang. Brother Joseph, who died at Shazhou. Brother Elijah, Brother Salang. Rijin, Old Mother Kang. Lü Shao. Qushen. Judge Yang. Guangzheng. Xiaoman. Ayi. Old Sa.

When he came to the last knot he said no name; he stood, gripping the knotted cord in his hand. Behind him, in the tent, someone was counting people—Xiaoman, copying what Jingfu did every morning: standing at the tent's door, looking back, counting those in the tent who had not yet gone, counting one by one; when she had counted she told Ayi: nineteen. The wind passed over the grass. The grass lay down patch by patch, lay down and stood up again, from near to far, as though a very great hand were stroking the grass lightly.
