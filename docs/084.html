Volume Twelve　Jade Gate

Chapter 84　Salang

In the second month of the next year, before the date palms on both banks of the Tigris had put out new leaves, they left Baghdad. Matthew saw them to the courtyard gate. The demon-faced board on the gate, rained on all winter, had darkened in colour. Salang, leading the yak under the gate, looked up at the board, and then at the door frame. The frame was of jujube wood, rubbed shiny by hands, and the threshold was worn into a hollow.

"Has the Patriarchate no guard for its gate?" asked Salang. He had learned a few phrases of Syriac this winter and asked it haltingly; Elijah, beside him, filled it out.

"There's a gatekeeper." Matthew lifted his chin at the young man inside the gate. "Him."

"He hasn't a staff in his hand."

"Not allowed," said Matthew. "We who don't hold the Arabs' faith may not wear swords, may not carry weapons, may not ride horses—only donkeys and mules—with wooden stirrups, and two wooden balls nailed behind the saddle, so that people can tell at a glance. A walking stick is allowed; the stick's head may not be shod with iron."

Salang looked at the halter in his hand, and then at the yak.

"And a yak?"

Matthew stood stock still, looking at the yak, and after a while the corner of his mouth moved.

"The order says nothing about yaks."

Elijah laughed, and Lingbao laughed in his pannier too.

"When my grandfather went out of this gate," said Salang, "the staff in his hand—was it shod with iron?"

"No," said Matthew. "The register records it: twenty-four men, and when they went out of the gate, what was in their hands were walking staves. Only when they reached the Tang lands did they shod them with iron."

Salang asked no more. Matthew looked at him and said something more, and Elijah rendered it sentence by sentence.

"The Patriarchate has shed blood too. That was nearly a hundred years ago, when the Patriarchate was still in the old courtyard at Ctesiphon; I heard it from the old deacons," said Matthew. "That year there was a struggle for the patriarch's throne, and the losing house hired an assassin, who stole by night into the archdeacon's room. The Great Worthy's uncle, the archdeacon Shapur, blocked the blow with his right arm and killed the assassin with his left hand; the right arm could not be saved. After that the Patriarchate set itself a rule: at night brothers keep the gate, and no knives in the courtyard. When it moved to Baghdad, the rule moved with it. Now the caliph has set it all over again."

Lingbao, in his pannier, turned his face toward the courtyard.

"Pa spoke of my uncle's arm," he said. "Brother lived in that old courtyard at Ctesiphon, and every morning watched my uncle tie his knot by himself."

"Take care on the road," said Matthew.

Lingbao sat in the sheepskin pannier, his right eye narrowed, looking at Matthew's shape, at that honey colour on his shoulder.

"Secretary Matthew," he said, "that line for Guangzheng—you wrote it well."

Matthew stood at the gate with his hands crossed, stood until they had gone out of the mouth of the quarter.

The shrine on the little hill outside Nishapur was still smoking. This time the caravan rested two days in the city. Salang did not wait for anyone to speak; he handed the yak to Elijah and set off up the hill. Elijah, holding Lingbao up, followed behind, walking slowly, walking a stretch and resting a stretch.

"What colour is the smoke?" asked Lingbao.

"Blue," said Elijah. "Thin, going straight up."

Lingbao tilted his face up and, his right eye narrowed, looked a long while, and saw it: against the greyish-white sky, a faint shadow a little deeper than the sky, standing straight.

"I see it," he said.

At the shrine's door sat an old man dressed all in white—white robe, white cap, and from under the cap a white cloth hanging down over his mouth: the fire-keeper's guard lest his breath defile the fire. The old man saw Salang coming up, stood, blocked the door, and said a sentence in Persian. Elijah came up panting and rendered for both sides.

"This man comes from the east, from Sina, from Chang'an," said Elijah. "He's brought something—the fire of a shrine in Chang'an. The shrine in Chang'an was sealed, the fire went out, and its keeper put the ash in a jar and entrusted it to him to carry west, to a place where there's fire."

The old man looked at Salang, at the earthenware jar on his chest. He pulled the white cloth over his mouth down a little.

"Where is Chang'an?"

"In the east, very far," said Elijah. "Even when the caravans go well, most of a year. We've been on the road five years and more."

"The shrine in Chang'an—who lit its fire?"

"A man of Kang, surnamed Zhu," said Salang, and Elijah rendered it. "The Zhu family brought the fire from Kang and kept it more than a hundred years in Chang'an. It went out on the twenty-third of the eighth month of the fifth year of Huichang; the keeper put it out himself, covering it with an iron plate; he wouldn't let them pour water on it."

The old man heard it out, put his two hands together at his breast, and recited something. When he had recited it he did not move from the door, but held out both hands.

"Into the fire chamber, those of other faiths may not go," said Elijah. "He's telling you to give him the jar; he'll take it in for you."

Salang untied the jar from his chest. The cord was tied in a hard knot; he took several tries to undo it. The oiled cloth over its mouth had been changed twice, once at Kashgar and once at the Stone City; the cloth had been changed, the jar had not, and that bright band round its body that Old Zhu had worn with his hands Salang had worn brighter. The ash in the jar—nearly six years now: from Chang'an to Jingzhou, from Jingzhou to Shazhou, from Shazhou to Xizhou, over the Pamirs, to Balkh, to Baghdad, and walked back again from Baghdad—he had tied to his chest all the way and never once opened it. He held out the jar; the old man took it, held it, turned, lifted the curtain hanging over the door, and went in.

Salang stood outside the door. The curtain hung down; inside the door it was very dark; he could see nothing. He looked up at the wisp of smoke over the shrine. After as long as it takes to recite a psalm through, the wisp of smoke stirred, leaned to one side, and straightened again—thicker than before, and deeper in colour. Salang watched it, watched it go straight up again.

"It moved," said Lingbao behind him. "Salang, I saw it move."

The old man came out with the earthenware jar in his hands; the jar was empty. He gave it back to Salang and said a sentence to Elijah.

"He says the fire has come home," said Elijah.

Salang took the empty jar and held it.

"That man Zhu stayed at Jingzhou, farming at the edge of Tang country, past seventy. He said he couldn't walk to where there's fire and asked me to walk it," he said. "Today I've walked it, but I can't go back to Jingzhou to tell him. If the old man is willing, let him say a line for Old Zhu."

Elijah rendered it. The old man put his hands together at his breast and recited something again.

Going down the hill, Salang did not throw away the empty jar. He wrapped it in the oiled cloth and tied it back on his chest.

"What are you tying an empty jar on for?" asked Elijah.

"I'm used to it," said Salang. "Without something on my chest, I don't walk steady."

At Merv, Elijah went no further.

Beside those two graves of Joseph's family behind the church outside the city, he asked the Metropolitan for a small room, its door facing the burial ground. On the morning they set out he saw them to the east gate of the city, got down from his donkey, came to the yak, and looked up at Lingbao.

"Great Worthy, I'm not going east."

"You've come home," said Lingbao. "Joseph's half—you've walked it to the end for him. From now on you'll stay in Merv and keep watch for him over his Pa and Ma."

Elijah took Lingbao's hand where it rested on the edge of the pannier and squeezed it, and went and squeezed Salang's hand.

"The road east—you know it," he said to Salang. "The speech on the road you can't understand; the Great Worthy understands half. The half the Great Worthy can't understand—look at people's faces."

"I'll look," said Salang.

They reached Balkh in the sixth month of that year. Fahd was waiting outside the west gate of the city; he said every evening he had come to stand a while at the city gate, and had stood a month. Lingbao looked at him from the sheepskin pannier and saw a broad shape with a grizzled beard.

"Fahd, your beard's gone half white."

Fahd stood stock still, looked at Lingbao, then at Salang.

"The Great Worthy can see," said Salang. "The Baghdad physician couched it with a needle."

In the open patch behind the church there was not one weed on Siniang's grave. The mulberry's branches reached over from the other side of the wall, the leaves summer leaves, green to blackness, their shadows falling on the grave in patches, and when the wind blew, the shadows moved. Lingbao knelt before the grave and looked at those moving shadows, looked a good while, before he spoke.

"Siniang, the mulberry isn't beside the grave; it's on the other side of the wall," he said. "I've seen it."

The glass bowl he gave to Priest John.

"This bowl the Son of Heaven granted to my Pa in the first year of Qianyuan. Pa was a man of Balkh; the bowl went with him and was used in Chang'an eighty-odd years. The monastery in Chang'an is gone; the bowl came out, came with me to Balkh, to Baghdad, and walked back to Balkh again," he said. "Pa couldn't come back; the bowl has come back. From now on, at the offering once in seven in the Balkh church, use this bowl. That ring of facets on its base—hold it to the light, and the light comes through the facets and throws ring upon ring of shadow. Pa said so. In Baghdad, I saw it."

That summer he went once more to the east gate. Salang and Fahd held him up and stood him against the wall of the gate passage. He tilted his face up and looked into the city, and saw a stretch of yellow-earth-coloured wall, and above the wall a square shadow, a little deeper than the wall, jutting into the sky.

"That's the watchtower," said Fahd.

Lingbao looked a while.

"Pa's home was under there," he said. "I've seen it. Let's go back."

Before autumn, his body failed day by day. He knew it himself. He died on the ninth of the seventh month of the fifth year of Dazhong. That night Salang kept watch in his room. An oil lamp was lit in the room, its light on his face; he narrowed his right eye and looked at the lamp, looked half the night. In the middle of the night he called Salang, and Salang leaned close.

"Is the road still open?"

"It's open. I've walked it once."

"Then it's well," said Lingbao. "Walk on."

He took the small book from his breast and laid it in Salang's hand.

"This one—take it back to the Patriarchate. Put it with Samaan's ledgers."

Before daylight he went. Salang laid that corner of blue cloth on his breast and folded his two hands together over it. The parchment document Salang put in too, under his hands.

He was buried beside Siniang, on the other side of his grandmother's grave, the side Elder Ahad had said to keep. Priest John presided; the dozen-odd households in the city of Balkh who still believed all came—Persians, Tokharians, Sogdians. They had heard that the man of Milis's family who came from the east had died, and they filled that open patch behind the church.

Fahd cut the stone. Priest John carved the Syriac letters: Adam, grandson of Milis, son of Yazdbozid, priest of the Daqin Monastery of Chang'an; born at Chang'an in the year of the Greeks one thousand and seventy-eight, died at Balkh in one thousand one hundred and sixty-two. Salang crouched beside it with charcoal to write the Tang characters, thought, and wrote three: Yi Lingbao.

When Salang had written them he put the charcoal in his breast, stood up, and went to the wall. A branch of the mulberry reached over the top of the wall, hanging very low. He reached up, picked a leaf, and pressed it in the small book—pressed it under Guangzheng's line, on the line Secretary Matthew had written.

Salang went west alone. That autumn he went out of the west gate of Balkh with a caravan going west, carrying goods for the caravan, earning his food. Fahd saw him to the city gate and said nothing about later; he knew this man would not come back.

At Merv he stopped a day and went to see Elijah behind the church outside the city. Elijah was sitting in the sun at the door of his small room. When he saw him he did not ask about Lingbao; he looked at the empty jar on his chest, at the bundle on his back, and knew. The two of them stood a while before those two graves of Joseph's family. That afternoon he bought a staff in the Merv market, of jujube wood, shoulder high. The seller asked whether he wanted the head shod with a band of iron; shod, it would wear well.

"No," said Salang.

"When the Great Worthy went, he asked whether the road was still open," said Salang. "I said it was."

"It's open," said Elijah.

He passed Nishapur again. On the little hill outside the city the wisp of smoke was still there, thin and straight. This time he did not go up. He stood by the road facing that wisp of smoke a while, felt the empty jar on his chest, and went on.

He reached Baghdad in the spring of the next year. He went into the quarter of the Romans and from far off saw a knot of people round the gate of the Patriarchate's courtyard. Coming closer, they were a few government men, one with a rope, one with an armful of wooden stakes, measuring along the courtyard wall section by section, and at each section driving a stake into the ground. Matthew stood at the gate talking with a man in an official's robe, a roll of documents in his hands, talking very urgently. The young gatekeeper stood inside the threshold, his beard grown full, his face white.

Salang learned only later that the caliph's order of the year before last had one more article: of the houses of those not of the Arabs' faith, one in ten was to be taken and made into a prayer hall of the Arabs. The government men had come to measure the Patriarchate's courtyard.

That day he did not ask. He set down the bundle on his back, went to the courtyard gate, and stood outside the threshold, beside Matthew, his two feet apart, as wide as the door frame. In his hand was only that wooden staff, its head without iron. He did not raise it; he planted it on the ground.

The government man with the rope, measuring as far as the courtyard gate, meant to pull the rope through the gate to measure how deep the courtyard was. Salang did not give way. The man pushed him; he did not move. Pushed him again; still he did not move. He did not look at the man. He looked at the well inside the gate.

Hold the gate, and you needn't fight. His Pa had said it, and his Pa had heard it from his grandfather.

The man in the official's robe looked at Salang a while, looked again at the roll of documents in Matthew's hands, and said something to the government men. They took up the rope, pulled up the few stakes driven in by the gate, and left.

Matthew rolled up the documents, his hands still shaking.

"Hunayn had someone in the palace write it," he said. "Today it's of use; tomorrow, who knows." Only then did he turn his face and know Salang. "Salang?"

Salang's Syriac was what he had learned along the road from Elijah—not much, enough to say these few sentences.

"The Great Worthy Lingbao has gone," he said. "At Balkh. Buried beside his grandfather, beside his wife."

Matthew stood at the gate; the string of knots in his hand stopped.

Salang took the small book from his breast and handed it to him. Matthew took it, opened it, turned to Guangzheng's page. The mulberry leaf slid out from the page, dried, yellow, and fell on the threshold. Matthew bent and picked it up, looked at it a while, put it back in that page, on the line he himself had written, and closed the book.

"The Great Worthy said: put this one with Samaan's ledgers," said Salang.

"With them," said Matthew.

Salang stood at the gate and did not go.

"My grandfather went out from here, a monastery guard," he said. "The Patriarchate's gate—I'll keep it."

Matthew looked at him, at the empty jar on his chest, looked a good while. He stepped aside and told the young gatekeeper to go in and bring out a stool.

That evening there was a low stool at the gate of the Patriarchate's courtyard, and Salang sat on it, leaning on the door frame, the empty earthenware jar tied to his chest, across his knees a wooden staff he had bought in the Merv market, shoulder high, its head not shod with iron.
