Volume Six　The Shuofang Army

Chapter 29　Liu Heng

The next day Yisi was up before light.

He did not put on the purple robe. He wore his old grey robe, its cuffs worn to a fuzz. The purple robe he took out of the chest, folded, wrapped in a piece of blue cloth, and tucked under his arm. No reply had come from the Patriarchate to this day, and he had never once worn it.

Jingjing stood at the door of the side room watching him, and asked what he was taking that for. Yisi said the memorial asked for it to be taken back; if he was going to be questioned, he had better bring the thing; if they wanted it, they could take it there and then, and there'd be no need for another trip. Jingjing wanted to ask something more, but Yisi was already out of the gate.

The Ministry of Rites was in the Imperial City: in through the Zhuque Gate and east, row upon row of galleries, and in the galleries room after room. A minor clerk led Yisi there, and he stood under the eaves half an hour before he was called in.

This time the Bureau of Sacrifices of the Ministry of Rites was questioning him jointly with the Court of State Ceremonial. The Bureau of Sacrifices had charge of all the monasteries and temples in the empire; the Court of State Ceremonial had charge of foreign visitors from every quarter, and monks from abroad came under it too. Three men sat in the room. The one in the middle was a little over fifty, thin, his face very white, his eyelids half lowered, both hands folded in his sleeves, as though he were dozing; the clerk who had brought Yisi in said low in his ear that this was Director Liu of the Bureau of Sacrifices, Liu Heng, who would conduct the questioning today. The one on the right was from the Court of State Ceremonial; Yisi had once gone there on business and met him, but could not remember his surname. The one on the left was about forty, square-faced and stiff-bearded, sitting bolt upright: the censor who had sent in the memorial. He was the accuser, and had come today to sit in too, and most of the questions would likely be his. By the wall stood an old Sogdian in hu dress, from the Sabao's office, which had charge of the fire-worshipping hu in Chang'an. When Yisi came in, the old man raised his eyes and looked at him once, and lowered his head again.

Yisi made his bow and stood. No one told him to sit.

The censor spoke first, asking question after question. Asked whether he was Yisi; asked whether the Daqin monastery in the Yining Ward was under his charge; asked whether the eleven who had killed a man in the Liquan Ward were men of his monastery. Yisi answered each in turn: this humble monk was Yisi; the affairs of the monastery were under this humble monk's charge; the eleven were all baptized men of this humble monk's monastery.

The censor had perhaps not expected him to answer so plainly; he paused, and only then asked: "Does your religion teach men to destroy other men's shrines and put out their incense?"

Yisi said it did not.

"It does not," said the censor. "Then where did they learn it?"

Yisi thought a while and said: "They learned it in this humble monk's monastery. In the monastery they heard the stories in the scriptures, heard them and remembered them, but remembered them wrong, and this humble monk did not keep watch on them. The men went out of this humble monk's monastery, and those few buckets of water too were drawn from the well of this humble monk's monastery."

For a moment the room was still. The man from the Court of State Ceremonial raised his head and looked at him; Liu Heng's eyelids moved, and did not open. A trace of a smile appeared on the censor's face, and he said: so, you admit it. Yisi said: that much, this humble monk admits.

The censor asked again: "Do your scriptures have words teaching men to kill those of other religions?"

This time Yisi was silent longer. He thought of Baaluo standing at the door of the side room, water dripping from his hair, saying that Elijah had killed four hundred and fifty of the prophets of Baal. He thought a long while before he said: "The scriptures have stories of killing; they are things from long, long ago, and this humble monk dares not say there are none. But the Lord in whom we humble monks believe never in his whole life killed one man. He was put to death by men, nailed to wood, and as he was dying, he still prayed for those who nailed him."

The censor looked at him as if waiting for him to go on. Yisi did not go on.

The censor took up the memorial from the table and read a passage, the few lines he had written himself: the hu religions were not the law of the Middle Kingdom to begin with; today they dared kill a man of a fire-temple; tomorrow what would they not dare? When he had read it, he put the memorial down, looked at Yisi, and asked him, since he had received the purple from the Son of Heaven, to say for himself whether he was still fit to wear it.

Yisi brought out the blue cloth bundle from under his arm, untied it, and, holding the purple robe in both hands, laid it on the table. He said: "Whether this humble monk is fit is not for this humble monk to say. This humble monk has never once worn this garment, and has brought it today. If you would take it, please take it back."

The censor was taken aback, and looked at the robe. It was folded neatly, lying among a heap of documents.

It was then that Liu Heng spoke.

Liu Heng spoke very slowly, and very softly; everyone in the room had to lean forward to hear him clearly.

He asked the censor what the code said. The censor, frowning, asked him which code. Liu Heng said: the code on fights and suits. Whoever kills a man in a fight, by strangling; when several plot together and beat a man, the one whose hand was heaviest bears the heaviest guilt, the one who first proposed it is reduced one degree, the followers another degree. He said these were the articles he remembered; the censor had read the code more closely than he, and if he had remembered wrongly, he begged the censor to point it out. The censor said nothing.

Liu Heng said again: "The code says: whose hand did the deed, his is the guilt; those who gave the counsel, those who followed along, are reduced degree by degree. I turned the pages all night, and did not find one article saying that when a man has killed someone, the temple where he worships is to be pulled down."

The censor said the hu religions were different. Liu Heng asked him how different. The censor said: the hu religions are not the law of the Middle Kingdom to begin with.

Liu Heng nodded, as if he found the remark very reasonable. He drew one hand out of his sleeve and took up from the table a roll of old paper. The paper was yellow, its edges brittle. He said this was an old record of the Court of State Ceremonial, which he had borrowed yesterday: in the twelfth year of Zhenguan, the Emperor Taizong had issued an edict that spoke of this very monastery, which in those days was still called the Persian monastery, and was only renamed the Daqin monastery in the fourth year of Tianbao. He unrolled the paper, narrowed his eyes, and read it out word by word:

"The Way has no constant name; the sage has no constant form. Teachings are set up to suit each place, and quietly save the multitudes."

When he had read it, he rolled the paper up, put it back on the table, and said slowly: "What the Emperor Taizong permitted, if the censor would abolish it, I dare not stand in the way. Only, the censor's memorial must have one sentence added, saying this edict of the Emperor Taizong was wrong. Add it, and I will put my name to it along with the censor."

No one in the room spoke. The man from the Court of State Ceremonial looked down at his own hands; the old Sogdian from the Sabao's office watched Liu Heng without blinking. The censor's face slowly reddened.

Liu Heng folded his hand back into his sleeve, and said there were two more things, which he had also happened on yesterday. One was an old case of the Metropolitan Prefecture: in the spring famine of the first year of Qianyuan, when the granary gate of the East Market was opened to give out rice, a dozen-odd people were trampled to death, even the Prefecture's Bureau of Revenue clerk among them; afterwards rice was given out west of the Qujiang Pool, six thousand people, in blocks of fifty, sitting to receive it, and not one fell, and the man whose idea it was, was this hu monk. The other was a merit report of the Ministry of War: in the battle at the Xiangji Temple in the ninth month of the second year of Zhide, the ambush cavalry in the ditch to the east were first seen by a scout under Lord Guo's command, and that scout was this man, who that day took an arrow in the back, one in the waist, and one in the face.

He paused, and said: "These two things have nothing to do with today's case. I only happened on them, and mention them in passing." He glanced at the purple robe on the table, and said again: "As for this garment, it was the Son of Heaven's gift. What the Son of Heaven has granted, the Bureau of Sacrifices cannot take back, nor can the Censorate. We three here today are to deliberate on the code alone."

When he had finished, he lowered his eyelids again, as though he had gone back to dozing.

That day's questioning came to no result. The censor said he would memorialize again; the man from the Court of State Ceremonial said he would go back and ask his superiors' instructions; the old man of the Sabao's office said not a word. When Yisi was called out, the purple robe was still on the table; he got to the door and looked back. Liu Heng, without opening his eyes, said: "Take it. If it stays here, I'll have to mind it for you." Yisi went back, picked up the robe, wrapped it, and tucked it under his arm again.

Afterwards the Metropolitan Prefecture's judgment came down, much like the code Liu Heng had read out that day. The butcher's son of seventeen, whose hand had been heaviest, was sentenced to strangling, and never came out of the prison again. Baaluo, who had given the counsel and led the men there, was reduced one degree: sixty strokes of the heavy rod and exile to Lingnan. The other nine, who had followed along, were reduced another degree, three years' penal servitude each, and after a year and more an amnesty came and they were released. Of the nine released, six afterwards came back to the monastery; the other three left, and no one knew where they went.

On the day the news reached the monastery that the butcher's son was to be strangled, Jingjing, in the felt tent in the back courtyard, found the wooden board the boy used to write characters on. The character keep on it was still there, square and true, written the winter before last. Jingjing sat a while holding the board, did not wipe it, and put it back where it had been.

Yisi came out of the Department of State Affairs and walked toward the Zhuque Gate, and halfway there someone behind him called him; he turned and looked, and it was Liu Heng. He was alone, without attendants, and came slowly up with his hands in his sleeves, and led Yisi round into a narrow lane behind the galleries, where there was an old locust tree, and no one under it.

"You must be wondering," said Liu Heng, "why I spoke for you."

Yisi said this humble monk was indeed wondering. Liu Heng said his grandmother's surname was Kang; she was from Kang; his family had lived in Chang'an four generations and long since become Tang. When he was small, every year in the twelfth month his grandmother would take him to the back door of the fire-temple in the Liquan Ward to beg a little new fire to bring home; nor did they go by the front gate; they begged it and went. The one who opened the back door was an old man who each time would stroke his head and bring out a sweet from his sleeve for him. Here Liu Heng paused, and said: "It was the keeper of the fire."

Yisi bowed his head and made him a bow. Liu Heng did not return it, but turned and, hands in his sleeves, walked slowly back the way he had come. After a few steps he turned his head and said: "Go to the fire-temple tomorrow. When you go, don't speak of your Lord."

Yisi said this humble monk would not.

The next day, Yisi went to the Liquan Ward.

Four went with him. Haddad spoke Sogdian and went to interpret. Ishaq brought medicines, and a bundle of dry flatbread too. Gabriel had asked to go himself; Baaluo had been baptized by him. Jingjing went too, carrying a bag of money Babai had counted out the night before. Babai had counted what could be spared on the monastery's books three times over, counted it out coin by coin and bagged it, and when he gave it to Jingjing said he had made an entry in the books, and when Jingjing came back he must make an entry too, and write clearly who it was given to. When the few households of old Luminous believers in the Liquan Ward heard Yisi was coming, two of them waited at the ward gate, and led them toward the fire-temple, their heads bowed all the way. At the shrine gate one of them told Yisi that since it happened, the Sogdians of the ward turned their faces away when they saw them; his child had gone to the well to draw water and had his bucket kicked over; they had lived in this ward decades, and did not know how they would face anyone from now on. Yisi said he would come often from now on.

Narsai went with them too, but at the gate of the fire-temple he stopped, and said he would not go in; it was a place where idols were served, and the Law did not permit it. Yisi looked at him once and did not press him. Narsai stood at the foot of the wall outside the gate. The sun was strong that day; he stood there the whole time and did not move from the spot.

The one who received them in a small room in the front courtyard was an old Sogdian merchant who managed the shrine's affairs. The shrine-master who had stabbed his belly with the knife that day had been hired for the festival and had left when it was over. The manager was sixty or so, with no expression whatever on his face; he did not ask them to sit, nor have tea brought. Haddad explained why they had come, and when he had heard it he nodded, had someone take the bag of money Jingjing carried, and having taken it did not open it to look. There were four injured, all lying in the rooms at the back: one with a broken arm, one with a broken head, two struck by buckets, their legs one great bruise. Ishaq went from room to room to see them and put medicine on them. The young man with the broken arm cursed the whole time, in Sogdian; Ishaq could not understand it, and nodded as he dressed the arm, as though the man were thanking him; Haddad, listening beside them, had a rather odd look on his face, and did not translate for him.

The old fire-keeper's family were an old woman and a boy of twelve or thirteen, the old man's grandson. The old woman was very old, badly bent, and sat on a low stool, both hands on her knees, looking at Yisi. Yisi stopped before her, bent, and bowed deeply. When he bowed, his back was to the basin of fire in the courtyard.

The old woman looked at him a long time before she spoke, in Sogdian, very slowly, and Haddad beside him translated sentence by sentence. She asked why they had wanted to put out the fire. Yisi thought, and said they had believed that putting out this basin of fire would be good for their Lord. Haddad translated it, and the old woman looked at him a long time again, and said: "This fire, his family's forebears brought from Kang. He kept it forty years, and in forty years never let it go out one day. The day he died, he didn't let it go out either."

Yisi kept his head bowed and listened as Haddad translated it sentence by sentence. The old woman got up with a hand on the wall and went slowly into the inner room; at the door she turned and said one more thing. Haddad did not translate it, and Yisi did not ask.

The boy had stood to one side the whole time, watching Ishaq. Ishaq took a dry flatbread from his bundle and held it out to him; the boy looked at the bread, looked at the door of the inner room, put out his hand and took it, and held it tight, and did not eat it. Jingjing stood behind Ishaq, about the boy's height. As he took the bread the boy raised his eyes and looked at Jingjing once; Jingjing wanted to say something to him, but did not know what to say, nor whether to say it in Tang or in Sogdian.

When they came out of the fire-temple, it was already past noon. Narsai was still standing at the foot of the wall; the sun had beaten on him all morning, and his forehead was covered in sweat. Under that high brow those stone eyes looked at Yisi, and he asked what the people inside had said. Yisi stood at the gate and did not answer at once, but looked back once. Through the crack of the gate the stone platform in the middle of the courtyard could still be seen, and the bronze basin on it. The fire in the basin was very small, almost invisible in the full sun; only the layer of heat above it made the wall behind it waver.

Yisi turned back and told Narsai that the old woman said the fire had been brought from Kang, and kept forty years. When he had said it, he set off toward the ward gate. Jingjing followed behind, and after a few steps looked back at Narsai. Narsai was still standing at the foot of the wall; only after a while did he follow.

The day Baaluo set out was in the twelfth month. Yisi went outside the Chunming Gate to see him off.

The rod wounds on his back had not yet quite healed, and he walked with a limp, a cangue round his neck, the two escorts following him one in front and one behind. His white clothes were long gone; he wore a drab grey prisoner's garment, dirty and thin. But he did not walk slowly at all, and when he saw Yisi he stopped while still a long way off and grinned.

Yisi handed him a water-skin; the water had been boiled, and was still warm. Baaluo took it and did not drink, but first asked how the boy was. Yisi did not answer. Baaluo asked no more, but bowed his head, and his lips moved a few times, as though he were praying. When he had prayed, he raised his head; the eye that had been swollen was no longer swollen.

"Master Yi," he said, "you needn't grieve for me. The Dharma King Baolu too went on his road in chains, and preached all the way as he went, preached all the way to the edge of the sky. Lingnan is beyond the pale of civilization; no one has ever gone there to speak the name of the Lord. The Lord is sending me there to do this thing. Once I go, I won't come back."

Yisi opened his mouth and did not know what to say to him. He had never been to Lingnan, but he knew that there were Guangzhou and Guizhou there, prefects and county magistrates, and more hu merchants in the city of Guangzhou than in Chang'an; it could not be called beyond the pale. He did not say it aloud. He stood a while, then turned and went to the escort in front, took a small string of cash from his sleeve and pressed it into his hand, and said low that the wind was strong on the road, the man had wounds on his back, and he begged the two of them to look after him a little on the way, and at the inns to let him have something hot to eat. The man weighed the cash in his hand, tucked it into his coat, and said: understood.

Baaluo saw it. His face flushed red all at once, and he shouted at Yisi, shouted till several people by the roadside turned to look: "Master Yi! What are you doing! What I suffer, I suffer for the Lord; there's no need to buy it off with money! You take benefactors' money and recite the scripture of safety for benefactors, and now you take money to buy smiles from the escorts—how is that any different from those who worship the Stove God and burn paper money! I don't want your money, and I don't want your hot food!"

Yisi stood there and did not answer him. The escort behind gave Baaluo a shove and said: move. Baaluo turned round, and in his cangue, limping, went off along the road toward Lantian, and as he walked began to sing a psalm in Syriac, not quite in tune, but very loud, so that even when he was far off it could still be faintly heard. Yisi stood by the roadside and watched him go. The water-skin Baaluo took with him after all.
