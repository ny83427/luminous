Volume Ten　Morning Praises

Chapter 68　He Qi

In the first month of the fifth year of Kaicheng, the Son of Heaven died.

People in the city said the Son of Heaven had died of vexation. After the year of the Sweet Dew he could not decide one thing in the palace; the eunuchs set up whom they pleased. The Son of Heaven sat in his hall like a piece of wood, sat five years, sat until he fell ill, was ill a winter, and in the first month was gone. The eunuchs set up the Son of Heaven's younger brother, the Prince of Ying. The Prince of Ying took the throne, the city hung white, the wards were forbidden feasting and music, and the wine shops of the West Market shut their doors a month. By the old custom recorded in Samaan's ledger, the monastery recited the scriptures once in the hall for the late emperor: when a Son of Heaven dies, the monastery recites scripture—the psalms—and does not recite the Son of Heaven's name.

That year twenty-three stood at the morning. In the twelfth month two old believers had died: one an old man who ran a vinegar works at the east end of the ward, one the old woman of the last of the seven households back from the south of the city. When she died she had a string of knots gripped in her hand, brought back from the south of the city, told for forty years until the knots shone. Jingfu laid the string in her coffin, and it was buried with her.

He Qi came back from Liangzhou in the second month. This time he had been on the road most of a year—to Liangzhou, to Ganzhou, to the Uighur borders—and had bought a load of goods: wool, horsehides, a few bolts of Uighur brocade, and a small chest of jade passed along from Khotan. He said this trip had doubled his capital, but he did not mean to go again. He had come back to sell off the shop.

He told his sister first. That night he came to Lingbao's house with a leg of mutton and two bottles of grape wine. He Siniang boiled the mutton, and the three of them sat in the room. Liu'er was two and walking on the floor, a few steps and down she sat, and up again, and she walked to He Qi's feet and hugged his leg, and he lifted her onto his knee and fed her a piece of mutton.

"Sister, I'm going back to Xizhou."

"Going back to Xizhou for what? Xizhou isn't our home," said He Siniang. "Our family's lived in Chang'an three generations. Pa was born in Chang'an; Grandfather's generation came here."

"I know. But the He ancestors came from Xizhou, and there are still He clansmen in the city of Xizhou. These years going west, I've stayed in Xizhou twice. The clan acknowledge me and tell me to come back. Xizhou is under the Uighurs now. The Uighurs trade, and Sogdians keep their accounts, interpret, lead their caravans for them; life is good," said He Qi. "Chang'an's finished."

"How is Chang'an finished?"

He Qi set Liu'er down and drank a mouthful of wine.

"Sister, you don't go out; you don't know. The Tang in Chang'an look at hu with different eyes. When I was small Pa took me walking in the West Market; when the Tang saw a hu merchant they called him hu-sir, and when they'd haggled over a price they'd slap him on the shoulder. Now when the Tang in the West Market see a hu, they say nothing, but there's something in their eyes," he said. "These years the Uighurs trading in Chang'an, relying on the Uighur khagan being nephew and uncle to the Son of Heaven, have swaggered through the West Market—owe Tang people money and don't pay, beat Tang people and don't pay for it—and the Metropolitan Prefecture can't do anything. The Tang hate the Uighurs, and hate them till they can't tell Uighurs from Sogdians, or Sogdians from other hu; anyone with a high nose and deep eyes, they hate. Last autumn a Tang cake-seller at the south end of the West Market quarrelled with a Uighur merchant over money and had his arm broken by the Uighur, and that same evening the Tang of the West Market surrounded the Uighur warehouse and smashed half a street. It wasn't only Uighurs they smashed; Sogdian shops got smashed too. Our shop had a hole knocked in its door planks. I wasn't there; the two lads braced it with the gate bar and didn't let them break in."

He Siniang said nothing.

"And that's a small thing," said He Qi. "This time in Liangzhou I heard the Uighur country's in turmoil. Last winter there was a great snow and more than half the cattle and sheep froze to death, and the khagan and his chief minister fought; the minister killed the khagan and set up another, and some of the tribes ran south and some ran west. With the Uighurs in turmoil, the Uighurs in Chang'an will have it harder still. If the khagan can't control his own tribes, whether the Son of Heaven still acknowledges this uncle-and-nephew business is hard to say. And when the Uighurs fall, the Tang of Chang'an, who've hated the Uighurs for decades, won't hate only Uighurs." He lowered his voice. "The new Son of Heaven's only been on the throne a month, and already it's said in the city that when he was Prince of Ying he loved the Daoist arts and kept company with Daoists in his residence. The Daoists are mortal enemies of the Buddhist monks, and they won't necessarily be any kinder to hu temples. Sister, that monastery of yours, that Great Worthy of yours—from now on their days won't be easy."

He looked at his sister.

"I've made up my mind. I'll sell the shop to a Tang silk merchant of the West Market—the very man Wang who wanted to marry you that year. The Wang family's offering a fair price. When the shop's sold, I'll take my family with a camel caravan going to Liangzhou next month, to Liangzhou, then on west, through Ganzhou, through Shazhou, to Xizhou," he said. "Sister, come with me."

"I won't go."

"I knew you'd say you won't go. I didn't come tonight to hear you say you won't go," said He Qi. "You're forty-nine, the Great Worthy is seventy-three, and his eyes are gone. You keep by him, and when he's gone you'll be alone in Chang'an, a hu widow keeping a hu temple, its people fewer every year—who will you lean on then? Come with me. In Xizhou there's the clan, there's a house; when you're old, the clan's nephews will look after you and see you to your grave; when you die you'll be buried in the He family's ancestral grave, and every year the clan will burn paper for you."

"I don't want anyone burning paper for me," said He Siniang. "I'll keep by the Great Worthy, and when the Great Worthy's gone, I'll keep the monastery. If the monastery's people are fewer, I'll stand there; one standing is one. What you say—the house in Xizhou, the clan's nephews, the He ancestral grave—I know they're all good. But they aren't mine."

"Then what is yours?"

He Siniang reached down and picked up Liu'er, who was sitting on the floor, held her in her arms, and wiped the grease from her mouth with her sleeve.

"This is mine," she said. "This child the Shi Second found under a willow outside the Jinguang Gate. Her Ma died, who her Pa is no one knows, where she's from no one knows. But she's here, in this room, in this ward, and every morning I carry her to the monastery and she stands beside Mother Kang watching everyone stand, and so she belongs here. So do I. I married once, and my husband died at sea and not even his body came back, and the uncles said I was keeping faith with a dead man. What I keep faith with is here."

He Qi drank a mouthful of wine, set down the cup, and turned to look at Lingbao. Lingbao had been sitting to one side all along, his eyes unable to see clearly, leaning his head forward, listening to the brother and sister talk. At this he set down the tea bowl in his hand.

"Great Worthy, then you—come with us. In Xizhou there are Luminous monasteries, one north of the city and another outside it; there are priests and monks there, their Syriac better than Chang'an's, more scriptures than Chang'an has. If the Great Worthy went, he wouldn't be the only one any more," said He Qi. "And further west, over the Pamirs, is Balkh. The Great Worthy's Pa was a man of Balkh; the Great Worthy's grandfather is buried in Balkh; and the Great Worthy in all his life has never once gone to see."

"I've never been outside the Guannei Circuit," said Lingbao. "Seventy-three, and the farthest I've been is Xianyang—that time to give the last communion to an old believer who was dying. I went for a day and came back. My eyes can't see; I need someone to hold my arm to walk. Tell me to walk to Xizhou, walk to Balkh, and I'd die by Liangzhou."

He paused a while.

"My Pa was a man of Balkh; I know it. My grandfather is buried in Balkh; I know that too. When Brother was ten he went once, and came back and told me that Grandfather's and Grandmother's graves were behind the church in Balkh, side by side, with a mulberry tree beside the graves. I want to go. I can't," he said. "He Qi, you go. When you go, stand a while in the church in Xizhou for me. At the offering once in seven, stand at the back; when they break the bread, take a piece, and count it as my having gone too."

He Qi looked at him, looked at his sister again, at Liu'er in his sister's arms. He urged no more, picked up his wine cup, and drank what was left.

The shop was sold in the third month. On the day the Wangs came to take it over, He Qi handed over the ledgers one by one, and when he had handed them all over stood a moment at the shop door. He had had the hole in the door plank mended, and the patch of wood was newer in colour than the rest; you could see it at a glance. He came out of the shop, did not look back, and came to the Yining Ward, to the monastery to find Jingfu.

That day Jingfu was picking over rice in the back courtyard. He Qi crouched to help him pick, and picking, began to speak.

"Director, what I said to my sister that night—the Director has probably heard. There's no future for hu in Chang'an," he said. "I dare say this to the Director because the Director is a hu's son too. The Director's Pa was a man of An, Sesame Cake of the Shence Army, who died in Huaixi. The Director was born in Chang'an, grew up in Chang'an, speaks Tang better than the Tang. But let the Director go walking in the West Market, and what the Tang look at is the Director's eyes and nose; the Director's Tang, they don't hear."

"I know," said Jingfu. "When I was small, people in the ward street shouted hu brat at me. The ones who shouted came later to draw gruel at the shed, and when they drew it they called me Your Reverence."

He went on picking rice and did not raise his head.

"He Qi, you say there's no future for hu in Chang'an. I won't argue with you. The road you're taking I can see, and what you say is all true. But I'm the Director, and the Director has no road going west. The Director's road is this monastery gate. Where the monastery gate is, there I am."

"And if there's no monastery gate any more?"

Jingfu's hands stopped in the rice. "Then I'll stand where there's no gate. The morning, I'll stand just the same."

He Qi put the handful of rice in his hand into the basket, stood up, and dusted off his hands.

"Before I go, I ask the Director one thing. This trip west, the road goes through Shazhou and across the Moheyan desert; on that stretch, one or two caravans in ten come to grief. Before I go I'd like the monastery to bless my caravan once."

"Blessing is the priest's work. I can't bless."

"I know. I've come to ask the Director to bring out the Great Worthy."

Lingbao came on He Siniang's arm.

That morning He Qi's caravan was waiting in the open ground outside the Kaiyuan Gate, a dozen-odd camels in a line, the packs all tied, the camels kneeling and chewing hay, the drivers sitting by the packs eating flatbread. He Qi's family—his wife, two sons and a daughter, the younger son only seven, sitting up on a small camel looking about him. Not many had come from the monastery: Jingfu, Joseph, Elijah, Qushen, Salang, the Shi brothers, and a few old believers from the ward.

Lingbao stood before the caravan. He could not make out the line of camels, only a row of dark shapes. He raised his hands and recited in Syriac the psalm for those setting out: "The Lord shall preserve thy going out and thy coming in from this time forth, and even for evermore." When he had finished he recited it once in Tang, very slowly, and when he reached from this time forth, and even for evermore, his voice went hoarse.

He Qi came before him, knelt and knocked his head to him once, rose, and went and stood before his sister, and opened his mouth.

"Sister."

He Siniang took a small cloth bundle from her breast and pushed it into his hand.

"There's a wooden cross in it, your brother-in-law's from that year. Before he boarded the ship he left the cross at home and said it was damp at sea, a cross carried there would rot easily; he'd wear it when he came back. He didn't come back, and I've kept the cross ten-odd years," she said. "You carry it. You're going by land, and the land isn't damp."

He Qi gripped the bundle and put it in his breast. "Sister, remember. If one day you want to go, go west, to Xizhou, and ask for the He family—He Qi. Every Sogdian in the city of Xizhou knows me."

"I'll remember."

The camel bells rang. The camels rose one after another, back legs first, then front legs, the packs swaying a few times and settling. He Qi mounted the first camel, turned his head and looked back at the Kaiyuan Gate, at the little group of people standing under the city gate, and turned his head away. The caravan went off west, the bells growing distant beat by beat. By the great road going west outside the Kaiyuan Gate there was an earth mound-marker, and on the marker was written West to Anxi, nine thousand nine hundred li. The Four Garrisons of Anxi had been lost for decades; the marker was still there, its characters blown illegible by the wind. The caravan passed beside the marker, went on to the edge of the sky, and could not be seen. Beyond the Long Mountains, Liangzhou and Shazhou were both in Tibetan hands, and the caravan would have to pay tax at the Tibetan checkpoints. He Qi said he had gone through three times; once the Tibetans had taken the money, they let people pass.

He Siniang walked Lingbao back on her arm, and in the gate passage Lingbao asked her, "That marker—is it nine thousand nine hundred li that's written on it?"

"I don't know. I don't know that many characters."

"I can't see either," said Lingbao. "When I was small Brother took me out of the city and pointed to that marker and read it to me. Brother said that the year he was ten he went west from here, to Balkh, to the Patriarchate, and didn't walk the full nine thousand nine hundred li, but it was far enough. So far that by the time he came back Pa's beard had gone half white."

That autumn, news from the west came into the city of Chang'an every so often. First it was said that a great Uighur general who had a feud with the khagan had brought in the Kirghiz. The Kirghiz were a tribe very far to the north, tall, red-haired, green-eyed; a hundred thousand horsemen of them broke into the Uighur royal tent, killed the khagan, and burned the city. What that city was called, few in Chang'an could say; they knew only that it was north of the desert, on the steppe, and the Uighurs had lived there nearly a hundred years. Later it was said the Uighurs had scattered, into several branches: one ran west, to the Anxi and Beiting country; one ran south, to Tiande and Zhenwu, to beg a mouthful of food beneath the walls of the Tang frontier towns.

Of the Uighur warehouses in the West Market, several closed in those months. Some said the owners had gone north overnight to find their own tribes; some said the owners didn't dare go, and hid in their warehouses without going out, while outside the gates Tang young men stood all day long, peering in. These were all rumours; they reached the Yining Ward, the ward talked of them once, and then talked no more.

There was never any word of He Qi. He had left in the third month; if the road went well he would reach Xizhou by summer. But how things stood now at Xizhou, in the Uighur country, no one knew. He Siniang came to the morning every day as always, standing on the women's side with Liu'er in her arms. When merchants came from the west, she went to the West Market to ask once, and when she had asked came back and cooked the meal as always. Lingbao did not ask either.

One day in the twelfth month it was snowing at the morning. Jingfu stood in the middle of the courtyard, and when the sky greyed he looked back and counted: twenty-one.

Under the jujube tree Lingbao stood telling a string of knots. It was his brother's, of wool, which Elder Ahad had given his brother in his day under the mulberry tree at Balkh; his brother had told it all his life and died with it gripped in his hand. He told it, a name for each knot: Pa, Ma, Brother, Elder Yonan, A-Lian, Elder Narsai, Elder Samaan, Siyuan, Hanan, Old Cao; and when he had told the dead, told the living: Jingfu, Joseph, Elijah, Qushen, Liu'er, Siniang, Guangzheng.

At the last knot he said: "He Qi."
