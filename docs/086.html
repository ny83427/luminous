Epilogue　The Steppe

Between the night Jingfu lit the first dish of mutton fat by that river and a snowy night by the river Tuul lie another hundred and sixty years. A hundred and sixty years, in Chang'an, is the business of several generations. Huang Chao entered the city; Zhu Wen pulled down the palaces, and the timber of Chang'an floated down the Wei to build new halls in Luoyang, and the Tang fell. The Central Plain passed through five dynasties and a dozen-odd emperors; in the end there was a Song in the south and a Liao in the north; the two fought twenty-odd years, tired of it, and swore a covenant at Chanzhou—one to send silk, one to receive it—and that was the end of that. Yining Ward had long since been outside the city walls; on the foundations of that monastery were fields, ploughed who knew how many times, sown with who knew how many crops of wheat; the stele still lay under the felt and the earth, and no one knew; it had another six hundred years and more to lie there. These things the people of the steppe did not much care about. What the people of the steppe cared about was grass, and snow, and horses, and whether this winter the sheep would freeze to death; they kept years, not reign-names; what they kept was which year the snow was deep, which year the horses sickened, which year which tribe fought which, and whose son did not come back; the names of the emperors in the south, by the time they reached here, had long since gone out of shape, and spoken aloud sounded like the cry of some other kind of bird.

That year, in the north of the steppe, about the river Tuul, there was a great tribe—many people, many horses, many sheep—their tents on both banks of the river joined together so that riding from morning to night you did not reach their end; later people said this tribe was the Kerait of the histories. The Kerait's king that year was thirty or so, just at the age to ride and shoot his best; after the start of winter he took a dozen-odd men into the mountains to hunt; the first day they shot a few gazelles; the second day they chased a stag whose antlers branched seven times, and which ran like wind skimming the grass-tips. The king's blood was up; he spurred his horse alone at the front, chased it over a ridge and into a valley; he did not catch the stag; the snow came. It was the steppe's kind of snow: first wind, from the north, scouring along the ground till a man could not open his eyes; then snow, and the snow did not fall either but flew sideways, striking the face like sand flung by the handful; in the time of a meal the sky was dark and the ground was white, sky and ground run together into one, so that up could not be told from down. The king turned to call his men; his men had long since scattered; his shouts were torn to pieces by the wind as soon as they left his mouth, so that even he could not hear them; he meant to blow his horn; the horn was frozen and would give no sound.

He rode on through the snow, not knowing where he was going, and neither did the horse. A steppe horse knows the way home; but in such snow even the horse did not know; at last the horse would go no further, and stood with its head down, the white breath from its nostrils freezing to ice splinters as it left them, a thick layer of snow piled on its back like a little grave it was carrying. The king got down and walked on himself, leading it by the reins, and thought: most likely he would die here. Every winter on the steppe people died like this; the next spring when the snow melted they were found in some gully or at the edge of some wood, man and horse frozen together, hard as two stones; he had seen it several times; one of his father's brothers was brought back so, still gripping the reins. He walked on a stretch; his legs were no longer his own; and then he saw a point of light.

That point of light was very small, small as a star fallen to the ground, now there, now not, in the snow; when the wind was strong it leaned and sank as if about to go out; when the wind eased a little it stood up again—still that one point, not large, and not going out. The king led his horse toward that point of light; that stretch of the way, when he thought of it later, always seemed to him to have taken a whole night; in fact it was only a few li. When he came up to it he saw clearly: it was a wooden post, chest-high, set in the snow, and on top of the post a clay dish with a chipped corner, and in the dish mutton fat, and in the fat a wick, burning a little tuft of fire, the flame yellow with a touch of blue at its root, quivering in the wind, a ring of congealed fat round the dish's edge. Behind the post was a tent, very small, very old, its felts covered with patches and patches on the patches, half buried in snow, icicles hanging from its door curtain. He lifted the curtain, bent, and went in.

Inside the tent it was dark, and warm; dry dung burned in the hearth, and the smoke went out through the smoke hole at the top. By the hearth sat an old woman, a man of middle age, and two children, the elder a girl and the younger a boy, the boy holding a little lamb in his arms; they saw a man covered in snow, ice hanging in his beard, squeeze in from behind the curtain, and all started, and the lamb bleated. The king said his name; the middle-aged man sprang up at once, his face white, not knowing where to put his hands and feet. The old woman did not get up; she sat by the hearth, lifted her eyelids and looked him over from head to foot, looked very ordinarily, as at a herdsman who had lost his way; then she sent the girl to ladle a bowl of hot milk, told the man to stir up the fire, and then go out and lead this man's horse to a place out of the wind and cover it with a felt. The king sat by the hearth holding that bowl of hot milk and drank it mouthful by mouthful; there was salt in the milk, and it burned the mouth; the snow on him melted and ran down his robe onto the felts; his fingers warmed bit by bit—first pain, pain like needles, and only then feeling. He raised his head and saw something hanging on the pole in the middle of the tent.

It was a cross made of wood, not large, a span or so long, tied with a leather thong, hung at the height of a man's eyes. The wood had long gone black, black and shining, as though touched by many hands for many years; where the two pieces crossed a notch had been cut in each and the two fitted together, with no nail and no cord; its four ends were whittled round, like four leaves. The king asked the old woman what it was. The old woman said it was for protection; the king asked: protection against what? She thought, and said she didn't know; her Ma said so, and her Ma's Ma said so too; it had come down many generations; the family's tent had been changed several times, moving all the way east from the foot of the Tianshan in the west; the sheep had died off several times; this thing had always hung on the pole; when they moved it was the first thing taken down, and when they arrived the first thing hung up. She stood up, took the cross down from the pole, and handed it to him. The king turned it over; on the back of the cross was cut a tiny dot, cut very shallow, and worn by hands all these years so that it could hardly be seen; he asked what this dot meant; the old woman shook her head and said no one could read it any more; she knew only that it must not be rubbed smooth.

She sat back down by the hearth and began, slowly, to tell. She said when she was small her Ma's Ma was still alive, already blind, and one winter, when the snow was just as heavy, she told her once that long, long ago, when their family was still at the foot of the mountains in the west, a man came from beyond the mountains to the south, with a face you couldn't say was hu or Tang; he learned their speech for several years and still got it back to front, and the children laughed at him; he couldn't ride well either, and was always sliding off his horse's back; but he could carve this; one summer he carved a great many, and whoever came to ask, he gave one to; on the back of each he cut a dot. When a child was born he went and said a name over the child and drew with his thumb on the child's forehead; when someone was dying he kept watch beside them and recited; what he recited no one remembered any more; by the time it came down, only one phrase was left. The king asked which phrase; the old woman held the cross in both hands, bowed her head, and said it. What came out was a few sounds; they sounded like moran, and like etrahan, and trailed off into something more, slurred, that could not be made out; it was not the speech of the steppe, nor the speech of any side the king had heard; the king could not understand it, nor could the old woman herself; she knew only that when you said these sounds, you had to draw with your hand. She drew—first down, then across—drew it crooked. The king looked at that hand of hers, a hand that had herded sheep all its life, its knuckles thick, the cracks of its nails black, drawing slowly in the firelight a stroke down and a stroke across, and he did not know why, but something in his heart moved.

He asked whether that lamp outside had been left by that man too, and whom it was lit for on snowy nights; the old woman nodded, looking at him as if she thought the question a little foolish, like asking why people drink water; she said on snowy nights it was always lit; there are people travelling at night. That night he slept in that tent, by the hearth, covered with the family's thickest sheepskin; in the middle of the night he woke once and heard the wind still roaring outside, the tent's felts bellying in and out in the wind; he thought of the post at the door and the dish of mutton fat on its top, and wondered whether it had gone out; he lay listening to the wind, listened, and at last fell asleep again.

The next morning the snow had stopped; the sky was clear and so blue it was like a sheet of ice; the steppe lay white as far as the eye could see, and when the sun shone on it, it was too bright to open one's eyes. His men came looking for him, a dozen-odd, their faces purple with cold, calling his name all the way, treading a deep trench in the snow; they stood outside the tent while the family's man led the king's horse out from its shelter; they looked at that wooden post, at the chipped clay dish on top of it; the fat in the dish had burned dry, and the wick had burned to a little black stub. Someone said that last night, far off, they had seen this point of firelight, and at first took it for a wolf's eye; the snow was too heavy to move in; at daybreak they came looking in that direction. The king mounted and rode away; when he had gone a long way he looked back; the tent was as small as a stone buried in the snow; a little further, and the tent could not be seen; only the wooden post still stood there, one thin stick, one black dot.

What came after, the histories recorded a little of. A history written by a Syrian says that in that year the king of a tribe on the steppe lost his way in the snow and, about to die, was led out; when he went home he sent for the merchants who travelled the steppe and questioned them; among the merchants were some who believed in this teaching, and they told him of it—told of a Lord who came from heaven, told of the cross, told of baptism; he heard and said he would believe; and the merchants wrote westward on his behalf, to the metropolitan of the city of Merv; the metropolitan of Merv received the letter and wrote further west, to the Patriarch at Baghdad. Before the metropolitan of Merv wrote his letter, he turned through the old registers his predecessors had left in the scripture room, wanting to see whether the steppe had ever had any of their own people before, and came to a page from more than a hundred and fifty years earlier, its paper yellow, its ink faded, on which a few lines were recorded: that there was a leader of the Chang'an church who had never had hands laid on him, who took two children and crossed the Tianshan with the Uighurs and went north, and after that there was no news. The metropolitan wrote in his letter that this tribe's king wished to be baptized together with his people, two hundred thousand of them; and asked the Patriarch one thing more: that on the steppe no wheat was sown—there was no flour, no vegetables; the people ate only meat and milk; when the great fast came, by the rule neither meat nor milk might be eaten—what were these people to eat? The Patriarch wrote back: no meat; milk they may drink.

Two hundred years more, and in the royal tents of the Kerait the cross was kept generation after generation; when the king's daughters married into other tribes, they took the cross with them; when the king's descendants went to war, there was someone in the tents holding the scriptures and reciting those few phrases that no one could quite understand. Later still a great man arose on the steppe who joined all the tribes into one and rode all the way to the edge of the sky; that man's daughter-in-law was a princess of the Kerait, and in her tent too a cross hung.

The day the king left, the old woman in that tent sat by the hearth till noon. She hung the cross back on the pole, hung it straight, and felt with her hand the dot on its back. The boy, holding his lamb, squeezed in beside her and asked whether that man would come again; she said she didn't know; the boy asked again: he's a king—why would a king get lost too? She said: when the snow is heavy, anyone gets lost. Toward evening the sky clouded over again; the wind rose from the north, and the snow on the steppe began to fly again. She came out of the tent, went up to that wooden post, took down the clay dish, tipped the burnt dregs onto the snow, dug a fresh lump of mutton fat out of a skin bag and put it in the dish, pulled a thread from the hem of her robe, rolled it in her palm into a wick, and set it in. She struck the flint, struck three times before it caught; the flame rose, blue first, then yellow, leaned in the wind and stood up again; she put the dish back on top of the post, stood and watched till it burned steady, and turned and went back into the tent.

(The End)
