Volume Eleven　Huichang

Chapter 69　The Royal Tent

In the autumn of the fifth year of Kaicheng it was rumoured in the city that the Son of Heaven had summoned Daoists into the palace.

It was said a Daoist named Zhao Guizhen, who had been in the palace once before in Jingzong's reign and been driven out after Jingzong died, had been invited back by the new Son of Heaven—he and his band of eighty-odd—all summoned into the palace to hold a Golden Register rite in the Three Halls, and the Son of Heaven himself went to receive the registers of the Law. When the people of the West Market spoke of it their eyebrows danced: what Zhao Guizhen looked like, how much he ate at a meal, that he did not kneel when he saw the Son of Heaven; that he told the Son of Heaven the Buddha was a hu god of the west, that the monks in the Buddhist monasteries ate the Tang's rice and wore the Tang's cloth and paid no tax and did no service, and the money of all the realm had been eaten by monks.

The monastery's people heard it and made nothing of it.

"Daoists and Buddhists have been fighting for hundreds of years," said Joseph. "Every time Chang'an changes its Son of Heaven, who's in front changes once. This time the Daoists are in front; next time the Buddhists will be in front again. It's nothing to do with our monastery."

Lingbao was seventy-three that year. He sat in the sun at the scripture-room door while Qushen read him the accounts.

"Whether it's to do with us or not, I don't know," said Lingbao. "I only know Zhao Guizhen says the Buddha is a hu god of the west. Our Lord came from the west too."

That year those at the morning dropped from twenty-one to twenty. When winter set in, an old believer at the east end of the ward moved with his son to the Liquan Ward; the son had found work in the West Market. On the day he left he came to stand the morning at the monastery one last time, and when he had stood it, said to Jingfu, "I'll still come to the offering once in seven after this. The Liquan Ward is two streets from the Yining Ward; I can walk it." He came twice after that, and the third time he did not come.

Salang lost his work early in the spring of the first year of Huichang.

He had carried loads twenty years at a Uighur warehouse in the West Market. The owner of the warehouse was an old Uighur, past fifty, who had lived twenty years in Chang'an, married a Tang wife, had three children, spoke Tang more fluently than Salang, and smiled at everyone, his two eyes narrowing to slits when he smiled. He was not a bad man; he paid full wages and at festivals gave an extra sheng of rice; only he reckoned accounts tight and would not be out by a single coin.

Last autumn, when news reached Chang'an that the royal tent had fallen, the old man sat a day and a night in the back yard of the warehouse without eating or drinking, and the next morning stood up and counted every last thing in the storeroom—wool, horsehides, Uighur brocade, ironware—and when he had counted them, called Salang and a few other porters.

"I'm going to shut up. The royal tent's gone, the khagan's dead, the tribes have scattered. From now on there's no one on the steppe to buy my goods, and no one to send me goods either. How long am I to sit in Chang'an keeping a roomful of things?"

He shut up all winter, and didn't manage to. When spring came, it was rumoured in the city that after the Uighurs scattered one branch had come south, a hundred thousand and more, and pitched camp outside the walls of the Tiande garrison, saying they had come to throw themselves on the Son of Heaven, asking for grain, asking for a place to live, and saying their khagan was the Son of Heaven's nephew, and the Princess Taihe was still with them. When the old man heard, he opened the warehouse door again.

"Wait and see. Perhaps the steppe can still be put to rights; perhaps the Son of Heaven still acknowledges the uncle and nephew."

The last time Salang went to the warehouse was in the second month. He arrived in the morning; the warehouse door was open, and the old man was sitting on the threshold with a cloth in his hand, scrubbing the door planks. Someone had written a few characters on the planks in charcoal. Salang couldn't read them, nor could the old man; a Tang cake-seller next door told them: Uighur hu, get out.

The old man scrubbed all morning and couldn't get it clean. The charcoal had been soaked by rain and sunk into the wood; it scrubbed white, and the characters were still there. Scrubbing, he said to Salang, "You needn't come tomorrow."

"What will you do from now on?"

"I don't know," said the old man. "My wife is Tang; my three children were born of a Tang mother. If I go north, there's no home for me on the steppe any more; if I stay in Chang'an, the people of Chang'an tell me to get out. Twenty years I've lived in Chang'an, and it's the first time anyone's written on my door."

His Tang wife came out of the back yard, forty or so, big-handed and big-footed, a farm girl from Fanchuan outside the city; twenty years ago, when she married a Uighur, her own family said she'd gone mad. She carried a basin of water. She snatched the cloth from the old man's hand, soaked it, and went on scrubbing, harder than the old man, cursing as she scrubbed—cursing the one who had written it, a curse and a scrub. The three children stood inside the door, the eldest seventeen, the youngest only eight. The eldest was a youth with a Tang face and Uighur eyes, standing with his fists clenched. His mother turned and told him to go in, and he would not.

That day the old man reckoned Salang's wages for the last time, reckoned them as tight as ever, not one coin out, and when he had reckoned them gave an extra sheng of rice.

"Take it. Same as at the festivals every year. From now on, no more festivals."

Salang carried the rice out of the warehouse, and at the street corner looked back. The Tang woman was still scrubbing, scrubbing the door plank to a patch of white, and the characters still showed through the white.

He came back to the monastery and told Jingfu.

"That youth had his fists clenched, and mine were clenched too. I very nearly went over and grabbed that cake-seller."

"You didn't grab him. That was right."

"I know it was right. My Pa guarded the monastery gate all his life, and he told me: if you hold the gate, you needn't fight. My grandfather Sargis said the same," said Salang. "I didn't grab him. But all the way back I was thinking: if that youth asked me where I'm from, in the end, I couldn't say either. My grandfather came from the Patriarchate, and where the Patriarchate is, I've never been. I was born in Chang'an, but when the people of Chang'an look at my face, what they see is a hu."

That year the monastery's income fell by half. Jingfu went over the accounts with Lingbao once, Qushen reading, Lingbao listening, Jingfu setting out counting rods on the table.

Before, half the monastery's income had come from the gifts of believers in the ward and the West Market, and half from the alms of the hu merchants of the West Market—Sogdians, Persians, and Uighurs too. The Uighurs believed in Mani, not in Aluohe, but they traded in the West Market alongside the Sogdians, and when the Sogdians gave alms they gave some too, saying it was for the gruel shed at the monastery gate; the gruel was for the poor to eat, and the poor didn't divide by what they believed. With He Qi gone, the He family's share was gone; with half the Uighur warehouses shut, the Uighurs' share was gone; some of the Sogdian shops had shut too, some had gone west, some to Luoyang, and those who stayed found business hard, and gave less.

Qushen read an entry and paused. Lingbao heard it through.

"Half what it was last year, three-tenths less than ten years ago."

"What about the gruel shed?"

"The gruel shed Brother set up in the West Market in the Dali years, moved to the ward street in the fourth year of Zhenyuan—two pots, seventy-odd years, and it's never stopped a single day," said Lingbao. "I don't know what to do. Fu'er, you're the Director. You say what to do."

"Cut the pots first. Two pots down to one, one pot a day, the rice in the pot the same as before, no extra water. Better one pot fewer than one pot thin," said Jingfu. "And I'll go looking for people again. The Mi aide-de-camp, Lü Shao, the Public Revenue people—borrow what can be borrowed, ask for what can be asked for."

"The Mi aide-de-camp died the year before last. His son's in the Shence Army; he doesn't keep up with us much."

"Then I'll go to his son."

The Mi aide-de-camp's son was in his thirties, also in the Shence Army, a squad commander, living in that great house three courtyards deep in the Buzheng Ward. The house was still the same house; half the place where horses had been tethered in the front courtyard was empty. He received Jingfu in the front hall, courteously, had tea brought, and heard Jingfu out.

"When my Pa was alive he often spoke of the Daqin Monastery—said my great-grandfather Lord Mi and Master Jing of the monastery were friends for life. As a child I went with my Pa to a few offerings once in seven too, sat at the back, dozed," he said. "The Director has come, and I ought to help. But the way the wind blows in the army now, the Director has probably heard. The Son of Heaven believes in the Daoists; the Commander of the Shence Army is a palace man, and the palace men all run to the Daoist temples now. In the army some are saying the Mis are hu, the Mis keep up with a hu temple. I've been a squad commander five years and can't rise. In my Pa's time he was aide-de-camp; in my generation, someone else sits in the aide-de-camp's seat."

He had a servant bring a bolt of silk.

"This bolt is my own. Take it, Director; count it for the gruel shed. After this I can't give any more. And the Director had better not come again. If he comes and the army's people see him, it won't be good for the monastery, and it won't be good for me."

Jingfu took the bolt of silk and thanked him with crossed hands. When he left, the young man saw him as far as the second gate, not to the front gate.

From the third month of that year the gruel shed made one pot a day. The first few days, those queuing in the ward street queued twice as before, and at the second queue the pot was empty, and those at the back stood there, not leaving, looking at the empty pot. An old woman wept.

"Come a little earlier tomorrow," said Jingfu, standing by the pot. "Tomorrow there'll still be a pot."

"Your Reverence, will there come a day when there isn't even one pot?"

"I don't know. So long as the monastery has rice, the pot will be open."

Lu Shouchong came that autumn.

He was a Daoist, forty or so, thin and tall, long-faced, a goatee on his chin trimmed very even, wearing a new blue Daoist robe of fine stuff, with a yellow sash at his waist and a piece of jade hanging from the sash. He brought two young Daoists, one holding a coil of rope and one holding a register. He stood at the monastery gate and looked first at the lamp by the gate, then at the cross-shaped hollow chiselled into the lintel, looked a good while, and only then sent a young Daoist in to announce him.

Jingfu came out to see him.

"This humble Daoist is from the Tongzhen Temple in the Jincheng Ward. The abbot has sent me to have a look."

"A look at what?"

"At the ground," said Lu Shouchong. "The Tongzhen Temple is one street from the Yining Ward. The temple's halls were built in the Kaiyuan years, small and old. Now that Master Zhao has won the Son of Heaven's favour in the palace, the Daoist temples of the city are all to be enlarged, and the Tongzhen Temple too. But the ground in the Jincheng Ward is all taken up, and there's nowhere to enlarge into. The abbot heard there's a hu temple in the Yining Ward with a lot of room and few people, and sent me to see how much of the monastery's ground is in use and how much stands empty."

"All the monastery's ground is in use."

Lu Shouchong smiled. "Don't be alarmed, Director. I've only come to take a look. Looking does no harm."

He had the young Daoist with the rope uncoil it and give one end to the other, and the two of them measured from the monastery gate—to the front courtyard, to before the hall, to the back courtyard, to the well curb—calling out the figures as they went, and Lu Shouchong wrote them in the register. The monastery's people had all come out and stood in the gallery watching. Joseph went to stop them, and Jingfu shook his head. Salang stood inside the gate holding a wooden staff his grandfather had left—shoulder high, iron-shod—its foot planted on the ground, his hand gripping the shaft until the knuckles were white.

When they had measured as far as the stele in the front courtyard, Lu Shouchong told the young Daoists to stop. He went up to the stele himself and tipped his head back to look. He looked from the cross at its head, to the two dragons, to the nine characters of the heading, and then down, line by line, his lips moving, reading. At one place he stopped and turned and called Jingfu.

"Director, this line—what does it mean?"

Jingfu went over. The line was: When the virtue of Zhou declined, the blue chariot ascended into the west; now that the Way of the great Tang shines, the Luminous wind blows east.

"My Master wrote this," said Jingfu. "It says that when the virtue of the Zhou declined, Lord Lao rode his blue ox away west; now that the Way of the great Tang shines bright, the Luminous wind blows from the west to the east. My Master wrote this line to say that Lord Lao went west in his day, and our teaching now comes from the west—going east and west, it's the same Way. The meaning on the stele honours Lord Lao."

Lu Shouchong shook his head.

"What the Director says is his Master's meaning; what this humble Daoist reads are the characters on the stele. The characters on the stele are arranged so: Lord Lao went west because the virtue of Zhou was lost; once Lord Lao left, the central lands had no Way; and then your Luminous wind blew in and filled the empty place. That is to say: after Lord Lao left, the Way in the central lands is what you gave." He put his hands behind his back. "In the Jianzhong, Zhenyuan, Yuanhe years, no one saw this line for what it was; or if they saw it, said nothing; in those days the Sons of Heaven honoured you. Now the Son of Heaven honours Lord Lao, Master Zhao is in the palace, and the Son of Heaven receives the registers every day. If the Son of Heaven saw this line on the stele, if Master Zhao saw it, what it would mean—the Director can think about that himself."

Jingfu stood before the stele, his two hands hanging.

Lu Shouchong left the words lying there, turned, and told the young Daoists to go on measuring. When they had measured it all, they coiled the rope, and he totalled the figures in the register.

"The monastery's ground, front and back, left and right, this humble Daoist has measured it all. How much the hall takes, how much the courtyards take, how much stands empty—it's all written down. I'll go back and report it to the abbot. What the abbot thinks, I don't know. Today I've only come to take a look."

He saluted with crossed hands, walked to the monastery gate, and turned back to look at the stele.

"The characters on the stele—the stone remembers them, and people remember them too. Isn't that so, Director?"

He left. Jingfu stood before the stele until the shadow of the gallery crept up its foot. Lingbao had Qushen help him out.

"What did that Daoist say?"

Jingfu told him. Lingbao reached out to touch the stele, felt his way to that line, felt those characters—the blue chariot ascended into the west. He could not see; he felt them stroke by stroke with his finger.

"This line—when Brother wrote it in the scripture room in his day, I was beside him grinding the ink. When Brother had written it, he read it to Pa, and Pa said it was a good line: Lord Lao went west, we come from the west, the road is one road," he said. "When Brother wrote this line, he never thought that one day someone would read it this way."

"If the Master were here, what would he say?"

"If Brother were here, he'd say: the characters are his, the meaning is his; what others read them as is their affair; he won't change them," said Lingbao. "But Brother isn't here. You are."

That night the two of them sat in the scripture room deliberating over that line.

"Standing before the stele, I was thinking whether that line could be covered with something," said Jingfu. "Chiselling it off won't do. The characters on the stele mustn't be chiselled; chisel them, and the monastery itself has accepted Daoist Lu's reading. What I'm thinking is to put up a wooden board before the stele, or hang a cloth, and cover the lower half of the stele, so outsiders who come can't see that line, and when this wind has passed, take it down. The monastery can't stand any trouble now. One sentence, one line, taken into the palace by the Daoists, is a crime. The stele is dead; people are alive. Protect the people first."

Lingbao sat at the table and heard him out, and shook his head.

"I can't see the characters on that stele any more, but I can feel them. Every line on the stele I can recite with my eyes shut. When I was small Brother wrote the stele and I ground the ink; Pa carved the Syriac letters and I handed him the chisels. The day that stele was set up, Pa stood before it and read it from beginning to end; it took him an hour," he said. "If you cover the lower half, it isn't only that line you cover. Below it are Pa's name, the patriarch Hnanisho's name, the Syriac letters Pa carved. Hang a cloth over it, and what you hang is the face of the whole monastery. Daoist Lu came today with a rope to measure the ground. What he wants is the ground; that line is the pretext he's found. Cover this line and he'll find the next; the stele has eighteen hundred characters, and he'll always find one."

"Then leave it, and wait for him to take this line to the palace and denounce us?"

"I don't know. I know only one thing: Pa never lied all his life, and Brother never changed a character all his life. The characters on the stele are true words. Cover them, and the monastery has shown itself guilty," said Lingbao. "You're the Director. If you want to cover it, I can't stop you. But on the day you cover it, I won't go to the stele to feel it any more."

Jingfu did not raise it again. He got up, went to the gate to light the lamp, lit it, and came back.

"Then we won't cover it," he said. "But if Daoist Lu comes again, I have to have thought out how to answer. The Master's meaning—I have to say it clearly, say it so Daoist Lu can't refute it. Today I said it badly, said half, and let him take it from me."

"You said it well enough today," said Lingbao. "In his day Brother argued with Elder Samaan over the characters on the stele for several nights, and to his death Elder Samaan never fully came round. But the stele was set up, and not one character was changed."

That night Salang came to find Jingfu.

"Director, I've lost my work. The Uighur warehouses in the West Market aren't taking people, and the Sogdian shops don't want porters either. I'm forty-five. I've still got strength, but no one wants it," he said. "I want to ask the Director one thing. I want to come back to the monastery. The register business I don't understand; I'm a lay believer; I just want to come back and keep the gate—keep it at night, and by day too. My grandfather guarded this monastery's gate, my Pa guarded it too, and with me it's been broken twenty years. I want to take it up again."

He held the wooden staff crosswise in his hands.

"Today, while that Daoist was measuring the courtyard with a rope, I stood inside the gate gripping this staff, and what I was thinking was: if one day people come, and what they carry isn't a rope, I have to be here."

"The monastery has no money to pay you wages. Two meals a day, and at night you sleep in the west wing."

"That's enough."

From that day Salang kept watch inside the monastery gate at night. He brought a low stool and set it inside the gate against the door frame. At dusk, when Jingfu had lit the lamp by the gate, he sat down beside the lamp and laid the wooden staff across his knees.
