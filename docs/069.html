Volume Eleven　Huichang

Chapter 69　The Royal Tent

In the autumn of the fifth year of Kaicheng it was rumoured in the city that the Son of Heaven had summoned Daoists into the palace. It was said that a Daoist called Zhao Guizhen, who had once been in the palace under Jingzong and was driven out after Jingzong died, had now been invited back by the new Son of Heaven, and he and his band of eighty-odd were all summoned into the Forbidden City to hold a Golden Register rite in the Three Halls, and the Son of Heaven went himself to receive the registers of the Way; the palace people said the Son of Heaven had loved such things as Prince of Ying—alchemy, breath-feeding, cultivation—and now that he was Son of Heaven he loved them more. The people of the West Market, talking of it, were all eyebrows and sparkle: what Zhao Guizhen looked like, how much he ate at a meal, that he didn't kneel to the Son of Heaven, that he told the Son of Heaven the Buddha was a hu god of the west, and the monks in the Buddhist monasteries ate Tang rice and wore Tang cloth, paid no tax and did no service; all the money in the empire was being eaten by monks.

The monastery's people heard it and took no account of it. Joseph said the Daoists and the monks had been quarrelling for hundreds of years; every time Chang'an changed Sons of Heaven it changed who was in front; this time the Daoists were in front, next time the monks would be in front again; it had nothing to do with our monastery. Lingbao was seventy-three that year, his eyes unable to see; he sat at the scripture room door in the sun while Qushen read him the accounts beside him; hearing Joseph say this, Lingbao said: whether it has to do with us or not he didn't know; he knew only that Zhao Guizhen said the Buddha was a hu god of the west, and our Lord too came from the west.

That year the number at morning devotion fell from twenty-one to twenty; at the start of winter an old believer at the east end of the ward moved with his son to Liquan Ward, saying Liquan Ward was nearer the West Market and his son had found work in the West Market. The day he left he came to the monastery and stood morning devotion one last time, and when he had stood it told Jingfu that he'd still come to the offering once in seven; Liquan Ward to Yining Ward was two streets apart; he could walk it. He came twice after that, and the third time he did not come.

Salang lost his work at the opening of spring in the first year of Huichang. He had carried goods twenty years in a Uighur warehouse in the West Market; the warehouse's owner was an old Uighur past fifty who had lived twenty years in Chang'an, married a Tang wife, had three children, spoke Tang more fluently than Salang, and smiled at everyone, his eyes narrowing to slits when he smiled. Salang said the old man was not a bad sort; he paid wages in full, gave an extra sheng of rice at festivals; only he reckoned accounts tight, not one coin wrong. Last autumn, when news reached Chang'an that the royal tent had fallen, the old man sat a day and a night in the warehouse's back courtyard without eating or drinking; his wife brought him food and he pushed the bowl aside; the next morning he stood up and counted the whole roomful of goods item by item—wool, horsehides, Uighur brocade, ironware—and when he had counted them called Salang and a few porters and said he was going to close. He said the royal tent was gone, the khagan was dead, the tribes had scattered; from now on there'd be no one on the steppe to take his goods and no one to send him goods; guarding this roomful of things in Chang'an—how long should he guard it?

He closed for a winter and did not manage to close. At the opening of spring it was rumoured in the city that after the Uighurs scattered, one band had come south, a hundred-odd thousand people, and reached the walls of the Tiande garrison, and pitched camp, saying they had come to submit to the Son of Heaven, asking the Son of Heaven for grain and a place to live, and saying too that their khagan was the Son of Heaven's nephew-in-law and the Taihe Princess was still with them, and the princess was the Son of Heaven's aunt. Some in the West Market said they came to submit, some said they came to plunder, saying that when Uighurs came to submit they always came to submit with swords. The old man heard it and opened the warehouse door again, saying he'd wait and see; perhaps the steppe could still be put back together; perhaps the Son of Heaven would still acknowledge the nephew-in-law.

Salang said his last time at the warehouse was in the second month. He arrived in the morning; the warehouse door was open, and the old man sat on the threshold with a cloth in his hand, scrubbing the door-board. Someone had written a few characters on the door-board in charcoal; Salang couldn't read them, and neither could the old man; a Tang cake-seller next door told them: it said Uighur hu, get out. The old man scrubbed all morning and couldn't get it clean; the charcoal ash had been soaked by the rain and sunk into the wood; he scrubbed it white and the characters were still there. As he scrubbed he told Salang: you needn't come tomorrow. Salang asked what he'd do now; he said he didn't know; his wife was Tang, his three children were born of a Tang woman, he was a Uighur; if he went north, there was no home for him on the steppe now; if he stayed in Chang'an, Chang'an's people told him to get out. He said he'd lived twenty years in Chang'an and this was the first time anyone had written on his door. That day the old man's Tang wife was in the warehouse too, forty-odd, big-handed and big-footed, a farm girl from Fanchuan outside the city; twenty years ago, when she married a Uighur, her family said she was mad. She came out of the back courtyard carrying a basin of water, snatched the cloth from the old man's hand, soaked it in the water, and went on scrubbing, scrubbing harder than the old man; as she scrubbed she cursed—cursed whoever wrote it, one curse, one scrub. The old man's three children stood inside the door, the eldest seventeen, the youngest only eight; the eldest was a young man with a Tang face and Uighur eyes; he stood with his fists clenched; his mother looked back and told him to go in, and he would not. Salang said that day the old man reckoned his wages with him for the last time, reckoned as tight as ever, not one coin wrong, and when he'd reckoned gave him an extra sheng of rice, as at festivals in other years, and said: take it; after this, there won't be any festivals. Salang carried that sheng of rice out of the warehouse, and at the street's mouth looked back: the Tang woman was still scrubbing the door-board, scrubbing it white all over, and those few characters still showed through the white.

He came back to the monastery and told Jingfu. Jingfu said: you were right not to go after that young man. Salang said he knew he was right; his grandfather, who guarded the monastery gate all his life, had told him: hold your ground and there's no need to fight; and his grandfather's father, Sargis, had said the same. He said he didn't go after him, but all the way back he kept thinking: if that young man asked him where he was really from, he couldn't say either; his grandfather's father came from the Patriarchate; where the Patriarchate was, he'd never been; he was born in Chang'an; but when Chang'an's people looked at his face, what they saw was a hu.

That year the monastery's income fell by half. Jingfu reckoned the accounts once with Lingbao, Qushen reading, Lingbao listening, Jingfu laying out counting rods on the table. Formerly half the monastery's income came from the gifts of believers of the ward and the West Market, and half from the donations of the hu merchants of the West Market—Sogdian, Persian, and Uighur too; the Uighurs believed in Mani, not in Aluohe, but they traded in the West Market alongside the Sogdians, and when the Sogdians gave, they gave something too, saying it was for the gruel shed at the monastery gate; gruel was for the poor to eat, and the poor weren't sorted by what they believed. With He Qi gone, the He family's share was gone; half the Uighur warehouses had closed, and the Uighurs' share was gone; some Sogdian shops had closed too, some had gone west, some to Luoyang; those who stayed found trade hard, and gave less. Qushen read that year's accounts, read an entry, paused; when Lingbao had heard it all he said: down by half on last year, down three tenths on ten years ago. Jingfu said: what about the gruel shed? Lingbao said the gruel shed was set up by Brother in the West Market in the Dali years and moved to the ward street in the fourth year of Zhenyuan, two pots, more than seventy years, and had not stopped a single day; he didn't know what to do; he asked Jingfu: you're the leader; you say what to do.

Jingfu said: first cut the pots. He said: cut two pots to one, one pot a day, the rice in the pot the same as ever, no water added; better one pot fewer than one pot thinner. He said too he'd go and find people again: the Mi officer, Lü Shao, the Department of Public Revenue—borrow what could be borrowed, ask for what could be asked. Lingbao said the Mi officer had died the year before last; his son was in the Shence Army and had little to do with them. Jingfu said: then he'd go to his son.

He went. The Mi officer's son was thirty-odd, also in the Shence Army, made a squad commander, living in that great three-courtyard house in Buzheng Ward; the house was the same house, but half the place in the front court where horses were tethered stood empty. He received Jingfu in the front hall, courteously, had tea brought, heard Jingfu out, and said that while his Pa lived he often spoke of the Daqin Monastery, said his great-grandfather Lord Mi had been friends all his life with the monastery's Great Worthy Jing; as a child he too had gone with his Pa to the offering once in seven a few times, sat at the back, and dozed. He said: the leader has come; he ought to help; but the leader has probably heard which way the wind blows in the army now: the Son of Heaven believes in the Way; the Shence Army's commandant is a palace man, and the palace men all run to the Daoist temples now; there's talk in the army that the Mis are hu, that the Mis keep company with a hu monastery; he'd been a squad commander five years and couldn't rise; while his Pa lived he was an officer of the guard; in his own generation, the officer's place was taken by someone else. He said every word of this was the truth. He had a servant bring a bolt of silk, and said: this bolt is his own; let the leader take it, for the gruel shed; after this he couldn't give again, and the leader shouldn't come again either; if he came and the army's people saw it, it would be bad for the monastery, and bad for him. Jingfu took the bolt of silk, clasped his hands and thanked him; when he went out, he was seen to the second gate, not to the main gate.

From the third month of that year, the gruel shed had one pot a day. The first few days, those queuing in the ward street queued twice as before; when it came to the second round the pot was empty; those at the back stood and did not go, looking at the empty pot; an old woman wept. Jingfu stood by the pot and told them: come a little earlier tomorrow; tomorrow there's still one pot. The old woman asked him: Great Worthy, after this, will there not even be one pot? Jingfu said he didn't know; he said so long as the monastery had rice, the pot would be open.

Lu Shouchong came that autumn. He was a Daoist, forty-odd, tall and thin, his face long, a tuft of goatee on his chin trimmed very neat, wearing a blue Daoist robe, the robe new, of fine stuff, a yellow sash at his waist and a piece of jade hanging from the sash. He brought two young Daoists, one holding a coil of rope, one holding a register. He stood at the monastery gate and looked first at the lamp at the gate, then at the cross-shaped hollow chiselled in the lintel, looked several times, and only then sent a young Daoist in to announce him.

Jingfu came out to see him. Lu Shouchong clasped his hands and said he was from the Tongzhen Temple in Jincheng Ward; the abbot had sent him to have a look. Jingfu asked: a look at what? Lu Shouchong said: at the land. He said the Tongzhen Temple was in Jincheng Ward, a street away from Yining Ward; the temple's hall was built in the Kaiyuan years, small and old; now that Master Zhao had won the Son of Heaven's favour in the palace, the Daoist temples of the city were all to be enlarged, and the Tongzhen Temple too; but the land of Jincheng Ward was all occupied; there was nowhere to enlarge it; the abbot had heard there was a hu monastery in Yining Ward, a big place with few people, and sent him to look—to see how much of this monastery's land was in use and how much stood empty.

Jingfu said the monastery's land was all in use. Lu Shouchong smiled and said: don't fret, Leader; he's only come to look; looking does no harm. He had the young Daoist with the rope undo it and hand one end to the other, and the two of them began to measure from the monastery gate, measured to the front courtyard, to before the hall, to the back courtyard, to the well curb, calling out figures as they measured, while Lu Shouchong recorded them in the register. The monastery's people had all come out and stood under the colonnade watching; Joseph wanted to go and stop them; Jingfu shook his head. Salang stood inside the gate holding a wooden staff, left by his grandfather's father, shoulder-high, iron-shod; the staff's head rested on the ground, and his hand gripped its shaft till his knuckles whitened.

When they had measured up to the stele in the front courtyard, Lu Shouchong had the young Daoists stop; he went himself to the stele and looked up at it. He looked very carefully, from the cross at the stele's head, to the two dragons, to the nine characters of the title, and on down line by line. His lips moved; he was reading; at one place he stopped, turned back, and called Jingfu. Jingfu went over; Lu Shouchong pointed to a line on the stele and said: Leader, this sentence—what does it mean?

Jingfu looked. That line was When the virtue of Zhou declined, the blue chariot ascended into the west; now that the Way of the great Tang shines, the Luminous wind blows east. Jingfu said this was his Master's writing; it said the virtue of the Zhou had declined, and Laozi rode his blue ox away to the west; now the Way of the great Tang shone bright, and the Luminous wind blew from the west to the east. He said that in writing this line his Master meant that Laozi went west long ago, and our teaching now comes from the west; west and east, coming and going, it is the same Way; the stele's meaning is to honour Laozi.

Lu Shouchong heard him out and shook his head, and said: what the leader speaks of is your Master's meaning; what he reads is the stele's characters. He said the characters on the stele were arranged like this: Laozi went west because the Zhou's virtue had declined; once Laozi left, the Central Plain had no Way; then your Luminous wind blew in and filled the gap; this says that after Laozi left, the Way of the Central Plain was given by you. He said in the Jianzhong, Zhenyuan and Yuanhe years no one noticed this line, and if they noticed they didn't say; the Sons of Heaven then honoured you; now the Son of Heaven honours Laozi; Master Zhao is in the palace; the Son of Heaven receives registers every day; let the Son of Heaven see this line on the stele, let Master Zhao see it—what will it mean? Let the leader think it over for himself.

Jingfu stood before the stele, both hands hanging. Lu Shouchong left his words lying there, turned, and had the young Daoists go on measuring; when they had finished he rolled up the rope, totalled the figures in the register, and told Jingfu: the monastery's land, front, back, left, right, he had measured all of it—how much the hall took, how much the courtyards took, how much stood empty; he'd recorded it all and would go back and report to the abbot; what the abbot would think he didn't know; today he'd only come to look. He clasped his hands, went to the monastery gate, turned back again, looked once at the stele, and said to Jingfu: the characters on the stele—the stone remembers them, and people remember them too; Leader, wouldn't you say so?

He left. Jingfu stood before the stele till the shadow of the colonnade crept up the stele's foot; Lingbao had Qushen help him out and asked what the Daoist had said. Jingfu told him. Lingbao reached out to touch the stele, touched that line, felt those characters—the blue chariot ascended into the west—he could not see, and felt them stroke by stroke with his fingers. He said: this line—when Brother wrote it in the scripture room long ago, he was beside him grinding ink; when Brother finished he read it to Pa, and Pa said this line is good; Laozi went west, we come from the west; the road is one road. He said when Brother wrote this line he never thought one day someone would read it so. Jingfu said: if Master were here, what would he say? Lingbao said: if Brother were here, he'd say the characters are his, the meaning is his; what others read it as is their business; he won't change it. He said: but Brother isn't here; you are.

That night the two of them sat in the scripture room debating that line. Jingfu said what he had thought as he stood before the stele was: could that line be covered with something? He said not chiselled off; the stele's characters mustn't be chiselled; chisel them, and the monastery would itself have accepted the Daoist Lu's reading; he was thinking of standing a wooden board before the stele, or hanging a cloth, to cover the lower half of the stele; outsiders coming would not see that line; when this wind had blown over, it could be taken away. He said the monastery couldn't bear trouble now; one sentence, one line of characters, taken by the palace Daoists, would be a charge; the stele is dead, people are alive; protect the people first.

Lingbao sat by the table, heard him out, and shook his head. He said he couldn't see the characters on the stele any more, but he could feel them; every line on the stele he could recite with his eyes shut; as a child, when Brother wrote the stele, he ground the ink; when Pa cut the Syriac letters, he handed the chisels; the day the stele was raised, Pa stood before it and read it from start to end, read it a whole watch. He said: if you cover the lower half, what you cover is not only that line; below it is Pa's name, and Patriarch Hnanisho's name, and the Syriac Pa cut; hang a cloth over it, and what you hang it over is the face of a whole monastery. He said the Daoist Lu came today with a rope to measure the land; what he wants is the land; that line is the pretext he found; cover this line and he'll find the next; the stele has eighteen hundred characters; he'll always find one.

Jingfu said: then leave it like this and wait for him to take this line to the palace and accuse us? Lingbao said he didn't know. He said he knew only one thing: Pa didn't lie all his life; Brother didn't change a character all his life; the characters on the stele are the truth; cover them, and the monastery itself has a guilty conscience. He said: you're the leader; if you want to cover it he can't stop you; but the day you cover it, he won't go to the stele to touch it any more.

Jingfu did not raise it again. He got up and went to the gate to light the lamp, and when the lamp was lit came back and told Lingbao: then we won't cover it. He said: but next time the Daoist Lu comes, he must have thought out how to answer; his Master's meaning he must explain clearly, so clearly the Daoist Lu can't refute it; today he spoke badly, said half, and let the man take it away. Lingbao said: you spoke well enough today; he said: long ago Brother argued with Elder Samaan over the stele's characters for several evenings, and Elder Samaan was never wholly convinced to his death; but the stele was raised, and not one character was changed.

That night Salang came to find Jingfu. He said he'd lost his work; the Uighur warehouses in the West Market took no one now, and the Sogdian shops wanted no porters either; he was forty-five, and still had strength, but no one wanted his strength. He said he wanted to ask the leader one thing: he wanted to come back to the monastery; register matters he didn't understand; he was a lay believer; he only wanted to come back and keep the gate, keep it at night and by day too; his grandfather's father had guarded this monastery's gate, his grandfather had guarded it too, and with him it had been broken off twenty years; he wanted to take it up again. He said: today that Daoist went round the courtyard measuring with a rope, and he stood inside the gate holding that staff, and holding it, what he thought was: after this, if someone comes and what he carries isn't a rope, he must be here.

Jingfu said the monastery had no money for wages: two meals a day, and at night sleep in the west wing. Salang said that was enough. From that day, Salang kept watch inside the monastery gate at night. He brought a low stool and set it inside the gate against the doorframe; at dusk, when Jingfu had lit the lamp at the gate, he sat down beside the lamp, the wooden staff laid across his knees.
