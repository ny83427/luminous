Volume Eight　The Daqin Monastery

Chapter 46　Alopen

Jingjing set pen to paper in the second month after the letter was sent.

The elder Azarel had said no one was to touch a stone. Jingjing did not touch a stone. He only went every night, when the people of the monastery were all asleep, alone to the scripture room, lit a lamp, and opened the elder Babai's wooden box.

When Babai gave him the key he said nothing, only pointed out the things in the box one by one: this pile was the Patriarchate's instructions, this pile the letters of past bishops, this pile the accounts, this bundle what was picked out of the mud after the troubles of Tianbao, the writing washed blurry, not yet sorted. At the very bottom was a small cloth bundle, and wrapped in the bundle a little book, parchment, the size of a palm, its corners worn round.

"This was Alopen's," said Babai.

Jingjing's hands stopped.

"He carried it with him," said Babai, "and wrote odds and ends in it. The first few pages were written on the road, the rest after he reached Chang'an. The writing is very small and scrawled; I've been reading it twenty years and made out half."

He put the book into Jingjing's hands, and added: "Wash your hands clean before you turn the pages."

Jingjing went and washed his hands. In hot water.

On the first page of the book was written only one line:

"Set out. Carrying twenty-seven books of scripture, some images, five companions."

The second page gave the name of a mountain Jingjing did not know. The third gave a river, and a camel that died by it. The fourth was sand. The fifth was sand again.

By the tenth page or so, the companions were three.

Jingjing turned the pages one by one. The wick burned short, he trimmed it, it burned short again, he trimmed it again. He came to a page with only two characters on it, the ink very thick, as if the writer had put great force into his hand.

"Chang'an."

The ninth year of Zhenguan, west of the city of Chang'an, outside the Kaiyuan Gate.

The wind was very strong that day. Alopen afterwards noted it in the book: strong wind, sand in my eyes, could not see clearly those who came to meet me.

He rode a camel, and on the camel's back were two wooden chests holding the scriptures and the images. Behind him came three men: two old believers who had followed him all the way from Persia, and a young disciple taken on in Tokharistan. Four men, two camels, a lame horse.

When they were three li from the city, they saw the road ahead lined with people.

There were banners, parasols, drums, officials in red robes, soldiers in armour, drawn up in perfect order from the roadside all the way to the city gate. Alopen sat stunned on his camel a long time. He thought some foreign envoy must be entering the city and he was in the way, and hurriedly had his disciple lead the camel to the side of the road.

Before the disciple had moved it, a man rode over from the other side.

The man was past fifty, in a purple robe, thin-faced, his beard not long, his eyes very bright. He dismounted before the camel, cupped his hands to Alopen, and said a great string of words.

Alopen understood not one.

When the man had finished, he waited. Alopen waited too. The two of them stood facing each other a while, until a minor official in a blue robe ran up from behind the man and asked in Persian:

"Are you the man of high virtue Alopen, come from the kingdom of Daqin?"

Alopen said he was.

The minor official turned and said it again in Tang. The man in purple nodded, and said another great string. The minor official said it again in Persian:

"The Chief Minister Duke Fang, by command of the Son of Heaven, awaits you here."

Alopen was stunned again. He looked back at the three men behind him, and the three looked at him, their faces covered in sand, covered in dust, their lips cracked. He looked again at the banners on the road, the parasols, the drums.

He asked the minor official: "All this—it's to meet me?"

The minor official said it was.

Alopen noted that day in the book. What he wrote was:

"The Chief Minister Duke Fang with the full honour guard at the western outskirts. I asked, all this is to meet me? He said yes. I did not believe it. Only at the city gate did I believe it."

When Jingjing had read this far, he put the book down.

He thought how the inscription should be written. He took up a brush and wrote on a sheet of rough paper:

"In the ninth year of Zhenguan he arrived in Chang'an. The emperor sent his chief minister, Duke Fang Xuanling, with the full honour guard to the western outskirts, to receive him as a guest and bring him in."

When he had written it he read it over, thought a while about the sense of I did not believe it, and did not write that in.

When Alopen entered Chang'an, he entered the library.

The library was inside the palace city, where the Son of Heaven kept his books; in its hall there were rows upon rows of bookshelves reaching to the beams. The Son of Heaven gave him a side hall, a few great tables, a few clerks, and had him translate the scriptures he had brought into Tang characters.

The book recorded how they translated. Jingjing read it three times before he understood.

What Alopen held was the scripture in Syriac. He spoke no Tang, so he read it sentence by sentence to his young disciple, and the disciple said it in Persian. The Court of State Ceremonial had sent an interpreter, a Sogdian who knew Persian and knew Tang, and he said it again in Tang. When he had said it, a scribe sitting beside them—a collator of the Imperial Library, a Tang man who had read a great many books—listened, thought, and wrote it down in the literary language.

One sentence had to pass through four mouths.

Alopen wrote in the book: "I say a sentence, and when it reaches the paper, I don't know whose sentence it is."

He said Alaha, the disciple said God, the interpreter said Heaven, the scribe thought and wrote 天尊, the Heavenly Honoured One. He said Mshiha, the disciple said it by sound, the interpreter said it by sound, the scribe heard it three times and wrote three characters: 迷詩所, Mishisuo.

He said the Lord's name, Jesus.

The scribe wrote 移鼠, Yishu. And 鼠 is rat.

At the bottom of the box Jingjing found that roll.

It was the earliest translated scripture, on yellow hemp paper, the characters very neatly written; you could see at a glance it was written by a man who had read books. The scripture had a very long name: the Book of Hearing Mishisuo. He read from the beginning, read a few lines, and could not read further.

Not that he could not understand it. That he understood it too well.

Heavenly Honoured One, the Buddhas, arhats—the whole page full of Buddhist words. Where it told of the Lord's birth it wrote: the Heavenly Honoured One… Yishu. Where it told of Mary it wrote 末艶, Moyan.

Jingjing stared at those two characters 移鼠 a long time.

He woke Babai. Babai came over with his clothes thrown on, squinted at the page under the lamp, and said "Oh. That."

"Who wrote this?"

"The scribe wrote it."

"Alopen didn't see to it?"

"Alopen didn't know Tang characters," said Babai. "Whatever they wrote, he didn't know. By the time he knew, the scripture had already been copied out and sent abroad."

Jingjing looked at those two characters once more.

"A hundred and forty years," he said. "For a hundred and forty years, on Tang paper, the Lord has been called a rat."

Babai thought and said: "It was changed later."

"Changed to what?"

"To 翳數, Yishu," said Babai. "A bit better."

Jingjing said nothing more. Babai yawned and went back to bed.

There was a page in the book about the Son of Heaven coming.

That day Alopen was translating when suddenly there was a tramp of feet outside the hall, and the clerks all knelt. Alopen did not know what to do, and knelt too. The man who came in was of middle years, in a yellow robe, very tall, thick-bearded, walking very fast.

Only later did Alopen learn that this was the Son of Heaven.

The Son of Heaven did not tell him to rise, but sat down himself at the table, picked up a newly written draft of scripture, looked at it a while, and asked one question.

That question passed through four mouths, and by the time it reached Alopen's ears it was this:

"The one you worship—what does he want men to do?"

Alopen thought a long time. He knew that his answer too would have to pass through four mouths to reach the Son of Heaven's ears, and when it got there, no one would know whose words they were. So he answered very briefly.

He said: "Love him. Love men."

The disciple said it once, the interpreter said it once. The scribe did not write it, because the Son of Heaven was there and the scribe did not dare move his brush. When the Son of Heaven had heard it, he nodded, put the draft down, rose and went.

On this page of the book that was all there was. The last line was: "I do not know what he heard."

Jingjing wrote on the rough paper: "His scriptures were translated in the library; his Way was inquired into within the palace. The emperor, knowing it deeply to be right and true, gave special command that it be taught."

When he had written his Way was inquired into within the palace, he paused. He thought: on the stele it is written that the Son of Heaven inquired into the Way. In truth that day the Son of Heaven asked only one question, and Alopen answered with only four words.

The translating went on three years.

In the latter half of the book the writing grew smaller and smaller, more and more disordered. There were several pages with only a date and a number, which Jingjing guessed was how many lines were translated that day. One page said: "Disciple sick, fever, three days." A few pages later: "Disciple well." A few pages more: "That Sogdian has gone; another in his place, speaks Persian badly." A few pages more: "Cold. The ink froze."

A page in the summer of the twelfth year of Zhenguan had only one line:

"Translation finished. Sleep."

On the next page the ink had changed, and the writing was a little steadier:

"Slept a day and a night. When I woke, someone was waiting outside the room. Said the edict had come down."

The man who came that day was an official of the Court of State Ceremonial. He stood outside Alopen's room the better part of the day; Alopen did not wake, and he did not dare call him. The disciple told him the man of high virtue had not slept properly in three years. The official said: then I'll wait.

When Alopen woke, it was dark again. He sat up and felt his body very light, so light it seemed not his own. He heard people talking outside the door, pushed it open, and saw that official, his disciple, and the newly come interpreter who spoke Persian badly.

The official unrolled a scroll of yellow paper and read it out.

The interpreter said it once in his poor Persian. The disciple said it once more in Syriac.

The words of the edict, as Alopen heard them, were these:

"The Way has no fixed name, the sage has no fixed form. Wherever one goes, one sets up a teaching after the fashion of that place, and quietly helps the many. Alopen of the kingdom of Daqin has brought scriptures from very far away. His teaching is deep and not noisy. It does men good, and may go throughout the world. The government will build him a monastery in the Yining Ward, and ordain twenty-one men as monks."

When Alopen had heard it, he stood at the door and for a long time said nothing.

Then he asked: "Where is the Yining Ward?"

The next day Alopen went to the Yining Ward.

It was still empty ground there, overgrown with weeds, a few old bricks in the grass. At the edge of the ground was a locust tree, and under the tree a well, its curb half fallen. The people of the ward stood far off watching him, watching this shaggy-bearded hu monk walk back and forth in the grass, walk a while, squat down, take up a handful of earth, look at it, and put it down.

He stood a while in the middle of the ground. That was where the hall later stood.

He went to the locust tree too, drew a bucket of water from the well, washed his hands, washed his face, and sat on the well curb a long time. That well, the people of the monastery drank from for more than a hundred years after.

Of the Yining Ward, the book recorded only one sentence. Jingjing looked at it a long time, did not settle how to write it into the stele, and in the end did not write it.

That sentence was: "The earth is soft."

By the time Jingjing had read this far, it was nearly dawn. He closed the book, wrapped it in its cloth, and put it back at the bottom of the box. He smoothed out the rough paper and read the few lines he had written from the beginning.

Then he began to copy the edict.

The copy of the edict was in the box—the very sheet shown that day to the assistant magistrate, yellowed and brittle, its edge eaten round by insects. Jingjing spread it under the lamp and copied it character by character.

"The Way has no constant name; the sage has no constant form. Teachings are set up to suit each place, and quietly save the multitudes."

He copied smoothly. At the third sentence his brush stopped.

What the copy said was: "The Persian monk Alopen has brought scriptures and teaching from afar and presented them at the capital."

Persian monk.

Jingjing looked at those three characters and thought of the sentence he had written earlier on the rough paper: In the kingdom of Daqin there was a man of high virtue named Alopen.

He sat there, and the ink on his brush tip slowly dried.

Yisi came in before daybreak.

He rose at this hour every day, first went to the monastery gate to blow out the lamp, then to the hall to light the lamps. When he had blown out the lamp at the gate he saw that the window paper of the scripture room was still lit, and pushed the door open and came in.

Jingjing pushed the copy across for him to see.

Yisi looked at it, slow at reading characters, and looked a while before he came to those three. He said nothing, and sat down across from Jingjing.

"The fourth year of Tianbao," said Yisi. "I was twenty."

Jingjing looked at him.

"That autumn the court issued a decree. It said the Persian teaching came at root from Daqin, and had been passed on in China a long time. When the monasteries were first built they were called Persian monasteries. Now men must know its root, so the Persian monasteries of the two capitals were all to be renamed Daqin monasteries."

"That day the old priest called a few of us young ones to take down the old tablet over the gate. The tablet was wood, had hung there a hundred years, the nails rusted fast. I climbed the ladder and hammered half the day before I got it down."

He paused.

"The day the new tablet went up, the old priest stood at the gate a while, and said something."

"What?"

"He said: 'Another name. The people are the same people.'"

Jingjing lowered his head and looked at the copy.

After a while he took up his brush, dipped it in ink, and wrote on the rough paper:

"Alopen, a man of great virtue from the kingdom of Daqin, has brought scriptures and images from afar and presented them at the capital."

Yisi watched him write, and said neither that it was right nor that it was wrong.

All that winter and spring, Jingjing was in the scripture room every night.

He went through Alopen's book several more times. He sorted out the old records from after Tianbao roll by roll. Emperor Gaozong made Alopen Great Lord of the Law, Guardian of the State, and set up Luminous monasteries in every prefecture. In the reign of the Empress Wu the Buddhists in the Eastern Capital mocked them. Emperor Xuanzong had the Prince of Ningguo and four other princes come to the monastery in person, and granted the portraits of five former emperors. In the Zhide years Emperor Suzong re-established the Luminous monasteries in Lingwu and the four other commanderies. Of these things some had documents, some had only a line or two in other men's letters, and some had only his father's word.

When he came to his father's part, he went to ask his father.

"How do you want me to write you?"

Yisi thought and said: "Write little."

Jingjing neither agreed nor refused.

In the fourth month of the first year of Jianzhong, the draft was finished.

That morning he pressed the last sheet of rough paper under the inkstone, blew out the lamp, laid his head on the table, and fell asleep.

He slept a day and a night.

Someone from the monastery came to call him to eat, and Yisi stopped them, saying: let him sleep. Babai came for the key, and Yisi untied it from Jingjing's belt and gave it to him. At noon the sun shone in at the window onto Jingjing's face, and Yisi got up and drew the window paper closed.

Yisi sat in the scripture room all the while. He took the pile of rough paper from the table and looked at it sheet by sheet. He looked very slowly; there were some characters he did not know, and those he skipped. When he came to the part about himself, he stopped a long time, then drew those few sheets out, turned them face down on the table, and went on reading.

At daybreak the next day, Jingjing woke.

He raised his head, his face printed with lines from the paper, his eyes not yet able to open. He saw his father sitting across from him with that little parchment book in his hands, turned to the last page.

Yisi closed the book and laid it on the table.

"Alopen slept a day and a night too," he said. "The ginger broth is on the stove."
