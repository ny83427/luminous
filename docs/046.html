Volume Eight　The Daqin Monastery

Chapter 46　Alopen

Jingjing set brush to it in the second month of the first year of Jianzhong. The stone had not yet been bought, the stonemason not yet hired; every night he came back from the north end of the ward—Shiyiniang kept watch at A-Lian's—and went alone to the scripture room, lit a lamp, and opened that wooden box of Elder Babai's.

When Babai handed him the box, he pointed out its contents to him one by one: this stack was the Patriarchate's orders, this stack the letters of successive bishops, this stack the accounts, this packet what was picked out of the mud after the Tianbao upheaval, the writing soaked into blurs, not yet sorted. At the very bottom was a small cloth bundle, and wrapped in it a little book, of parchment, the size of a palm, its corners worn round.

Babai said this had been Alopen's, carried on his person, for noting down odds and ends; the first few pages were written on the road, the rest after he reached Chang'an; the writing was very small and scrawled; he had been reading it twenty years and made out half. He put the little book in Jingjing's hands and added: wash your hands clean before you turn the pages. Jingjing went and washed his hands, in hot water.

On the first page of the little book only one line was written: "Set out. Scriptures carried, twenty-seven works; images, several; companions, five." The second page was the name of a mountain, which Jingjing did not know. The third, a river; by the river a camel had died. The fourth said sand; the fifth, sand again. By the tenth-odd page, the companions were three. Jingjing turned page after page; the wick burned short, he trimmed it, it burned short again. He came to a page with only two words on it, the ink very heavy, as if the writer had pressed with great force: "Chang'an."

The ninth year of Zhenguan, west of the city of Chang'an, outside the Kaiyuan Gate.

The wind was very strong that day. Alopen afterwards noted this in his little book: strong wind; sand got in my eyes; could not see clearly those who came to meet us.

He rode a camel, and on the camel's back two wooden chests, and in the chests the scriptures and the images. Behind him came three men: two old believers who had followed him all the way from Persia, and a young disciple taken on in Tokharistan. Four men, two camels, and a horse lame in one leg.

When they came within three li of the city they saw the road ahead full of people: banners, parasols, drums, officials in red robes, soldiers in armour, drawn up in perfect order from the roadside all the way to the city gate. Alopen, on his camel, froze. He thought the envoy of some country was entering the city and he was in the way, and hurriedly told his disciple to lead the camel to the side of the road. Before the disciple had led it aside, a man rode toward them from the other side. He was past fifty, in a purple robe, his face lean, his beard not long, his eyes very bright. He dismounted before the camel, saluted Alopen with clasped hands, and said a great string of words, of which Alopen understood not one. The two of them stood facing each other a while; then a minor official in a blue robe ran up from behind the man and asked something in Persian; Alopen's disciple heard it and turned it into Syriac for him: was he the man of high virtue Alopen, come from the west? Alopen said he was; the disciple turned it for the minor official; the minor official turned and said it in Tang; the man in purple said another great string, and it came back all the way round: the Chief Minister, Lord Fang, by the command of the Son of Heaven, was here to receive him. Alopen stood stunned. He looked back at the three behind him, and the three looked at him, faces covered in sand, covered in dust, lips cracked. He looked back again at the banners on the road, the parasols, the drums.

He asked the minor official: all these are to welcome me? The minor official said yes. Alopen recorded that day in his little book:

"The Chief Minister Lord Fang in charge of the procession at the western outskirts. I asked: all these are to welcome me? He said yes. I did not believe it. Only at the city gate did I believe."

When Jingjing had read this far, he put down the little book, took up his brush, and wrote on a sheet of rough paper:

"In the ninth year of Zhenguan he arrived in Chang'an. The Emperor sent his Chief Minister Lord Fang Xuanling, in charge of the procession at the western outskirts, to receive him as a guest and bring him in."

When he had written it he read it over, thought a while about the meaning of the words "I did not believe it," and did not put them in.

Alopen entered Chang'an, and entered the Library Hall. The Library Hall was within the palace city, the place where the Son of Heaven kept his books; inside, row upon row of bookshelves reached right up to the beams. The Son of Heaven gave him a side hall, a few great tables, a few clerks, and had him translate the scriptures he had brought into Tang characters.

The little book recorded the method of translation; Jingjing read it three times before he understood. What Alopen held was the Syriac scripture. He knew no Tang, so he read it sentence by sentence to that young disciple, and the disciple said it in Persian. The Court of State Ceremonial sent an interpreter, a Sogdian, who knew Persian and knew Tang too; he said it again in Tang. When he had said it, beside him sat an amanuensis, a collator of the Imperial Library, a Tang, who had read many books; he listened, thought, and wrote it down in literary Chinese. One sentence had to pass through four mouths. Alopen wrote in the little book: "I say a sentence, and when it is on paper, I do not know whose sentence it is."

He said "Alaha," and the disciple said "God," and the interpreter said "Heaven," and the amanuensis thought and wrote "Tianzun," the Heavenly Worthy. He said "Meshiha," and the disciple said it by sound, and the interpreter said it by sound, and the amanuensis listened three times and wrote three characters: Mishisuo. He said the Lord's name, Yeshu, and the amanuensis wrote Yishu, "shifting rat."

Jingjing remembered that year when Shiyiniang under the lamp read that old scripture, came to the two characters Yishu, and burst out laughing, saying: whose rat is this, moving house? He had thought then it was only people a hundred-odd years ago writing by sound, who didn't know what shu meant in Tang. It turned out the writer did know: he was a Tang, a collator of the Imperial Library, who had read many books. He knew; but he did not know whose name it was; to his ear it was only a sound a hu had made. Babai said the later scriptures changed it to Yishu, written with other characters, a little better. Jingjing wrote a line of small characters at the edge of his rough paper: the Lord's name not to go on the stele; on the stele call him only Meshiha.

A few days later he went to the Great Ci'en Temple in the Jinchang Ward. At the foot of that temple's pagoda stood two steles: one the Emperor Taizong's preface to the Dharma Master Xuanzang's translations, one the Emperor Gaozong's record, the calligraphy by Chu Suiliang; of all the readers of books in the city of Chang'an, none had not gone to see them. Jingjing went early that day; there was no one before the steles; he stood before them and read them twice from beginning to end, and at "translated the essential texts of the holy teaching, six hundred and fifty-seven works in all," stopped. An old monk sweeping came over, saw him in hu robes, and asked where he was from. Jingjing said the Daqin monastery of the Yining Ward. The old monk said "Ah—" and said that was the Persian monastery; he'd heard his master speak of it when he was small; Jingjing said it was called the Daqin monastery now. The old monk did not speak of the monastery's name again, and asked what he'd come to see. Jingjing said he'd come to see how others wrote a stele.

The old monk was talkative, and leaning on his broom talked with him a while. He said when the Dharma Master Xuanzang translated scripture it was not one man translating but a translation hall, a roomful of people: a chief translator, amanuenses, verifiers of meaning, composers of text, polishers of text, verifiers of the Sanskrit; one sentence going from Sanskrit to Tang passed through seven or eight hands; the chief translator was the Dharma Master Xuanzang himself, who knew both Sanskrit and Tang, and when the sentence reached the paper, whose it was he knew in his heart. Jingjing asked: and if the chief translator knew only Sanskrit and not Tang? The old monk thought and said: then it's hard; then you can only trust the amanuensis, and whatever the amanuensis believes is what goes on the paper. When he had said it he looked at Jingjing and said again: your house, back then, was probably like that. Jingjing did not argue, thanked him, left the Ci'en Temple, and all the way back to the Yining Ward thought of that line in Alopen's little book: I say a sentence, and when it is on paper, I do not know whose sentence it is.

That night he wrote one sentence on the rough paper: "He translated the scriptures in the Library Hall; he was questioned on the Way within the forbidden gates." When he had written it, he wrote beside it a line of small characters he did not mean to carve: Alopen did not know Tang writing; the amanuensis was a Tang; a hundred and forty years, and only today do we write for ourselves.

There was a page in the little book that told of the Son of Heaven's coming.

That day Alopen was translating; there was a sound of footsteps outside the hall; the clerks all knelt, and he knelt with them. The one who came in was a man of middle years, in a yellow robe, very tall, his beard very thick, walking very fast; only afterward did Alopen learn that this was the Son of Heaven. The Son of Heaven did not tell him to rise, but first sat down himself by the table, took up a sheet of scripture just written, looked at it a while, and asked one question.

That question passed through four mouths and reached Alopen's ear as: the one you worship—what does he want of men? Alopen thought. He knew that this answer, once given, would also pass through four mouths before it reached the Son of Heaven's ear, and when it got there, there was no knowing whose words it would be. So he answered very briefly.

He said: "To love him; to love men." The disciple said it once; the interpreter said it once. The amanuensis did not write it, for with the Son of Heaven there the amanuensis dared not move his brush. The Son of Heaven, when he had heard it, nodded, put down the sheet, rose, and left.

On this page of the little book only so much was recorded. The last line was: "I do not know what he heard."

Jingjing wrote on the rough paper: "He translated the scriptures in the Library Hall; he was questioned on the Way within the forbidden gates. Deeply knowing it to be right and true, the Emperor gave special command for it to be spread."

When he had written the four characters "questioned on the Way within the forbidden gates," he paused. That day the Son of Heaven had asked only one question, and Alopen had answered only one sentence.

The translating went on three years. In the latter half of the little book the writing grew smaller and more disordered. Several pages recorded only a date and a number, and Jingjing guessed that was how many lines had been translated that day. One page recorded: "The disciple is sick; fever; three days." A few pages on: "The disciple is well." A few pages more: "That Sogdian has gone; another in his place, whose Persian is poor." A few pages more: "Cold; the ink froze."

A page of the seventh month of the twelfth year of Zhenguan had only one line:

"Translation finished. Sleep."

On the next page the ink had changed colour, and the writing was a little steadier:

"Slept a day and a night. When I woke, someone was waiting outside the room. Said the edict had come down."

The one who came that day was an official of the Court of State Ceremonial. He stood outside Alopen's room most of the day, and Alopen did not wake, and he dared not call him. The disciple told him the man of high virtue had not had a proper sleep in three years. The official said: then we'll wait.

When Alopen woke, it was dark again. He sat up and felt his body very light, so light it seemed not his own. He heard people talking outside the door, pushed it open, and saw the official, saw the disciple, saw the newly arrived interpreter whose Persian was poor.

The official unrolled a scroll of yellow paper and read it out once.

The interpreter said it once in his not very good Persian. The disciple said it once more in Syriac.

The words of the edict Alopen heard thus:

"The Way has no fixed name; the sage has no fixed form. Wherever he goes, he sets up the teaching after the fashion of that place, and quietly helps the many. The monk from Persia, Alopen, has brought scriptures from very far away. His teaching is deep and not noisy. It does men good, and may go through the realm. The government will build him a monastery in the Yining Ward, and ordain twenty-one men as monks."

When Alopen had heard it, he stood at the door and asked: "Where is the Yining Ward?"

The next day, Alopen went to the Yining Ward.

It was still an empty lot then, wild grass growing on the ground, a few old bricks in the grass. At the edge of the lot was a locust tree, and under the tree a well, its rim half fallen in. The people of the ward stood far off watching him, watching this shaggy-bearded hu monk walk back and forth in the grass, walk a while, squat, take up a handful of earth, look at it, and put it down.

He stood a while in the middle of the lot, where the hall would later be. He walked to the locust tree, drew a bucket of water from the well, washed his hands, washed his face, and sat on the rim of the well. Of the Yining Ward the little book recorded only one sentence, which Jingjing could not decide how to put on the stele, and in the end did not. The sentence was: "The earth is soft."

When Jingjing had read this far it was nearly dawn. He wrapped the little book up, put it back at the bottom of the box, and began copying the edict. The copy of the edict was the same sheet he had shown the registrar that day, yellow and brittle; he spread it under the lamp and copied it character by character.

"The Way has no constant name; the sage has no constant form. Teachings are set up to suit each place, and quietly save the multitudes."

He copied very smoothly. At the third sentence his brush stopped.

What the copy said was: "The Persian monk Alopen has brought scriptures and teaching from afar to present at the capital." On that old stone behind the hall it was the same three characters, and what the registrar, squatting there, had read out was the same three characters too. Jingjing looked at them, thought of the sentence he had written earlier on the rough paper—In the kingdom of Daqin there was a man of high virtue named Alopen—and sat there while the ink on his brush-tip slowly dried.

Yisi came in before dawn.

He rose at this hour every day, went first to the monastery gate to put out the lamp, then to the hall to light the lamps. He put out the lamp at the gate, saw the window paper of the scripture room still lit, and pushed open the door and came in.

Jingjing pushed that copy over for him to see.

Yisi read slowly; he looked a while before he came to those three characters, and sat down across from Jingjing. He said that in the fourth year of Tianbao he was twenty; that autumn the court issued an edict saying the Persian scriptures and teaching had come originally from Daqin, and the Persian monasteries of the two capitals should all be called Daqin monasteries; that day Bishop Yonan had a few of them young ones take down the old plaque at the gate; the plaque had hung a hundred years, the nails had rusted solid; he climbed the ladder and had to bang a good many times with a hammer before it came down; the day the new plaque was hung, Bishop Yonan stood at the gate a while and said: another change of name, and the people are still these people. Jingjing bent his head over the copy, took up his brush, dipped it in ink, and wrote on the rough paper:

"Alopen, Great Virtue of the kingdom of Daqin, has brought scriptures and images from afar to present at the capital."

Yisi watched him write it, and got up and went to light the lamps in the hall.

At the beginning of the third month A-Lian died. For more than a month after, Jingjing by day was at the gruel kitchen, at the medicine chest, and at night in the scripture room; in the corner leaned three wooden boards, the Xizhou one turned round, the other two brought back from the West Market.

Alopen's little book he went through several more times. The old records from after Tianbao he sorted out roll by roll too. The Emperor Gaozong made Alopen Great Lord of the Law, Guardian of the State, and Luminous monasteries were set up in every prefecture. In the reign of the Empress Wu the Buddhists mocked them in the eastern capital. The Emperor Xuanzong had the Prince of Ning and four other princes come in person to the monastery, and granted portraits of five former emperors. In the Zhide years the Emperor Suzong rebuilt Luminous monasteries in Lingwu and four other commanderies. Some of these things had documents, some only a line or two in others' letters, some only his Pa's mouth.

The passage of Xuanzong's granting the portraits was told him by the old Tocharian believer of the monastery. The old man was past eighty and hard of hearing; to talk to him you had to shout in his ear; but the events of the early Tianbao years he remembered more clearly than yesterday's. He said that year he was twenty, a servant at the monastery; General Gao came in person, riding a white horse, a company of palace people behind him carrying five paintings wrapped in yellow damask; they carried them into the hall, opened them one by one, hung them on the walls: portraits of five former emperors, painted as big as life, their eyes all looking down at the people below. General Gao brought a hundred bolts of silk too, saying they were granted by the Son of Heaven, and told the monastery's people to enshrine them well. He said that day the monastery's people all knelt, Bishop Yonan at the very front and he at the very back, knelt half an hour till his legs went numb; when General Gao had left, those paintings hung there a good many years; in the year Chang'an fell, rioting soldiers tore them down and burned them for a fire; he saw it with his own eyes; as they burned, those few pairs of eyes in the paintings were still looking down. Jingjing asked him what General Gao had said that day; the old man thought a long time and said General Gao said one thing: these hu monks are well-behaved, at least. Jingjing wrote a few lines: "Early in Tianbao, the Great General Gao Lishi was commanded to bring the true portraits of the five sage emperors to be placed in the monastery, and a hundred bolts of silk were granted, in reverent celebration of the sacred likenesses." When he had written it, he remembered how that year Official Pei asked whether they would ask for the portraits again, and Narsai was displeased, and Sabrisho said wait till the hall is built and then discuss it; the hall was built, and the portraits were never asked for again.

For Gaozong's passage he wrote "The Law flowed through the ten circuits, and the state was rich in peace; monasteries filled a hundred cities, and every house abounded in Luminous blessing." When he wrote "ten circuits," he paused, went to the corner and got out his register of the prefectures, turned to those three empty pages—Hebei, Shannan, Jiangnan—not a character on the three pages. He shut the register and did not change that line. What the stele wrote was what happened in Gaozong's time; at that time there probably were such; he didn't know; the old records wrote it so, and he wrote it as they did.

When it came to his Pa's passage, he could not write it. His Pa had said write little; but the stele had to write of the great benefactor, and to write of the great benefactor it had to say who he was and what he had done; he asked his Pa, and all his Pa spoke of were others: Old Duan, Wei the Third, Shi Six, Du De, General Hun; of himself he said only one sentence: he followed behind Lord Guo and saw the road. Jingjing went once to the Qinren Ward, wanting to see Guo Ziyi. The men at the gate of the Guo mansion knew him, and said the Lord was ill and saw no visitors, and had him wait in the gatehouse; he waited a good half hour, and out came an old guardsman: Hou Siyuan, his hair white, lame in one leg; he said the Lord had heard, and sent him out to say a few things to you.

Hou Siyuan sat on the bench in the little room by the mansion gate and talked with him till the sun sank west. He told of Changshan, of how the hu monk who saw through the spy had a keen nose; of the ditch east of the Xiangji Temple; of the Uighurs; of how on the day Chang'an was recovered Master Yi rode in at the Mingde Gate on Old Duan's horse; of how in camp he never went into the rear tent—when Lord Guo called him, he stood at the tent door; when he didn't, he was outside the camp gate seeing the road. He said men in camp said behind his back that Master Yi was Lord Guo's ears, Lord Guo's eyes; once he heard Lord Guo himself say so, and when he had said it say too: ears and eyes are grown on other men's faces; they're of use, but you can't lean on them. When Hou Siyuan had said this, he stopped, looked at Jingjing, and said: don't write that one. Jingjing said he would not.

He went back and wrote a few lines: "He served the Duke as claws and teeth, and the army as eyes and ears. He could give away his stipend and gifts, and stored nothing at home." He wrote too of his Pa's giving gruel and medicine in Chang'an, wrote "The hungry came and were fed; the cold came and were clothed; the sick were healed and raised up; the dead were buried and laid to rest." When he wrote "the dead were buried and laid to rest," he paused; that line was written of his Pa, and of Narsai too; Narsai's name would not be on the stele. He wrote it down all the same. When it was written, he took those few sheets to show his Pa; his Pa read them and said: too much. He said he had already cut it; his Pa said: cut it again; he said it couldn't be cut any more; and his Pa said no more, and gave the sheets back to him.

In mid-fourth month the draft was finished.

That morning he pressed the last sheet of rough paper under the inkstone, blew out the lamp, laid his head on the table, and fell asleep.

He slept a day and a night. People from the monastery came to call him to eat; Yisi stopped them and said let him sleep. Babai came looking for the key; Yisi untied it from Jingjing's belt and gave it him. At noon the sun came in at the window and shone on Jingjing's face; Yisi got up and drew the window paper across.

Yisi sat in the scripture room the whole time. He took the stack of rough paper from the table and looked through it sheet by sheet. He read very slowly; there were a few characters he did not know, and he skipped them. When he came to the passage about himself he stopped, drew out those few sheets, turned them face down on the table, and read on. At dawn the next day Jingjing woke and raised his head, his face pressed with lines from the paper, his eyes not yet able to open. He saw his Pa sitting across from him holding that little parchment book, just turning to its last page. Yisi shut the book and laid it on the table.

"Alopen slept a day and a night too," he said. "There's ginger broth on the stove."
