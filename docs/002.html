Volume One　Guo Ziyi

Chapter 2　Rajagriha

Old Duan pushed his empty bowl to one side and said it had happened in the twelfth month of last year.

He told it all out of order, a piece here as he thought of it and a piece there. Some of it he had seen with his own eyes, some he had heard later in camp, and some of it he still did not know to this day. What happened that day was this.

In the eleventh month of the fourteenth year of Tianbao, An Lushan rose in revolt at Fanyang, with two hundred thousand men, so he claimed, and marched south. The commanderies of Hebei had known peace for decades and had not seen war in years. Of their prefects and magistrates, some opened their gates and went out to welcome him, some abandoned their cities and fled, some were captured or killed, and in less than a month most of the land north of the Yellow River was no longer Tang soil.

When the news reached Chang'an, the Emperor Xuanzong at first refused to believe it. When he had no choice but to believe it, he sent out one edict after another. One of them made Guo Ziyi, governor of Jiuyuan, military governor of Shuofang, and told him to bring his troops out of the northwest and march east to cut behind the rebels.

Guo Ziyi was fifty-nine that year. He had come up through the military examinations and served in the army more than thirty years. He had fought the Turks and held frontier towns. His rank was not small, but his name was not great, and few people at court knew who he was. He did not seem to care much about such things himself. He took the edict, mustered his men and horses, and in the twelfth month marched out of Lingzhou, east along the Yellow River.

The cold came early that winter. The Yellow River was frozen over, snow had fallen on the ice, and wherever you looked there was only white, so that you could not tell which was river and which was bank.

On the fourth day out of Lingzhou, the scouts of the vanguard came back to report: ahead, on the bank of the Yellow River, there was a small town, and outside the town a caravan of foreign merchants was being robbed. Who was robbing them, it was too far to see. The townspeople had all shut their doors; nobody dared come out.

The officers around Guo Ziyi paid it no mind. In those days there were bandit soldiers looting everywhere, and they had already come across several such affairs on the road. Someone said that the army's march mattered more; they could go round.

Guo Ziyi thought about it on his horse, and said: "Go and look."

The officers did not understand, and Guo Ziyi did not explain. What he was thinking was really very simple. The army was marching east. Its grain and fodder would have to follow from behind, its dispatches would have to go back from the front. If someone was robbing on this road, it was not only a caravan of merchants they would be robbing. He needed to know who was robbing it, where they came from, and how many they were.

He picked out a hundred riders and went forward himself. Old Duan was among the hundred that day, riding behind Lord Guo's horse, half a length back.

The town really was pitifully small: a few dozen households, mud-brick houses in a row along the riverbank. At the east end of the town there was an open space, used for markets; just now, in the middle of the open space, carts had been drawn up in a ring, with bales of goods lashed on them, and the camels that pulled them had all been led inside the ring.

Outside the ring of carts, a dozen or so mounted men were circling.

Guo Ziyi reined in on a low rise of earth and watched from a distance. The dozen men were a motley lot. Some wore sheepskin coats, some ragged iron armour; some wore felt caps, and some were bareheaded, their crowns shaved, with a small braid left at each temple. Their weapons were mixed too—spears, curved swords, bows. They rode round and round the ring of carts, shouting as they went, shouting in a foreign tongue with a few words of Chinese mixed in, all of them curses.

"Tongra," said an old captain beside Guo Ziyi, in a low voice. "See the little braids. They're Tongra."

Guo Ziyi nodded. The Tongra were a tribe of the Tiele, and An Lushan's army had a great many Tongra horsemen, said to be his best. These dozen were most likely patrol riders thrown out by the rebels, either scattered from their unit or sent ahead to scout the road.

But outside the ring of carts there was one more man on horseback.

He wore an old grey robe, its hem splashed all over with mud and snow. He was not tall, but broad in the shoulder, with a small round shield slung on his back, and he rode a blue roan, back and forth along the edge of the ring, a bow in his hand. A few strides, and he loosed an arrow; a few strides more, and another. He did not charge into the knot of riders. He kept close to the ring of carts, and whichever side they tried to close in on, he went to that side—one man and one bow, and he held a dozen riders off at thirty or forty paces.

Old Duan watched for a while, and said, "Huh."

"He's shooting the horses," said the old captain, who had seen it too.

It was the horses. The man's arrows did not go for the men. They went for the horses' chests, the horses' necks. An arrow struck, the horse gave a long scream, its forelegs buckled, and it pitched its rider into the snow. The Tongra soldier who fell would scramble up out of the snow and try to charge again, and find that his two legs could not outrun another man's horse, and shrink back behind his comrades.

Old Duan was watching the man outside. Lord Guo was watching the ring of carts.

There were twenty or thirty people inside it, mostly foreign merchants, old and young, and several women. Each time the man in grey rode past the gap in the ring, he checked his horse and shouted something inside in a foreign tongue, and then rode on. Each time he shouted, the people inside moved. The old and the women shrank back behind the carts. A few young merchants with sticks and short knives split up to guard the gaps between one cart and the next. The camels were led over one after another and made to kneel in front of the women, a wall of them. Bales were unloaded from the carts and stuffed into the spaces beneath them, leaving only a single gap, so narrow that it would let through one man at a time.

Guo Ziyi watched for a good while, and said to those beside him: "That one knows how to lead men."

The old captain said: "From his clothes, he looks like a monk."

Guo Ziyi did not answer. His eyes stayed on the man outside the ring and on his quiver.

At last the monk ran out of arrows.

He reached into his quiver and found nothing. He hung his bow on the saddle and drew a long spear from beside it. The riders saw that he had no more arrows, and with a howl three or four of them charged at once.

Guo Ziyi gathered his reins and said one word: "Go."

The hundred riders went down off the rise. The rise was more than a li from the carts, and below it the snow lay knee-deep; the horses could not run, but sank at every step. Old Duan rode bent low over his horse's neck, and his eyes never left the man ahead for a moment.

The monk did not dodge. He rode straight at the first rider. As the horses passed, he drove his spear into the other horse's chest—drove it too deep, and the horse pitched forward, and the shaft broke with a crack, the head left in the horse's body. He had only half a shaft in his hand when the second rider's sword came at him. He blocked with the half shaft, and it broke again.

At that moment a flight of arrows came from the riders. His roan took two in the neck, screamed, reared, and fell heavily. In the instant the horse went down the monk rolled from the saddle, rolled twice in the snow, got up, and in three or four strides was back in the gap of the ring of carts, pulling the small round shield off his back.

Old Duan's heart tightened as he watched from his horse. With the horse gone, the man had nothing but his two legs, and the Tang riders were still more than half a li of snow away.

Just then, one of the riders lost patience.

He was probably their leader. He rode a black horse, with a brocade robe looted from who knew where thrown over his armour, and iron showing beneath the brocade. He spurred up to the gap and used his spear shaft to flip a bale off one of the carts. The bale fell to the ground and burst open, and out rolled scrolls, roll after roll of them, and some things in cloth bags that looked like herbs. The leader swore—probably because it was worth nothing—and edged his horse up against the cart, bent down, and reached under it.

Under the cart a child was hiding.

He was nine or so, very thin, wrapped in a sheepskin coat far too big for him. The leader seized the collar of the coat and dragged. The child did not cry out or weep. He clung with both hands to the spokes of a cartwheel, his small face going red with the strain.

The monk moved.

Here Old Duan paused, and looked across the table at Jingjing. "This bit," he said, "you were under the cart. You didn't see it."

In twenty years in the army Old Duan had come to close quarters with men more times than he could count, and seen more ways of dodging a spear than he could reckon. That day, the monk did not dodge. When the leader saw him coming he let go of the child, turned his spear, and thrust at his chest. The monk tilted his shield, and the spearhead slid along its face. He stepped forward along the spear shaft and was already under the horse's belly. The thing in his right hand came down, and only then did Old Duan see what it was: a short mace, its head no bigger than a fist, its haft not much more than a foot long.

By now the Tang riders had come close, and Old Duan heard a dull sound—even through the wind and snow he heard it plainly—like a mallet coming down on a lump of raw meat on a chopping board. The wrist of the hand that held the spear bent all at once in a direction a wrist should not go. The spear dropped into the snow. The monk took hold of the man's belt and pulled him down off his horse, and then set his foot on the man's knee and stamped.

The leader screamed. The scream was high and long, and carried a long way over the empty snow.

The riders went wild.

Two of them jumped down from their horses and came at the gap one from each side; where a horse could not get in, a man could. The one on the left reached him first and brought his sword down. The monk caught it on his shield, but the man followed with another cut, slantwise, and it caught him on his left arm. The grey robe split open and blood soaked through at once. The monk did not step back. He drove the rim of his shield forward into the man's chest, and his mace came down on the back of the hand that held the sword, and the sword fell. The one on the right was just about to spring when a young merchant in a gap between the carts swung a camel-driver's stick and brought it down square on the back of his head, and the man swayed twice and went face first into the snow.

The rest stayed outside and shot arrows. The arrows hit carts, hit bales, hit camels; the camels, stung, bellowed and jumped. The monk held the gap with his shield up, and the blood from his left arm ran down his fingers and dripped onto the snow, drop by drop, a red that hurt the eyes.

At last the hundred riders broke out of the deep snow. When the patrol saw the Tang banners they had no more stomach for the fight. They left their comrades on the ground and fled—east, out onto the river, their horses sliding on the ice, a sorry sight. The few without horses could not run fast, and two of them were caught and bound by the Tang riders who came up behind. Guo Ziyi would not let them pursue far. They chased for two li, shot two of them down, and came back.

When Guo Ziyi came back to the front of the ring of carts, the monk was squatting in the snow.

The cut on his left arm was still oozing blood, and he paid it no attention. With one hand he pressed down on the leader's chest so that he could not get up; with the other he undid the leader's belt and used it to bind the broken wrist against his body. The leader was sweating all over with the pain, and still cursing. The monk ignored him. When he had tied it, he bent his head close to the man's ear and asked something in a foreign tongue. The leader would not say. The monk asked again, his voice level, and his finger brushed the broken wrist, lightly. The leader's face went white all at once, and this time he talked, fast and urgent, a long string of it.

Guo Ziyi, on his horse, saw this clearly. This man would not kill, and yet he knew how to use pain. He said nothing; he only looked once more at that hand.

When the monk had heard it all, he stood up and turned round, and only then saw Guo Ziyi on his horse. He looked at Guo Ziyi's armour, and at the banners behind him, then pushed the mace back into his belt and bowed to him. It was not a Tang bow. His right hand went to his chest, and he bent low from the waist.

"Thank you, General." He spoke Chinese, very slowly, every word bitten off clean.

Guo Ziyi did not dismount. He looked at the ground. Four or five Tongra soldiers lay or knelt there, some clutching their wrists, some clutching their heads, groaning in the snow, all of them still alive. Several dead horses lay outside the ring of carts, with arrows in them, all in the horses' chests and necks.

"What did you ask him?"

"I've asked him a few things," the monk said. Having said that, he said no more, and only looked at Guo Ziyi.

The old captain beside Guo Ziyi could not hold himself in. He urged his horse forward a step and barked: "Foreign monk, have you no sense? This is Lord Guo, military governor of Shuofang, come in person to save you. When he asks you something, you answer!"

The monk looked at the old captain, and then at Guo Ziyi again. He looked very carefully: at his armour, at his horse, at the hundred riders behind him, at the banner the wind was tearing at with a great flapping noise. He had heard Guo Ziyi's name—in Lingzhou, and in the inns along the road—heard it said that the court had a new military governor of Shuofang, who had fought the Turks and held frontier towns, a steady, careful man. But to have heard of a man was not to have seen him.

"Forgive me, General," he said. "This humble monk has heard the name of Military Governor Guo. Only he has never seen Military Governor Guo's face. On this road this humble monk has met several bands of men who said they were government troops, and some who said they belonged to such-and-such a general. A military governor commands tens of thousands of men. That he should come in person with a hundred riders to save a caravan of foreign merchants—this humble monk did not dare simply believe it."

The old captain's face flushed dark red, and his hand went to his sword hilt. "What did you say? You're saying we're pretending to be—"

"Old Liang," said Guo Ziyi.

The rest stuck in the old captain's throat, though his hand stayed on his sword hilt.

Guo Ziyi was not angry. He looked at this foreign monk standing in the snow covered in blood, looked at him for a while, and something like a smile showed on his face. He thought: on this road, in these times of war and chaos, he had heard of plenty of men pretending to be government troops, pretending to be generals. A foreigner travelling this road with a child, if he believed everyone he met, would have been dead several times over. A man who looks at the road ought to be just this suspicious.

"Show him a tally," he said.

Old Duan took his own bronze waist-tally from inside his coat, rode forward, and bent to hand it down. The tally was the size of a palm, cast in bronze, with Headquarters of the Military Governor of Shuofang cut on the front and a number on the back, its edges worn bright.

The monk took it and turned it over and over, and ran his thumb along the carved characters. In all his years on the merchant roads, passing gates and cities, he had seen plenty of official tallies and warrants; true or false, he had only to weigh one in his hand and look at it, and he knew. He handed the tally back to Old Duan with both hands, and looked up at Guo Ziyi again; and this time, looking at that face, at that beard crusted with frost, he found that it matched the Guo Ziyi the people on the road had talked of.

He bowed again, and this time bent lower. "This humble monk was too suspicious, and has given offence to Lord Guo, and to this general."

"No blame to you," said Guo Ziyi. "What did you find out?"

Only then did the monk tell it all, point by point. "He says they are Tongra, who came south with An Lushan's army and were scattered in Hebei. Their leader sent them west to scout the roads—to see whether the Shuofang army had marched, how many, and by which road. They came, forty-odd riders in all, in three bands. Of the other two bands, one went north, and one went south along the river."

The officers around Guo Ziyi exchanged glances. The captain called Old Liang took his hand off his sword hilt.

"What else did he say?"

The monk paused. "He also said they robbed this caravan because they were hungry. They hadn't had a full meal in three days."

Guo Ziyi said "Hm," and looked at the dead horses, and asked: "That mace of yours—you hit men with it and don't kill them? And your arrows go only into the horses?"

The monk raised his head and looked at him. His eyes were brown, and very deep. "When the horse goes down, the man can't charge. When the man is beaten to death, you can't ask him anything."

Some of the officers laughed. Guo Ziyi did not. He thought what the man said was true, but only half of it. The other half, the monk had not said.

The child crawled out from under the cart just then.

His sheepskin coat had been torn, and there was snow and mud on his face, but his eyes were very bright. He ran to the monk's side and saw the blood on his arm, and stopped short. He did not cry, and he did not throw himself on him. He only stood beside him, and turned up his face and said: "Pa, fourteen."

The monk looked down at him.

"They had fourteen horses altogether," the child said. "Pa shot four down, and speared one, and nine ran away." He stopped, and his voice got smaller. "Our roan died too."

The monk nodded, and put his hand on the top of the child's head and pressed it. "Well counted."

Guo Ziyi found the pair of them, the big one and the little one, amusing. He asked the child, "Who told you to count?"

The child was a little afraid of him, and slipped behind the monk, and said in a small voice: "Pa told me to hide under the cart and count the horses. If I counted them right, I wouldn't be scared."

Guo Ziyi smiled again. He turned and told Old Duan: "Tear off a strip of cloth and bind up his arm for now. When we get back to camp, have the physician look at it properly." Then he turned back to the monk. "Where are you from?"

"I'm a man of Tokharistan," the monk said. "I was born in the city of Balkh. When your country's Master Xuanzang went west long ago, he passed through it, and said it was Little Rajagriha. My father was a priest at the monastery in the city. I came to Chang'an with a caravan when I was seventeen. That's thirteen years ago now."

"Which monastery in Chang'an?"

"The Daqin monastery, in the Yining Ward."

Guo Ziyi thought about it. He seemed to have heard someone mention that monastery, and then again perhaps not. There were too many monasteries in Chang'an.

"What's on the carts?"

"Scriptures, medicines, and some things the monastery uses," the monk said. "We have a monastery in Lingzhou. It's small and short of things. The elder told me to bring them over."

"There's a war on, and still you bring them?"

"There's a war on," the monk said. "So they're shorter still."

Guo Ziyi sat on his horse and thought for a while.

It would soon be dark. The army was still behind them, the place to camp that night had not yet been fixed, and the officers behind him were all waiting for his orders. By rights, having saved a caravan of foreign merchants and found out how matters stood with the enemy, that would be the end of it. But Guo Ziyi asked another question, one that seemed to have nothing to do with anything. "The road from Lingzhou to Hedong—have you travelled it?"

"Twice."

"Taiyuan?"

"Once."

"Jingxing?"

The monk hesitated. "No. I've heard the men in caravans talk of it."

"You foreign merchants," said Guo Ziyi, glancing at the people in the ring of carts, still shaken, "coming and going east and west—what you see on the road, what you hear—do you pass it on to one another?"

"We do," said the monk. "Chang'an, Lingzhou, Taiyuan—wherever we have a monastery, most of the foreign merchants who pass will stop at it to rest, burn incense, send letters and goods by someone's hand. Whose caravan has come from where, which pass it came through, where there's fighting, where the road is cut—the people at the monastery often know before the officials do. I lived in Chang'an more than ten years, and the foreign merchants I know from all over are not few."

Guo Ziyi asked no more. He only nodded.

What came into his mind was An Lushan. An Lushan was himself a mixed-blood Hu from Yingzhou, who in his early years had been a go-between in the frontier markets and spoke several foreign tongues. In his years at Fanyang he had had the foreign merchants of every circuit carry goods for him; nobody knew how much treasure and wealth went to Fanyang every year, and wherever the foreign merchants went, they carried his news with them. There were foreign merchants in the rebels' money and grain, and foreign merchants among the rebels' eyes and ears. Guo Ziyi did not lack scouts. What he lacked was a man who could go to and fro among foreigners, foreign merchants and foreign soldiers without making anyone suspicious.

He said: "Come with me a stretch."

The monk did not answer at once. He looked down at the child beside him, then up at the merchants in the ring of carts. The merchants were all looking at him, and there was fear in their faces, as if they were afraid that once he left, the riders would come back.

"I'll send men to take the caravan to Lingzhou," said Guo Ziyi. "The child will go with it."

Still the monk did not answer. It was a good while before he spoke, and his voice was lower than before. "General, I am a monk."

"I know."

"Going with an army, there's no avoiding blood," the monk said. "What I must do, I'll weigh for myself. Only, where a life can be spared, I'd like to spare it."

Guo Ziyi looked at him. This time he looked very carefully: from his face to his hands, to the short mace at his belt, to the cut on his arm still oozing blood, and back to his face.

"I don't want you to kill anyone," said Guo Ziyi. "I want you to look at the road."

That night Guo Ziyi made camp beside the Yellow River.

The monk did not go back to Lingzhou with the caravan. With his arm bound in cloth, he lifted the child up onto a cart, squatted down, and said a few words to him—not in Chinese; Old Duan did not understand a word of it. The child listened, and nodded, and did not cry. When the carts moved off, the child lay on the tail of the cart looking back, looking back a long time, until the caravan went round the bend of the river and could not be seen any more.

Old Duan stood to one side holding two horses. One was his own grey; the other was a chestnut gelding Lord Guo had given the monk—four years old by its teeth, steady of temper—to make up for the roan that had died in the snow. The monk took the reins and stroked the horse's neck a few times, very slowly.

"You can ride?" Old Duan asked. As soon as he had asked it he felt the question was a waste of breath. He had watched that whole business in the snow from the slope.

The monk swung up onto the horse. His left arm had no strength in it; he only put his right hand on the pommel and pressed, and he was up. When he was settled in the saddle he looked back at Old Duan. "Where I come from, children ride before they walk."

Old Duan gave a grunt of a laugh and mounted too.

Guo Ziyi had already turned his horse toward the camp. A few paces on, he reined in again, turned round, and asked: "What's your name?"

The monk told him a name. It was a long name, a whole string of sounds, and Guo Ziyi did not keep a single syllable of it. The monk saw that, and put it another way.

"The Tang people call me Yisi."
