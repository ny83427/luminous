Volume Seven　A-Lian

Chapter 41　The Wedding

At the end of the sixth month of the eleventh year of Dali, the locust trees of Chang'an flowered.

There were many locust trees in the city of Chang'an: locusts along both sides of the Avenue of the Vermilion Bird, locusts at the foot of every ward wall, locusts too at the gates of the great houses, standing row on row along the streets, standing there a hundred years and more; the people of Chang'an called them the locust ranks. In spring when other trees flowered the locust did not, but only put out leaves, and only when the dog days came and the other flowers had all faded did it flower, and when it flowered it filled the whole city, pale yellow specks hung at the ends of the branches, and when the wind blew they fell all over the streets, crushed under cart wheels, trodden under hooves, stuck to the soles of people's shoes and carried into their houses. The children of the wards beat them down with bamboo poles, a basketful at a time; the medicine shops bought the unopened buds, called locust rice, for medicine; the dye shops bought the open flowers and boiled them to dye things yellow. Scholars in those months most dreaded the sight of locust flowers, saying: when the locust flowers yellow, the candidates are busy—the examinations came in autumn. At the gate of the Daqin monastery in the Yining Ward there was a locust too, older than the monastery; the monastery was set up in the Zhenguan years, and the tree was there already. Under the tree was a well, its rim of blue stone worn shiny, the well rope having cut several deep grooves into the stone. The well was the ward's well; the people of the ward drank its water, and the monastery's people drank it too.

Every morning Yonan gave out flatbread under this tree. He had done it some years now; every day at first light he came out with a bamboo basket and stood under the locust tree, and to each one who came gave a flatbread. Those who came were beggars, porters, people fled from famine in the countryside, and children of poor families in the ward; he asked no one who he was, nor what he believed; when he had given them out he drew a bucket at the well and washed his hands, the first time of the day. Since last autumn there had been more bread in the basket, half of it sent from that shop in the West Market; the sparrows' share he still broke off separately, half a cake, and laid at the root of the tree.

On the morning of the twenty-sixth of the sixth month he gave out the bread as usual, washed his hands as usual, went back into the monastery, and put on a new robe. That day he was to be married. He was seventy-one.

The Patriarch's reply had come in the second month, and the words in the letter the monastery's people all came to know afterward: Alaha pitieth our weakness. Once they knew, each took it in his own way.

Ishaq wept. He was fatter than ever now, and needed two men to help him sit down; when he wept his belly heaved, and when he had done weeping he went to the kitchen and asked how many sheep should be stewed for the wedding feast; the kitchen said the day wasn't fixed yet; he said then buy the sheep now and keep them, so there'd be no running about looking when the time came. Babai brought out the monastery's register of persons, turned to Yonan's name, and left a line empty below it; left it empty, did not write, and waited. Gabriel said that on the banks of the Two Rivers those being married wore crowns on their heads; the year Yisi married, the monastery's crowns were two old bronze ones brought from the Patriarchate, one with a corner missing, which Ishaq afterwards took to trade for a sick man's medicine; now Chang'an had no crowns like that, and so he said when the day came he would go out of the city to the banks of the Wei and break willow wands and weave them himself. Sabrisho, when he had heard the letter, stroked his beard and said the same thing as in the year Yisi married: the rule says it's allowed, so it's allowed. Haddad said nothing at all; he had known long since, since that year at Xizhou. Narsai too said nothing at all; he said his prayers as before, cut paper and trimmed pens under the window of the west wing as before, taught Lingbao a letter every other day as before. That spring the monastery's people often saw him sitting under the gallery trimming pens, the reed shavings falling flake by flake at his feet, swept up and falling again.

On the seventh day after the reply came, A-Lian went once to the side courtyard.

She went in the morning; the shop was not open; she had the young helper hang a board at the door saying closed today. She came in at the monastery gate, did not go toward the hall, but went round by the gallery toward the side courtyard, and at the door of the side courtyard stopped. The door of the side courtyard was still the door—thirty years ago an old wooden door, changed now, made new the year Yisi married, a small wooden cross nailed on the lintel; the jujube tree by the door was still there, thicker than before. She stood at the door, raised her hand, lowered it, raised it again, and knocked three times on the door.

It was Yisi who opened it. He had an axe in his hand and was about to go to the back courtyard to split wood; seeing who stood at the door, he put down the axe. The two of them faced each other across the threshold, and neither spoke first. A-Lian knelt first. She knelt outside the threshold, knelt on the spot where she had stood thirty years ago; that day she had not knelt; today she knelt; the spot had been earth then and was paved with brick now, and as she knelt her knees struck the brick loudly. Yisi reached out to raise her; she would not get up, and said thirty years ago she had come here to beg him, and today she had come to thank him. She said that day she had stood at this door with the child in her arms, in the rain, with nothing, only a child; she handed him the child and he took it; his wife came out from behind him and took it, carried it in, squatted at the stove to add wood; and seeing that wife go to heat water, she knew this child would have hot water to wash in, and she left. She said in these thirty years she had not forgotten that stove-fire one day.

Yisi stood inside the door and heard her out, and squatted down, level with her, and said that wife of his had died long ago, died the year the child was six; it was she who raised the child. He said he himself was away those years, in the army, not home for ten days or half a month at a time, and when he was home it was to make the child recite scripture, and punish him when he got it wrong, punish him till he cried, and even when he cried not let him drink cold water; he was not a good father. He said the child had grown by himself, grown better than he had, grown to be like you. When he had said it he reached out and raised her up, and this time she got up, her knees dirty with earth from between the bricks; he did not brush it off for her, but stood at the door looking at her.

Shiyiniang came out of the inner room, saw, asked nothing at all, brought A-Lian into the house, and told Lingbao to fetch hot water from the kitchen. Lingbao came out carrying a bowl, too full, spilling all the way, and by the time he got to A-Lian only half a bowl was left. A-Lian took it and looked at this child—the brows faint, the nose high, the eyes brown—and asked his name. Lingbao said his Tang name was Lingbao and his own name Adam, the same as his brother's. A-Lian, holding that half bowl of hot water, trembled once, spilling a little more, and put the bowl down, and said she knew.

That morning A-Lian sat a good half hour in the side courtyard talking with Shiyiniang, while Yisi split wood in the back courtyard, the sound of the splitting coming over blow by blow. Jingjing was in the west wing that day and did not come over. When A-Lian left, coming out of the side courtyard she passed the door of the west wing; the door of the west wing was shut, and on the window paper was the shadow of a man, head bowed, writing. She stood a moment at the door, did not knock, and left.

The wedding day Yonan fixed himself, at the end of the sixth month. Yisi asked him why wait till the sixth month; he said that when he was bishop in Chang'an long ago, one year when the locusts flowered a household in the ward took a bride, and he stood at the monastery gate and watched, watched their cart go under the locust trees, the roof of the cart covered with flowers, and he thought then: in this life I'll never see my own. Yisi heard it, nodded, and said: then the sixth month.

Where they would live after the wedding Official Pei arranged for them. A-Lian's shop could not be given up—she had saved it up cake by cake—and Yonan could not go to live in the West Market: the West Market shut at night, and he had to come out at first light to give out bread and come back at evening to light the lamp, and would not get in at the ward gate. Official Pei found a small courtyard at the north end of the Yining Ward, where an old oil-seller had lived; the old oil-seller had died the year before and his son was in Luoyang, and the courtyard stood empty: two rooms, a small well, a grapevine trained at the foot of the wall; they rented it, and the year's rent was not much. A-Lian's household registration Official Pei put in order for her too: as Director Liu had said that year, when foreigners of one kind married among themselves it went by their own custom; she had been baptized, and counted as one of your people; on the Metropolitan Prefecture's side, she need only be struck off the West Market household and attached to Yonan's name. The ward headman came once to the monastery to look; he looked at Yonan and looked at the register, and muttered that in twenty years as ward headman this was the first time he'd set up a household for a hu monk of seventy-one; and when he had said it he entered it in the book, took the two flatbreads Ishaq pressed on him, and left. A-Lian said by day she would still go to the shop, and come back when the gong sounded; from the West Market to the Yining Ward was two wards, she walked fast, she'd be in before the ward gate shut. Yonan said every evening when he'd lit the lamp he would go to the north end of the ward to meet her. A-Lian said meet her for what—she'd walked roads thirty years; did she need meeting? Yonan said then he'd stand at the courtyard gate and wait. A-Lian said: as you like.

Yonan asked Jingjing to be his groomsman.

That was in the third month. Yonan came to the west wing, sat down across from Jingjing, and said that when the Tang took a bride, the bridegroom had at his side a groomsman, who spoke for the bridegroom, drank in the bridegroom's place, and went to the bride's door to recite poems for the bridegroom; he said his Tang was poor, he couldn't recite poems, he couldn't drink wine; for all these someone must stand in for him; he had thought it over and over, and only Jingjing could. Jingjing sat there with his brush still raised, not putting it down, and Yonan did not press him, but waited. Jingjing asked Yonan: a groomsman was one of the bridegroom's family—a brother, a son or nephew; if the Elder made him groomsman, and people asked, what was he to the bridegroom's family? Yonan said: count as a student; he'd taught him, and he'd taught him; the two of them had taught each other; for a student to be groomsman to his teacher—among the Tang that was done too. Jingjing asked again: and if the bride's family asked? Yonan looked at him and said the bride had no family; if she asked, say you're a deacon of the monastery. Jingjing put down the brush and said good.

From then till the sixth month Yonan came to sit in the west wing every few days; sometimes at the sand tray by the door, writing, still heart; sometimes in Babai's seat, watching Jingjing translate, and after a while asking how a Tang character was said, and when he had asked did not write it, but said it, said it a few times till he remembered it, and went back and said it to A-Lian, and A-Lian said he'd said it wrong and taught him again. Once he asked Jingjing how to write the character xi, joy. Jingjing wrote it on paper; Yonan looked at it and said this character was too hard, he'd never learn to write it in his life. Jingjing said the Elder had learned heart; joy had a mouth at the bottom too, and the top was like a drum; learn it, and from now on he could write joy. Yonan shook his head and said: the second character he learned, he'd wait for her to teach him; whatever she taught him, he would learn. Jingjing, hearing it, made no answer, cut off the strip of paper with joy written on it, and slipped it under Yonan's sand tray. That strip of paper stayed slipped there.

That spring A-Lian began coming to worship at the monastery. On the Lord's Day she shut the shop before light and walked over from the West Market, came into the monastery once the ward gates opened, and stood in the last row of the hall, stood in the place where she had stood thirty years before. She could not understand Syriac; when Elder Marya preached, Jingjing beside him put it into Tang, for the few dozen Tang believers of the ward, and for her. She stood in the last row and Jingjing at the front, a hall full of people between them; when he translated, his eyes were on the scroll and not on people, but he translated more slowly than usual, sentence by sentence, enunciating every word clearly, so that even those in the last row could hear. Babai told Ishaq afterwards that in those months Jingjing translated the scripture the way his Pa spoke Tang.

A groomsman had to say a few words at the wedding feast; that was the Tang custom. The first night, Jingjing wrote a draft in parallel prose, four-and-six, balanced couplets: "thirty years of wind and frost scatter like clouds in a single morning," "three dots of the heart at last make one branch of two trees"—wrote a whole sheet of it, and when it was written, read it over once, and at the second line his own face went red first; he held that sheet to the lamp and burned it, and wrote again. The second draft was only seven or eight lines, all things he had seen with his own eyes: the wooden tablet at Xizhou, the one character Yonan had written for twenty years; at the end, coming to speak of her, he held the brush and wrote on the paper the two characters a niang, Ma, and when they were written the brush-tip rested on them, rested till the ink was about to drip, and then drew a line through them, drew it very hard, the ink soaking through to the back of the paper, and beside them wrote four other characters, A-Lian niangzi, Lady A-Lian. When he had written it he did not read it over again, but folded the sheet and pressed it under the inkstone, and there it stayed three months.

Shiyiniang, tidying the west wing later, moving the inkstone, saw that paper, opened it and looked once, folded it again just as it was, and pressed it back. She told no one.

The day before the wedding, Shiyiniang went to the West Market.

A-Lian had no family, no sisters; on the night before she went out from her home, some woman ought to comb her hair for her. The women of the few neighbouring households in the West Market were all willing to come, but A-Lian asked none of them; she sent word to the monastery by the porter that she would like to ask Master Yisi's wife to come. When Shiyiniang heard it, she stood a while in the kitchen, washed the dough off her hands, and said she would go.

That night Shiyiniang stayed at the shop. Behind the shop was a little room where A-Lian lived: a bed, a chest, a small table, on the table an oil-dish lamp, and nailed on the wall a wooden board written on in charcoal with four letters, Alaph, Lamadh, Yudh, Nun, and below them a heart; A-Lian had written it herself, many years ago, and the charcoal marks had faded. Shiyiniang had A-Lian sit on the edge of the bed and took down her knot; her hair fell loose, more than half white, long, hanging to her waist. Shiyiniang combed it stroke by stroke with a wooden comb, combed very slowly, and with each stroke said something, all of it household talk: how Lingbao kicked off the covers at night, how Yisi ate vinegared celery so sour his eyes shut and still wanted more, how when Jingjing was small in Lingzhou his Pa wouldn't let him drink cold water and he secretly drank some once and rolled about the bed in pain—Yisi had told her that. A-Lian listened, and at that asked her how long he'd hurt that time. Shiyiniang said she'd heard it was all night, and the next day it still hurt. A-Lian did not take it up, and after a while said that when she was carrying him, she hurt at night too, hurt so she couldn't sleep; she thought it was the child kicking her; only later did she know it was her own stomach that was bad; the child took after her. Shiyiniang's comb paused, and went on combing.

When she had combed it, Shiyiniang, by Tang custom, put her hair up in a higher knot and set in it a new pin, of silver: Shiyiniang's own, given her by her mother the year she first married; when she married Yisi she had not worn it, but kept it in her chest. A-Lian touched the pin and said this was too precious. Shiyiniang said it was lent: wear it tomorrow, give it back the day after. A-Lian said: good, the day after I'll give it back. Before they slept, A-Lian took down from the wall that board with the four letters and the heart, wrapped it in a cloth, and gave it to Shiyiniang, saying she'd hung this board many years; when she moved there'd be nowhere to hang it; from now on if he asked about these years, give it to him; if he didn't ask, keep it with you. Shiyiniang took it and said she would. The two of them sat facing the oil-dish lamp till midnight; A-Lian coughed a few times, a very deep cough; Shiyiniang poured her a bowl of hot water and asked how long she'd been coughing. A-Lian said several years; worse in winter, lighter in summer; it was nothing. Shiyiniang said tomorrow she'd have Elder Ishaq look at her. A-Lian said she wouldn't be looked at; she was afraid of taking medicine; medicine was bitter. Shiyiniang said then have Yisi make it up for her; the medicines Yisi made up weren't bitter. A-Lian laughed and said then she'd try.

On the wedding day, from the afternoon on, the West Market side was full of bustle.

When the Tang took a bride, the bridegroom had to go to the bride's home to fetch her, with the groomsman following; at the bride's door the women of her family kept the door shut and demanded that the bridegroom's party recite poems to hurry the bride's dressing, and only when they were recited well would the door open; when the bride was in her carriage, the bridegroom had to ride his horse three times round the carriage; and on the way back the people of the wards would stop the carriage—blocking the carriage, it was called—and once it was stopped the bridegroom's party had to give wine, give cakes, give money, and only when they had given enough would it be let pass; the harder it was blocked, the more good luck it added to the couple. Yonan could ride, but at seventy-one would not; he said: three times round the carriage, he would walk round it; A-Lian said she wasn't used to riding in a carriage; she'd walk too; Yonan said: then round the person three times. Yisi, hearing it, said: then round the person.

Early that day Yonan gave out the bread, then went back to his room to change his robe. The robe was new; Ishaq had had a tailor in the West Market make it; when he was measured Yonan would not take off his outer garment, and the tailor had to measure him through his robe, and what he made was big in the shoulders, and the seam down the back carelessly sewn. Jingjing came to fetch him and found him standing in the middle of the room tugging at the front of the robe with both hands, tugging this way and that, and could not get it to lie flat. Jingjing tied his girdle again for him, a little tighter, and the shoulders gaped less. Yonan stood and let him tie it, and when it was tied asked him: at the door, when the bride's people ask questions, how should he answer? Jingjing said the Elder needn't answer; the groomsman answers. Yonan said: and if he must answer something? Jingjing thought and said: then the Elder answers, lai le—I've come. Yonan said it twice, lai le, lai le, very slowly, and when he had said it said he knew those two words; he didn't need to learn them.

At the hour of wei that day Yonan came out of the monastery, Jingjing at his side, and behind them six guard brothers Sargis had picked, all changed into clean black, carrying no staves, no shields on their backs, their hands empty, bearing two bamboo baskets on a pole, and in the baskets sesame cakes Ishaq had baked and two jars of vinegar. Xu Jing came too; he had long since stopped being a soldier and minded a shop for someone in the West Market; hearing Elder Yonan was to be married, he ran to the monastery first thing, and said he wanted to go along; he knew the people of the West Market; the carriage-blockers he could keep down. Jingjing said there was no need to keep them down; when people block the way, it's to give good luck. Xu Jing said he knew; he just wanted to go along.

When the party reached that street on the south side of the West Market, the shop door was shut, the door curtain let down, and inside the door was a roomful of women: on the left the wife of Wang the vinegar-seller, on the right the wife of Zhao the shoe-seller, the daughter-in-law of the old hu merchant across the way, and that porter's wife too, holding her two-year-old child, who wriggled in her arms to get down. Someone inside the curtain shouted, asking what honoured guest was at the door, daring to come here. Jingjing stepped forward one pace, made a bow toward the curtain, and said it was Elder Yonan of the Daqin monastery of the Yining Ward, come to fetch Lady A-Lian of the West Market. Inside they shouted again, asking where the Elder was from; the Elder was from the kingdom of Daqin; where is the kingdom of Daqin? Jingjing said: where the sun goes down; it takes a year to walk there. Inside they all burst out laughing and said: a year to walk there! then if the bride marries into it, a trip home to her family takes two years—she won't marry. Jingjing said the Elder wasn't going back to Daqin; he lived at the north end of the Yining Ward; walk there from here, and you're there before the gong finishes; for the lady to go home to her family would be nearer than going to her shop. Inside they laughed again and said: the deacon has a clever tongue; then recite a poem; recite it well, and the door opens.

Jingjing had written one the night before, four lines of seven characters, and had thought it very good—it had locust flowers in it, and lamp, and thirty years; but now, standing at the door before a whole street of people, what he recited was another, one he made up standing there, very plain, so that the children of the ward could all understand it:

"Before the door the locust tree lets fall its yellow flowers; within the door the bride is at her dressing. No need to wait for the drum's third round—come out a little early to see your a lang."

When he came to the two words a lang—your man; my lad—he faltered a moment himself. The people inside did not catch it, but only laughed and said: this poem's no good, too common; but it's said loud; open the door. The door curtain was lifted and out came a big stout woman, the wife of Wang the vinegar-seller, a rolling pin in her hand, and said: by the old Tang custom, when the bridegroom comes in the bride's people beat him three times; the louder the beating, the less he'll dare bully the bride after. Yonan stood there not knowing what to do; Jingjing stepped in front of him in one stride and said the Elder was old; he'd take the three for the Elder. Wang's wife said: if the groomsman takes them for the bridegroom, he takes six. Xu Jing behind egged them on, saying the deacon took a bandit's cudgel that year at Xizhou; what's six? So Jingjing stood at the door and bent over and let Wang's wife beat him; Wang's wife took the rolling pin and struck him six times on the back, very lightly, one after another, like beating a quilt; and when she had done the whole street clapped, and she herself laughed till she could not straighten up, and said: this hu monk deacon has a back harder than her old man's.

A-Lian came out from inside.

She wore a blue skirt and jacket, not new, washed very clean, her hair in a high knot with a silver pin in it, no powder on her face, a bamboo basket in her hand, and in the basket jujube-paste cakes she had baked the night before. Shiyiniang followed behind her, carrying a small bundle for her. Yonan went to meet her and, by the custom, walked round her, walked three times, very slowly, the whole street counting, and at the third round A-Lian laughed first herself and said: stop going round, you're making me dizzy.

The party went north, out of the north gate of the West Market, past the corner of the Liquan Ward; at the corner people were already waiting: a few porters who often came to eat at the shop and had run up accounts, with a long bench set across the street and three men sitting on it, bare to the waist, one holding an empty bowl, one a rolling pin, and one holding nothing, but slapping his own belly, saying he'd been hungry three days; if the bridegroom gave no cakes, the bride wasn't getting past. Jingjing bowed to the three and said: today Lady A-Lian of the West Market goes out from her home, and Elder Yonan of the Yining Ward has come to fetch her; the Elder gives cakes, cakes Elder Ishaq of the monastery baked with his own hands; Elder Ishaq has baked cakes all his life and eaten half of them himself, and the other half are all in this basket today. He had the guard brothers bring up the bamboo baskets and hand them out to the blockers, a cake each. The belly-slapping porter took one, bit into it, and said: no filling; doesn't count. Jingjing said the ones with filling were in the bride's basket. So A-Lian took jujube-paste cakes from her basket and handed them out one by one, and when she came to the one with the rolling pin he was embarrassed to take one, and said he still owed her for three bowls of soup. A-Lian said never mind those three bowls of soup; take the cake. The one with the rolling pin took the cake, and said: cakes enough, but we want wine too. Yonan said the monastery's people didn't drink wine, and he didn't either. Jingjing said: the Elder doesn't drink; the Elder has brought vinegar; and had the two jars of vinegar brought up, saying this was vinegar from old Master Wang's in the West Market; those who can't drink wine, drink a bowl of vinegar—just as sour, just as joyful. Old Master Wang, following behind, heard it and was so delighted he slapped his leg, and said he'd sold vinegar all his life, and this was the first time anyone had used his vinegar to buy off a carriage-blocking. The bench was taken away; the people of the West Market had come this far, and now went back; they had to get back to the West Market to pack up before the gong.

All the way the locust flowers fell, fell on Yonan's new robe, fell in A-Lian's knot of hair, fell into the empty baskets the guard brothers carried. The people of the wards stood at their doors watching; some who knew him called out "Elder Yonan"; some who didn't asked whose wedding this was—why was the bridegroom so old, and why was the bride so old too? And those beside them said: you don't understand; this one's waited thirty years. At the south gate of the Yining Ward stood Yisi, in that grey robe of his, the one patched at the elbow, the purple robe locked in the chest, leading Lingbao by the hand. Lingbao was nine, growing fast, holding a willow basket, and in the basket were two willow crowns, from willow Gabriel had broken on the banks of the Wei three days before; he had sat under the gallery two days weaving them, and when he spoiled one, unpicked it, and when unpicked, wove it again; the willow leaves were a little dry, curling at the edges. Over the well outside the monastery gate lay a reed mat: that was the old Tang custom; on a wedding day every well the bride passed was to be covered; Ishaq had gone to draw water first thing, lifted it, looked, and put it back, muttering that the kitchen's water today would have to be carried from the well at the north end of the ward.

The wedding was held not in the hall but in the back courtyard. Yonan himself had asked it. Yisi asked him why, and he said: the hall is the Lord's; the back courtyard is men's. Yisi thought, and asked no more. Sargis and his men had taken down and washed that empty felt tent in the back courtyard and put it up again, with a piece of blue cloth hung at its door; when the Tang took a bride they put up a blue lodge, and the couple performed the rite in the lodge; this would count as the blue lodge.

The back courtyard was not large; on its west side was the west wing, the translating room, paper heaped under its window; on its east the kitchen, a few rows of scallions planted at the kitchen door; in the very middle a pomegranate tree, grown from a seedling an old believer of the monastery had brought from Liangzhou in early years, in full flower in the sixth month, the flowers red, one by one, like lit lamps in the dusk. Sabrisho had brought a few guard brothers early to sweep the courtyard, carry in long benches and set them out row by row, set them dead straight; when they were set he stepped back a few paces to look, and went up again and moved one bench half an inch.

More people came than expected. All the monastery's people came. Of the twenty-four guard brothers of those days a dozen-odd were left, their hair grizzled, standing at the back, their backs still straight, Sargis at the very edge, half a head taller than the rest. The few dozen Tang believers of the ward came too; Granny He had died some years before, and her grandson's wife came carrying her child. Official Pei came too, bringing his son who served in the Metropolitan Prefecture; Official Pei was past fifty now, still walking a little lopsided; he sat in the front row beside Ishaq, and the two of them talked a while, all about sheep. And there were others standing under the locust tree outside the monastery gate who did not come in: those who came every morning for bread; they had heard the old hu monk who gave out bread was marrying today, and they came, and having come did not come in at the gate, but stood and squatted under the tree and waited. Ishaq had two great basins of mutton soup carried out to them; those sheep he had bought in the second month and kept in the back courtyard four months, kept them till they were fatter than he. Just before dark, the rite was performed.

When A-Lian came into the back courtyard, the courtyard went quiet for a moment. She was fifty-one, her hair more than half white, put up in that high knot, the silver pin catching a little light in the dusk; not tall, somewhat stout, walking very steadily, her eyes ahead, not looking to either side. Jingjing stood behind Yonan and watched her come toward them, and moved his eyes away, onto the branches of the pomegranate tree; on a branch sat a sparrow, its head on one side, watching too.

Yonan stood under the pomegranate tree waiting for her. He wore that new robe, his back straight as a rod, his face still as it always was, clean, beardless, almost shining in the dusk. He watched her come toward him step by step, his two hands hanging at his sides, the fingers moving once, and again; Jingjing knew that was the wish to go and wash them. A-Lian came up to him and stopped, and A-Lian spoke first, in Tang, her voice very low, so that only the few standing near heard it: "That robe of yours—the seam's come open at the back."

Yonan was taken aback, and reached round to feel his back. A-Lian had already taken a needle from her sleeve, threaded, and told him to turn round, and right there under the pomegranate tree, before a whole courtyard of people, in three or five stitches sewed up that opened seam, and when it was sewn bent her head and bit off the thread with her teeth. The whole courtyard watched, and Ishaq's eyes went red again. Shiyiniang, standing in the crowd, turned her head to look at Yisi, and Yisi was looking at her too.

Elder Marya officiated. The year Yisi married he had officiated, and this time the elders had discussed it, and it was still he. The rite was performed by the old custom of the banks of the Two Rivers, only simplified, at the door of that felt tent hung with blue cloth. Marya took up an earthenware cup; in it was clear water, with a little earth in it brought from before the Holy Sepulchre, just as when Yisi married; Yonan did not drink wine, and A-Lian said she didn't either, so water it was. Marya said the blessing in Syriac; he had preached all his life and his voice was long gone hoarse; he said it very slowly, and when he had said it drew a cross over the cup with his hand. Yonan drank a mouthful, and A-Lian drank a mouthful. Yisi stood behind Marya in that grey robe, the scar on his left brow faded in colour after twenty years.

Lingbao came up holding the willow basket. He walked very carefully, step by step, his eyes fixed on the two crowns in the basket for fear one would fall; when he reached Marya, Marya took one from the basket and set it on Yonan's head, then took the other and set it on A-Lian's. The willow was a little dry, the leaves curling at the edges; as A-Lian's was set on, a leaf fell off onto her shoulder, and Yonan reached out and took it away for her. Lingbao stood to one side holding the empty basket, tilting his head back to look at A-Lian, at the crown on her head, at that silver pin of hers; he recognized it and said softly to his father: that's Ma's pin. Yisi said it was lent; it'd be given back the day after tomorrow. Lingbao said: then he could still see it tomorrow.

Babai sat at a small table to one side, opened the register, his eyes very close to it, and in the empty line below Yonan's name wrote two names, one in Syriac letters, Susanna, and one in Tang characters, A-Lian, the Tang characters written square and upright. When he had written them he had A-Lian come and make her mark. Beside the table Official Pei read out the marriage document he had written, written the Tang way: the man so-and-so, the woman so-and-so, the go-between so-and-so, the year, month and day; in the go-between's place he had written himself. A-Lian took the pen and at the end of that line wrote four letters, the tail of the Nun flicking up. Babai looked at it and did not have her change it.

When the rite was done, it was the feast.

Ishaq's mutton soup was brought, basin after basin; the sesame cakes had been baked first thing by the young man at A-Lian's shop and sent over, stack after stack, still too hot to hold. The people on the benches began to talk, began to laugh; Xu Jing began on Xizhou again, and this time no one stuffed a cake in his mouth; everyone listened while he turned a band of forty or fifty bandits into two hundred, and told how Jingjing, ten years old that year, had stood at the gate of the monastery north of the city and recited a passage of scripture at the bandit chief, and the bandit chief, not understanding it, thought it was a spell and ran away in fright. Jingjing sat beside Yonan listening and did not stop him; that part Xu Jing had made up; no bandits had ever come to the monastery north of the city that year at Xizhou; but Xu Jing was telling it happily, and the whole courtyard was listening happily, so he let him tell it. Halfway through the feast, Lingbao was carrying a bowl of mutton soup toward the gallery of the west wing, for Narsai, who was standing there; at the foot of the steps his foot caught and he pitched forward, the bowl flying out of his hands; the first to reach out was Narsai. In his right hand he still held that little pen-knife; his left hand seized the child by the arm, and his right wrist turned outward, the blade out, a foot from the child's face; the soup bowl smashed on the steps. Shiyiniang, over there, saw it and called out: "Elder—the knife." Narsai looked down at his right hand, put the knife away in his sleeve, set Lingbao upright, brushed the soup off him, said nothing at all, and leaned back against the pillar again. Lingbao stood there, looked at the broken bowl on the ground, looked at Narsai, and said: Elder, the soup's spilled. Narsai said: spilled is spilled; go and ladle another.

Yisi stood up and said he would say a few words.

The courtyard went quiet. Yisi stood under the pomegranate tree holding an earthenware cup of clear water. He looked at Yonan, looked at A-Lian, looked at the courtyard full of people, and said he was no talker. Someone below laughed; the monastery's people all knew that was the first thing he said every time he opened his mouth. He said he came to Chang'an at seventeen, and two years later Elder Yonan came from the Patriarchate and became bishop and had charge of him, strict charge; if he recited the scripture with one word wrong he had to recite it again ten times. More laughter. He paused, and said: later Elder Yonan went back; the day he left it was snowing, and he told him to keep the lamp lit, and he kept it lit; later Elder Yonan came again; the road he came by, he knew; his son had travelled it. He did not look at Jingjing; Jingjing, sitting beside Yonan, did not look at him either. Yisi said: "He's seventy-one this year. He's waited thirty years." He raised the cup, not high, to about his chest, and said: "The road's open." He drank that little clear water in the cup, and sat down.

The courtyard was still, and then Sargis in the back row shouted something in Syriac, and the guard brothers all shouted with him, till the whole courtyard hummed; the people from the West Market couldn't understand it, and clapped along.

Then it was Jingjing. He was thirty, taller than Yisi and shorter than Yonan, his face very pale—whom he got that from, nobody said. He had charge now of the monastery's translating; of that pile of paper in the west wing, half was in his hand; today he was groomsman, and the groomsman had to say a few words; that sheet was still pressed under the inkstone in the west wing; he did not go to fetch it; those few lines he remembered.

He stood up, first bowed to Yonan, and then bowed to A-Lian, bowed in the Tang manner, the bow of a disciple before an elder, very properly, exactly the bow he had made to Old Duan in Lingzhou the year he was ten. He said Elder Yonan was his teacher. He spoke the speech of Chang'an, very clearly, not fast, not slow, and the Tang believers of the ward sitting at the back, every one of them, understood. He said that twenty years ago at Xizhou the seven elders took turns at night coming to his room to borrow the lamp and learn characters: Elder Ishaq learned medicine, sickness, peace; Elder Babai learned scripture; Elder Gabriel learned light; Elder Yonan asked him only four characters, lotus, flower, ten, cross, and the others he wrote a few times, but the character for lotus, lian, he wrote twenty-odd times. He said that since coming to Chang'an, all these years at the sand tray by the west wing door, Elder Yonan had written only one character, written it twenty years, written it better than he. He paused, and said: "That character is heart."

The courtyard was very quiet; pomegranate flowers fell, fell on the benches, fell on people's shoulders.

Jingjing said Elder Yonan had told him that this character was taught him many years ago by someone in Chang'an; he was slow, and had learned only this one. He said that today, the one who taught him to write this character had come; from now on he could learn the second. As he said this he remembered which two characters were struck through on that sheet. He turned toward A-Lian, bowed once more, and said: "Lady A-Lian, from now on the Elder's Tang characters are entrusted to you."

A-Lian sat there looking at him. Her hands lay on her knees; on her blue skirt was a small patch of flour, rubbed on, probably, in the shop that morning before she came out; her fingers wiped at that patch once, and again, and it did not come off. She looked at him, looked until he straightened his back, until he moved his eyes away, onto the willow crown on her head; only then did she speak, and say: "Good." Just that one word. Jingjing sat down. Ishaq beside him was wiping his eyes; Haddad bent his head over his soup; the handkerchief in Shiyiniang's hand was crushed into a ball. Yisi held that empty cup, rubbing the rim back and forth with his thumb. Yonan, his head raised, looked at Jingjing as if to fix his face in memory. Jingjing remembered that twenty years ago, in that small room at Ctesiphon, the first time Yonan saw him, he had looked at him like this too.

Narsai had stood at the edge of the courtyard the whole time.

He did not sit on a bench, did not drink soup; when Yisi raised his cup he did not raise one; when the guard brothers shouted he did not shout. He stood under the gallery of the west wing, his back against a pillar, a reed pen and a small knife in his hands, trimming. A shaving fell, and another. Someone brought him a bowl of soup; he nodded and did not take it, and the man set it down on the steps of the gallery, and the soup slowly cooled, a white skin of fat forming on it. When Jingjing sat down he glanced toward the gallery; Narsai was looking at him too, and their eyes met. Narsai's eyes were still like two stones, and nothing could be read in them. He finished trimming the pen, tried the nib with his thumb, put the knife away in his sleeve, nodded once to Jingjing, turned, and left.

As he left he passed under the pomegranate tree, past Yonan and A-Lian. Yonan saw him and stood up. Narsai did not stop, but passed. Yonan stood there watching him go out of the back courtyard gate, and sat down again. A-Lian turned her head, glanced at him, did not ask, and reached out and picked off a pomegranate petal stuck to his cuff.

The guests went away by degrees.

When the believers of the ward left, one by one they bowed to Yonan and said a pile of words of good luck, and Yonan answered each in turn, haltingly, and toward the end Jingjing beside him answered for him. Those under the locust tree outside the monastery gate drank up the mutton soup and dispersed too; before leaving, a child brought the empty bowl in, licked spotless. When Official Pei left he took Yisi's hand and said that twice in his life he had written a marriage document, once for Yisi and once today, and today was the first time he had felt himself sitting on the bride's family's side. Yisi said: you always were.

Jingjing was helping stack the benches in the back courtyard. He was carrying a long bench toward the west wing, and halfway there someone behind him called: "Deacon." It was A-Lian. She held a bowl, steam rising from it, and said: "I hear the deacon's stomach is weak. Today's mutton soup is rich; this will settle it." Jingjing looked down at the bowl: in it was hot water, a few slices of ginger floating on top, cut very fine. He put down the bench and took the bowl; it was hot; he held it in both hands and said: thank you, Lady A-Lian. A-Lian looked at him; her eyes were dark in the dusk; she nodded, turned, and went back under the pomegranate tree to clear the bowls and chopsticks from the table. Yonan stood there waiting for her and helped her stack the bowls, stacked them crooked, and she stacked them again. Jingjing stood under the gallery and drank that bowl of ginger water, and when he had drunk it he washed the bowl clean and took it back to the kitchen.

The ward drum had sounded; Yonan and A-Lian were to go back to that small courtyard at the north end of the ward. Yonan went first to the monastery gate and added oil to the lamp, and when he had added it stood under the lamp waiting for A-Lian. A-Lian came out and stood beside him, and the two of them stood a while under the lamp, then went off toward the north end of the ward. Shiyiniang took a small lantern and saw them on their way, saw them to the mouth of the lane, and came back.

That night Yisi walked Jingjing back to his room. Jingjing's room was at the north end of the west wing, next to the scripture room; Yisi and Shiyiniang and Lingbao lived in the side courtyard; half a courtyard lay between them. The two of them walked along the gallery; the moon had not yet risen; the gallery was dark; only that small lamp at the monastery gate shone a point of light far off. At Jingjing's door Yisi stopped and said: that water she gave you—you drank it. Jingjing said he had. Yisi said nothing more, but turned and went toward the side courtyard. Jingjing stood at the door and watched him go a few steps, then called once more: Pa. Yisi stopped and turned his head. Jingjing said: "It was hot."

That night Jingjing went back into his room, did not light the lamp, sat a while, then got up again, felt for the brass key to the scripture room at his belt, opened the scripture room cupboard, lit a small lamp, took out the register of baptisms, and turned to the page of the ninth month of the fifth year of Tianbao. That thin line of Syriac was still there, Son of A-Lian, and below it those four letters, the tail of the Nun flicking up. He thought of the line Babai had written in the register of persons at dusk today; that was another book; he did not go to fetch it; he only thought that below today's line she had made her mark again, and that tail too had probably flicked up. He shut the register, locked it away, and blew out the lamp.

The next day Yonan as usual came at first light to the monastery gate to give out bread, and when he had given it out went to sit at the west wing door and write; beside the sand tray there was a new bamboo stylus, newly shaved, smooth, sharpened to a point at one end and rounded at the other, so that it did not dig into the hand. Yonan picked it up and asked Jingjing who had shaved it. Jingjing looked at the knife marks on the stylus, stroke by stroke, level as water, and said he didn't know. So Yonan took that stylus and wrote a heart, and when it was written smoothed it out and wrote another. Under the window, Narsai was cutting paper, and did not raise his head.

The day after that, Shiyiniang went to the gruel kitchen to collect the accounts, and A-Lian came over from the south side holding something wrapped in a handkerchief: that silver pin, and in the handkerchief two jujube-paste cakes as well. She gave the pin to Shiyiniang and said: the day after tomorrow, I give it back; today is the day after tomorrow. Shiyiniang took it and said: you remember well. A-Lian said she had kept accounts all her life; what she owed people, she wouldn't let go one day over. Shiyiniang put the pin back in her own hair, took the cakes, and asked her whether she was used to the rooms over there. A-Lian said she was, only there were a lot of mosquitoes under that grapevine; he got up at night and fanned them off her, fanned till dawn; she said you sleep, and he said he couldn't sleep; at seventy-one you don't need much sleep. As she said it she laughed herself, and coughed twice, and Shiyiniang told her to stop laughing and go and get Yisi to make her up some medicine. A-Lian said she would; this time she'd drink it.
