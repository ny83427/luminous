Volume Four　Xiangji Temple

Chapter 16　The Guardian Kings

At the end of the eighth month there were more troops outside Fengxiang every day.

First came the troops of Anxi, led by Li Siye, a big man on a big horse who stood a head taller than anyone in a crowd. The Anxi men used the long mo blade: stood on end it was taller than a man, edged on both sides, and they walked with it on their shoulders, the blade-heads swaying, while the children of the camp trailed after them, staring until their eyes went fixed.

Then came troops from the south, whose speech no one could understand.

Then came the Uighurs. The Uighur yabghu led four thousand horsemen down from the north, and you could hear the hoofbeats from far off. The Uighurs loved to whoop—they whooped dismounting, whooped mounting, whooped over their wine, high and long, and when the camp dogs heard it they put their tails between their legs and hid under the carts.

And there was one more troop, who wore black and carried black banners.

The first time Old Duan saw them was at the camp gate. He was squatting there gnawing a flatbread when that troop in black rode past in front of him, the horses very tall and very strong, the bows very short, the swords very curved. He forgot to chew the bread in his mouth, and watched them all the way until they were far off before he turned his head and said to Yisi beside him:

"It's them."

"Who?"

"The ones I saw on the road," said Old Duan. "The ones going east. I even had Jingjing make a note of it on paper."

He swallowed the mouthful of bread, swallowed it a bit too fast, choked, and spent a long while thumping his chest.

"Ten thousand li I walked," he said, "and here I run into them again."

When the men in camp knew Old Duan was back from the west, they all came to hear him tell of it.

By day there was drill and no time for it. In the evening, when drill was over and they had eaten, a few men would sit round a fire with Old Duan in the middle, and he would begin. The first few nights he told of the Arabs, of that archway at Ctesiphon with half of it fallen, of how the robbers in the Pamirs rolled rocks down from the peaks. As he went on there were more and more listeners, one fire became three or four, and in the end men from other camps came running over too and stood round the outer ring craning their necks.

Hou Siyuan came every night without fail. He sat closest to Old Duan, feeding the fire as he listened, and fed it so often that the flames kept leaping up at Old Duan's beard.

On the fifth night, when he had finished with the Arabs and the robbers, someone asked:

"Old Duan, those hu monks—what did they do all the way?"

Old Duan thought and said: "Chanted scripture."

"What scripture?"

"How would I understand that?" said Old Duan. "But at night when they'd made camp, one of them told stories. His face had been burned, half of it all wrinkled—he told them well. There was an elder who could speak Tang standing by to put it into Tang, and Jingjing helped too, and I listened all the way and got most of it. The old ones who wrote their scriptures and founded their teaching, they call them all Dharma Kings."

"Dharma Kings? Same as the Buddhist monks?"

"Not the same at all," said Old Duan. "With the monks there's just the one Dharma King, enthroned in the middle of the great hall. With them, you grab a handful and they're all Dharma Kings—the ones that wrote the scriptures are Dharma Kings, the ones that spread the teaching are Dharma Kings too; I counted the whole way and couldn't get to the end of them."

He spat into the fire, and the spit hissed. He cleared his throat.

"Out of that handful," he said, "there are four that matter most, the ones that guard the teaching. I call them the Four Great Guardian Dharma Kings."

Yisi was sitting by the fire that night too.

He sat in the outer ring, leaning against a cart, and had been listening with his eyes shut. At the words Four Great Guardian Dharma Kings he opened his eyes. From boyhood on, in the church at Balkh and in the Daqin monastery in the Yining Ward of Chang'an, he had heard elders preach at least a few hundred times, and never once had anyone said Guardian Dharma Kings.

Old Duan did not see him open his eyes. Old Duan was warming to his tale.

"The first," said Old Duan, holding up one finger, "is called the Dharma King Mingtai."

"Mingtai? Sounds like the name of a drugshop."

"Never mind what it sounds like, that's what he's called," said Old Duan. "This Dharma King, before he followed their Lord, was a tax collector."

Someone by the fire laughed out loud.

"What are you laughing at? What's wrong with a tax collector?" Old Duan glared at him. "A tax collector, what's he got in his hand? A brush. An account book. Who owes how much, who's paid how much, entry by entry, all written down clear. This Dharma King Mingtai, after he followed their Lord, didn't throw that brush away either. He kept it."

He jabbed the finger forward.

"A judge's brush."

The men round the fire said "Ohh."

"The judge in the hall of the King of Hell holds a brush too," said Old Duan. "One tick of the brush and your name's gone from the Book of Life and Death. The Dharma King Mingtai's brush is worse than the judge's. He kept accounts half his life; where the vital points on a man are, he's got the whole ledger in his head. One touch of the tip on you—"

His finger dabbed the air.

"—and you can't move."

"Pressure points!" cried Hou Siyuan.

"That's it, pressure points." Old Duan nodded, very pleased, as if he had taught the trick himself.

Yisi, leaning against the cart, opened his mouth, and shut it again.

"The second," said Old Duan, holding up two fingers, "the Dharma King Lujia."

"What does this one do?"

"Doctor," said Old Duan. "Sees to the sick."

"What weapon's a doctor got?"

"A doctor's got a knife," said Old Duan. "Any of you seen a doctor's knife? I have. When I took an arrow in the arm, Xu Jing dug out the head with one just like it. Thin, narrow, pointed at both ends, like a willow leaf. What the Dharma King Lujia uses is a willow-leaf blade."

He put his two fingers together and measured them against his own arm.

"This blade doesn't cut men down," he said. "It's just for cutting away rotten flesh. Wherever a man's gone bad, wherever there's poison in him, one cut—the rotten's off, the good's left. When he's done, the man can still live."

An old soldier by the fire nodded and said: "That's a good weapon, that is."

"Isn't it," said Old Duan. "If we had one like him in our camp, half the men in the wounded tents wouldn't die, and Xu Jing's rotten old knife—I wouldn't have had to bite on a leather strap and suffer it."

This time no one by the fire laughed. The old soldier had only three fingers left on his left hand; the other two had rotted off in the wounded tents.

"The third, the Dharma King Yuhannan." Old Duan held up three fingers. "A fisherman."

"What does a fisherman use?"

"A net," said Old Duan. "And a fork. One net over your head and you can't get away. One fish-fork stuck in you and you can't get away either. The net's for trapping, the fork's for taking. Think about it—in a battle, a man in front of you, net in one hand, fork in the other, you charge him, one net over you, one fork—"

"That's like the Uighurs lassoing horses," someone said.

Old Duan thought. "Like that. Worse than lassoing horses. Lassoing's just for horses; he lassoes the man and all."

At the back, Yisi coughed.

Old Duan looked round, saw him, grinned at him with half his face, and turned back.

"The fourth," said Old Duan, holding up four fingers, "the Dharma King Mojuci. This one's the youngest."

"What does he use?"

"This one," said Old Duan, lowering his voice, and the men round the fire all leaned in, "has a cloak."

"A cloak?"

"The night their Lord was arrested," said Old Duan, "all the others ran away. The Dharma King Mojuci was just a lad then, wearing a cloak, following behind. The soldiers making the arrest saw him and reached out to grab him. Got a grip on his cloak—"

He grabbed Hou Siyuan by the collar. Hou Siyuan jumped, and the stick in his hand fell into the fire.

"—he throws off the cloak," Old Duan let go, "and he's away. All the soldier's got left in his hand is a cloak."

The men round the fire roared.

"The cicada sheds its shell!" someone shouted.

"Not finished," said Old Duan. "When he ran, he had a cord in his hand too, with a stone in a pouch on it. He whirls it, whoosh-whoosh, round and round, and then lets go—"

His hand circled twice over his head and flung outward.

"—the stone flies off and hits a big fellow smack on the forehead. That big fellow was over ten foot tall—"

"Old Duan."

Yisi spoke.

The men round the fire all turned to look at him.

Yisi stood up from beside the cart, walked into the ring and squatted down by Old Duan. He looked at Old Duan with an expression hard to put a name to. Old Duan had known him more than a year and had never seen such a look on his face—as if he wanted to laugh, and wanted to cry, and had toothache.

"The stone," said Yisi, "was Duohui's."

Old Duan was taken aback. "Duohui?"

"Duohui is another man, a thousand years before Mojuci," said Yisi. "He slung a stone and killed a big fellow."

"And the Dharma King Mojuci?"

"Mojuci had no stone."

"Then what did he have?"

Yisi thought a moment and said: "He had a piece of linen."

"A cloak."

"Linen," said Yisi. "He had nothing on but a piece of linen wrapped round him. The soldiers grabbed the linen, he let go of it, and ran off naked."

There was a moment's silence by the fire.

Then the laughter burst out. Hou Siyuan laughed so hard he fell backwards off the stump he was sitting on, all four limbs in the air. The man who had said the cicada sheds its shell slapped his thighs. The men standing round the outer ring did not know what was funny but laughed along, asking the men next to them what they were laughing at, and the men next to them could not say either, and laughed all the harder.

Old Duan did not laugh. He looked at Yisi, frowning, as if thinking over a very hard question.

"You mean," he asked, "he ran off with his backside bare?"

"Not a stitch," said Yisi. "All the soldier had left was the linen."

Old Duan thought a long time and said: "That's even better. Stark naked and still got away from the soldiers."

Yisi looked at him and said nothing.

The laughter slowly died.

Old Duan would not let go of the stone. He said if the stone wasn't Mojuci's, it had to have a home somewhere—who was Duohui, and who was the big fellow he killed? Master Yi, you'll have to tell it properly, or I listened the whole way for nothing.

Yisi sat down by the fire, picked up a stick, turned it over in his hand and threw it into the fire.

"Duohui kept sheep," he said. "Youngest in the family. His elder brothers had all gone to war, and he stayed home with the sheep. In those days their country was at war with a country to the west, and the two armies were camped facing each other across a valley. From the other side came a big fellow in bronze armour, carrying a spear with a shaft as thick as a weaver's beam, and every day he stood in the valley and called for a challenger, called for forty days, and on this side not one man dared go out."

"Forty days?" said Hou Siyuan. "Didn't he lose his voice?"

"Duohui was taking food to his brothers and heard him." Yisi paid him no attention. "He went to the king and said, I'll go. The king was uneasy and gave him his own armour to wear; he put it on, took two steps, said he wasn't used to it, and took it off again. He went to the stream and picked five smooth stones, put them in his shepherd's bag, took his sling, and went out."

"Just went out like that? Not even armour?"

"He said he couldn't walk in it," said Yisi. "The big fellow saw it was a boy and cursed him: you come at me with a stick, am I a dog? Duohui didn't waste a word. He took a stone from the bag, slung it, and it sank into the man's forehead. The man fell on his face. Duohui ran up, drew the man's own sword, and cut off his head."

There was not a sound round the fire. After a while the old soldier said: "Good lad."

"Afterwards he became king," said Yisi. "Fought all his life. Before he was king, the women in the cities beat their drums and sang: The old king has slain his thousands, Duohui his ten thousands."

"Ten thousands?" Hou Siyuan was counting on his fingers. "What year would he be done killing?"

"When women sing, they sing for joy. I don't suppose anyone counted," said Yisi.

Old Duan stabbed the twig in his hand into the ground and said: "Now that's a Dharma King."

Yisi glanced at him.

"He could fight, that's true," said Yisi. "I'm not fooling you."

"There's another," said Yisi, "who could really fight too. His name was Moushi."

Old Duan's ears pricked up at once. He no longer cared about guardians or not guardians, Four Great or Eight Great.

"Moushi was the first of their people to lead the people out," said Yisi. "He grew up in a palace as a boy—a foreign king's palace—while his own people were outside doing forced labour for them. When he was grown, one day he went out to look, and saw an overseer beating one of his people, beating him to death. He looked this way and that, saw no one, went up and killed the overseer, and buried him in the sand."

"Killed him with one punch?"

"The scripture doesn't say what he used, only that he killed him," said Yisi. "Later, when he led the people out, a tribe came to raid them on the road. He sent his men to fight, and stood himself on top of a hill with his hands raised. While his hands were raised, this side won; when his arms got tired and came down, the other side won. In the end two men, one on each side, held up his arms and kept them raised until the sun went down. That battle was won."

"Raise your hands and you win?" someone asked.

"Raising his hands was praying to Heaven," said Yisi. "The battle was still fought by the men below."

Old Duan slapped his thigh. "Moushi, Duohui. Right—those two had real skill."

Yisi nodded.

"These two men," he said, "really did have strength of arms. Our scriptures record it plainly: they killed, fought battles, led armies. There's no denying it, and no need to deny it."

He paused. The men round the fire waited for him to go on, but he looked down at the fire for a while before he went on.

"But when Moushi killed that overseer, the next day people knew. He had to run, ran off into the wilderness, kept sheep for someone, and kept them forty years. And Duohui fought all his life, and when he was old he wanted to build a house for Alaha. Alaha would not let him build it. He said: you have shed too much blood. Let your son build it."

"Alaha?" asked Hou Siyuan.

"That's the Heavenly Honoured One," Old Duan answered for Yisi. "They call the Heavenly Honoured One Alaha."

"Wouldn't let him build it?" said Old Duan. "He fought Alaha's battles all his life, and in the end he's not even allowed to build him a house?"

"He wasn't," said Yisi. "That house was built by his son."

Old Duan opened his mouth as if to say something, and did not. He glanced at Yisi's waist, where a short mace usually hung; tonight it was not there.

"Then let me ask you one more," said Old Duan. "This one I saw with my own eyes."

"With your own eyes?"

"At Ctesiphon," said Old Duan. "In the market there was a merchant from Fulin with a stall selling painted boards—wood, the size of your palm, with people painted on them, gold behind, faces painted long, eyes painted big. I squatted there looking at them half the day, and the merchant thought I was going to buy and showed me them one by one. There was one with an old man on it, bald, beard pointed, holding a scroll in one hand and a long sword in the other, and the sword longer than half of him. I asked Jingjing who it was, Jingjing asked the merchant, and the merchant said: Baolu."

He turned to the men round the fire.

"The Dharma King Baolu," he said. "The one with the sword. Think about it—an old bookish man, and the painter doesn't give him a brush, he paints him a sword. So that sword must have been his great skill."

The men round the fire all nodded; it made sense.

"I thought at the time," said Old Duan, "this Dharma King Baolu, his swordsmanship must have been something."

Yisi laughed. He seldom laughed like that; his shoulders shook.

"Baolu made tents," he said.

"Made tents?"

"Wherever he went, he preached, and when he ran out of money he made tents for people to earn his keep," said Yisi. "He read a great deal and wrote a good many letters; of the books left in the scriptures, he alone has more than a dozen. He never used a sword in his life."

"Then the sword in the picture?"

"In the end, in the city of Rome, the authorities cut off his head," said Yisi. "With a sword. The painter put that sword in his hand so that whoever looked at the picture would remember how he died."

The fire was quiet again.

It was a long while before Old Duan spoke, and his voice had dropped. "You mean what he's holding is the very one that cut his head off?"

"Yes. The painter wants whoever looks at it to remember that."

"Then what was he after?"

Yisi thought.

"He wrote of a sword himself, in a letter," said Yisi. "He said those who believe in the Messiah must put on a whole suit of armour—truth bound round the waist for a belt, righteousness on the breast for a breastplate, faith in the hand for a shield, salvation on the head for a helmet. And they must take a sword, and that sword is the word of Alaha."

"Alaha's word is a sword?" said Hou Siyuan.

"That's what he wrote."

Old Duan bent his head and thought a while. He thought very seriously, muttering: "Belt, breastplate, shield, helmet … sword." When he had counted them he looked up. "That's the whole kit."

"It's the whole kit," said Yisi. "Count them again. Which one of them cuts a man down?"

The men round the fire laughed and all began talking at once.

Someone said that the year Shaolin Monastery helped Emperor Taizong fight Wang Shichong, thirteen monks went out, every one with a staff, and beat Wang Shichong's nephew till he messed himself, and Emperor Taizong gave them land afterwards. The Buddhists have their Thirteen Staff Monks; your Luminous Dharma Kings, only two of them can fight, and both a thousand years ago—a bit thin on the ground.

Old Duan said they might be few, but the weapons were complete: brush, knife, net, fork, linen, stone—whatever you want, they've got it. And besides, they've got twenty-four monk-soldiers who use staves too, iron-shod at both ends, harder than Shaolin's.

Someone said, what about those Dharma Kings against us? Against the Anxi mo blades, who'd win?

Old Duan said one stroke of a mo blade smashes man and horse. One dab of the judge's brush and a man can't move. One makes you smash, the other makes you not move; each has its merits.

Someone said, but if it came to a fight—

"If it's Guardian Dharma Kings you want," a soldier by the fire called Big-Head Wang suddenly put in, "these ones of yours fall a bit short."

Big-Head Wang fed the horses; he had a big head and a thick neck, said little as a rule, and when he did open his mouth it was to argue. Everyone turned to look at him, and he grew pleased with himself, tucked his two hands into his sleeves and said: "Kang Alu in the stables—you know him? Sogdian, a Manichaean. He told me the Manichaeans have Four Great Guardian Dharma Kings too. Much tougher than these of yours."

Old Duan's eyebrows shot up.

"Four Great Guardian Dharma Kings?" he said. "I made that name up. How come the Manichaeans have four as well?"

"They've had them for ages," said Big-Head Wang.

"For ages?" said Old Duan. "I only said it a few nights ago, and today they've had them for ages? Some night he squatted in the outer ring and heard it, went back and changed it a bit, and he's using it to bamboozle you."

The men round the fire roared. Big-Head Wang went red and his neck grew thicker still. "Do you want to hear it or not?"

"I do," said Old Duan. "Go on."

Big-Head Wang cleared his throat and held up one finger, copying Old Duan.

"The first, the Golden-Haired Lion King," he said. "A head of golden hair like a lion's; he stands there, opens his mouth and roars, and a thousand men opposite, their ears all go deaf, and some of them drop down on the spot."

"One roar and they drop?" said Hou Siyuan.

"One roar and they drop," said Big-Head Wang. "Kang Alu said so. The second, the White-Browed Eagle King. Two eyebrows white as snow, hanging right down to his cheeks, and ten fingers like an eagle's talons; one grab at you and that's five holes. The third, the Green-Winged Bat King—runs faster than a horse, goes over roofs and up walls like a bat, comes out at night and sucks people's blood."

"Sucks blood?" Someone by the fire drew back a little.

"Sucks blood," said Big-Head Wang, giving himself the creeps. "The fourth is the most tremendous of all, the Purple-Robed Dragon King. She's a woman, dressed all in purple, more beautiful than a woman in a painting, and such a swimmer she can stay underwater a whole day without coming up."

For a moment no one by the fire made a sound. Some sat with their mouths open, some looked at each other; the old soldier muttered "a woman can be a Dharma King too," and Hou Siyuan forgot for a long while to put the stick in his hand on the fire.

Old Duan was stunned for quite a while before he turned his head to find Yisi. "Master Yi, is there such a thing?"

Everyone turned to look at Yisi.

Yisi sat beside the cart, his face wearing the same look as before, as if he had toothache. He thought a long time. He knew the Manichaeans called themselves the party of light, said that the world was light and darkness and the two had been fighting a thousand years and were not done yet; he knew too that their people were divided into grades with strange titles—muzhe, fuduodan—and he had heard it said in Chang'an that in the Kaiyuan years a fuduodan had even come to the capital and presented a scripture to the court. But lions, eagles, bats and dragons he had never heard of.

Yet to say there were none, just because he had never heard of them—he could not bring himself to say that either.

"I don't know," said Yisi. "I've never read the Manichaean scriptures. Whether they have them or not, I wouldn't dare say they do, and I wouldn't dare say they don't."

Big-Head Wang slapped his thigh. "Hear that? Even Master Yi doesn't dare say they don't!"

The fire buzzed again. Some said, then they do; some said, Master Yi said he didn't know, not that they did; some said not knowing means they do, if there weren't any Master Yi would have said so long ago. The more they argued the truer it got: the Golden-Haired Lion King's hair grew longer with every telling, and the men who dropped at one roar went from a thousand to three thousand.

Old Duan had said nothing all this while; he was frowning, counting something on his fingers. When he had finished counting, he looked up and asked Big-Head Wang: "That Golden-Haired Lion King of yours—if he met the Dharma King Mingtai, who'd win?"

Big-Head Wang was caught off guard. "Well… well, he roars—"

"He roars," said Old Duan, "so he's got to open his mouth, right? He opens his mouth, and the Dharma King Mingtai's brush dabs him right under the throat. Once he's dabbed, can he still roar?"

Big-Head Wang's mouth hung open, and he had no answer.

"And the bat?" Hou Siyuan was getting into it now.

"The bat comes out at night," said Old Duan. "The Dharma King Yuhannan's got a net, he's a fisherman, casting nets at night is his trade—one cast, and bat or no bat, it's all fish."

"And the Purple-Robed Dragon King? She swims!"

Old Duan thought, and was out of words. He looked at Yisi; Yisi was looking at the fire, not at him.

"The Purple-Robed Dragon King," Old Duan said at last, "is a woman. Our Dharma Kings don't raise their hands to women."

The men round the fire laughed till they were falling over each other. Big-Head Wang shouted in a fury that tomorrow he'd drag Kang Alu over to have it out. Old Duan said drag him over then; I'd like to ask him what year those four Dharma Kings of his were made, and how many days before mine.

Yisi leaned against the cart and had not laughed all this while. After a moment he turned his face into the shadow, and his shoulders shook twice.

"Master Yi," said a young soldier. He was seventeen or eighteen, sitting by the fire, and had hardly said anything all evening. "Of all these Dharma Kings, which one's the mightiest?"

The fire grew quiet again.

Everyone was looking at Yisi.

Yisi sat by the fire looking at the fire. The flames leapt and leapt, shining on his face and lighting up his brown eyes. He looked a long time before he spoke. He spoke very slowly, biting each word clear.

"For mightiness," he said, "not one of these counts. They spent their whole lives writing, and what they wrote was not about themselves, but about another man. For mightiness, it's that man."

"That man," asked the young soldier, "what weapon did he use?"

Yisi thought a while again.

"He is called Mishihe, the Messiah," said Yisi. "He had no weapon."

"None at all? Not even a stick?"

"Not even a stick."

Someone by the fire said something under his breath, and the man next to him nudged him.

"That night," said Yisi, "the very night Mojuci lost his linen, soldiers came to take him. There was a man beside him who had a sword, drew it, and struck one of the soldiers. Cut off one of his ears."

"Good stroke!" said Hou Siyuan.

Yisi glanced at him.

"The man who cut off the ear was called Cenwenseng," said Yisi. "A fisherman too, like Yuhannan. Of all of them he was the first to follow the Messiah, and the loudest; that very evening at supper he'd said, even if all the others run, I won't."

"He had guts."

"The Messiah told him to put his sword away," said Yisi. "The Messiah said: those who take the sword will die by the sword. Then he put that ear back on the soldier."

There was not a sound left round the fire.

Only the fire burning, crackling. Far off in the Uighur camp someone was whooping, high and long; he whooped a while, and stopped.

"P… put it back on?" asked Hou Siyuan. His voice was much smaller.

"Put it back on, as if it had never been cut."

"And then?"

"And then the soldiers took him away."

Hou Siyuan's mouth hung open and did not close for a long time.

It was a long while before Old Duan spoke.

"That skill," he said, "is bigger than pressure points."

Nobody laughed.

Old Duan picked up a twig from by his foot and poked the fire. Sparks flew up, high up, flared once in the dark, and went out.

"Master Yi," he said. "Then who killed him?"

"He was nailed to wood," said Yisi. "The authorities nailed him."

"And the Dharma Kings? Where were they then?"

Yisi said: "They ran away."

"All of them?"

"Nearly all."

The fire buzzed; several men started talking at once.

"Ran away?" said the old soldier. "The master's taken and the disciples run off one by one?"

"That's a disgrace," someone said. "Why would you put a disgrace in the scripture?"

"Right," said another. "When our camp loses a battle, the report has to call it luring the enemy in deep. And them—they run off, and write it down themselves, for people after them to read out."

"These Dharma Kings," said Hou Siyuan, scratching his head, "don't seem too particular."

Old Duan said nothing and turned to look at Yisi.

"They wrote it themselves," said Yisi. "Mingtai wrote it, Lujia wrote it, Yuhannan wrote it, Mojuci wrote it too. That bit about Mojuci losing his linen and running off naked—nobody else wrote it; he wrote it himself."

"Wrote about his own bare backside?" said Hou Siyuan.

"He wrote it himself. Nobody else mentions it."

Hou Siyuan's mouth hung open; he wanted to laugh, and didn't.

"What they meant to write about was the Messiah," said Yisi. "What he was like that night, they wrote as it was. What they themselves were like that night, they wrote as it was along with it. If they'd only picked out what looked good, the people after them would read this far and not know what kind of men he went to die for."

The old soldier nodded and after a long while said: "Those were honest men."

"There's worse," said Yisi. "Cenwenseng didn't run far; he followed into the yard of the authorities and sat by the fire warming himself. Someone recognised him and asked: aren't you one of that man's lot? He said no. Someone else asked, and again he said no. When he was asked the third time, he swore an oath and said: I don't even know the man. As he said it, the cock crowed."

"The cock crowed?"

"The Messiah had told him before: before the cock crows, you will say three times you don't know me," said Yisi. "When the cock crowed, he remembered, and went out and wept."

Old Duan poked the fire again. He poked it very slowly, one poke and a pause.

"The very one with the loudest voice, who said even if the others ran he wouldn't?" he said.

"The very one."

"Ran away," said Old Duan, "and then came back, and wrote a book."

"Yes," said Yisi. "Cenwenseng died in the city of Rome too, about the same time as Baolu. They say that when he was to be nailed to the wood, he begged them to nail him upside down, saying he was not worthy to be like the Messiah."

Old Duan nodded and said nothing more.

The men round the fire said nothing more either. A few stood up, slapped the dust off their seats and went back to the tents. As they went they trod very softly, as if afraid of waking someone.

They broke up late that night.

When most had gone and the fire had shrunk to a heap of red embers with a film of white ash on top, Hou Siyuan still had not gone; he squatted there drawing on the ground with a twig, a circle and another circle. Old Duan sat on the stump, Yisi beside him.

Hun Shizhi had come at some point too, and had been standing in the shadow beyond the outer ring with his arms folded, not making a sound. Now he came over, squatted by the fire and held out his two hands to warm them.

"Over at Pugu's," Hun Shizhi said to Yisi, "tomorrow someone'll be saying you were preaching in camp again."

Yisi said: "I wasn't. He was."

He glanced at Old Duan.

Hun Shizhi glanced at Old Duan too, and chuckled. That half-missing ear of his twitched in the firelight.

"The shepherd boy who killed the big fellow," said Hun Shizhi. "That one I like. Next time you tell it, I'll bring my camp along."

"He told it today." Old Duan pointed at Yisi.

"Then have him tell it again," said Hun Shizhi. "And if anyone wags his tongue again about him preaching in camp, send the fellow to me. I'll tell him what he told was a shepherd boy smashing a big fellow with a stone—that's war!"

He stood up, clapped Yisi on the shoulder, and went.

Old Duan had not said a word all this while.

He still had that twig clenched in his hand. One end of it was burned black and sending up a thin thread of smoke. He held the twig up, looked at the burned end, and put it down again.

"Master Yi."

"Mm."

"Moushi, Duohui—they really could fight," said Old Duan. "Baolu's sword was the one that cut his head off. Cenwenseng's sword was the one the Messiah made him put away."

"Yes."

"That judge's brush, then," said Old Duan. "It really doesn't do pressure points?"

Yisi looked at him.

"I mean," said Old Duan, "he kept accounts half his life. Where the vital points on a man are, he ought to know. Even if he doesn't use them, he ought to know how."

Yisi did not answer.

Old Duan did not wait for him to. He was working it out for himself, muttering: "A tax collector, holding a brush… a tip that fine… if you were going to dab, where would you dab…"

He held the twig out and dabbed the air once. And again. He dabbed very slowly and very seriously, as if looking for something.

Hou Siyuan was still squatting beside him drawing circles. The twig in Old Duan's hand swerved, and the burned end dabbed Hou Siyuan right under the ribs.

Hou Siyuan yelped and leapt up.

Old Duan looked at the twig in his hand, looked at Hou Siyuan, and narrowed one eye.

"Got him," he said.
