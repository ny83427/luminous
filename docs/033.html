Volume Six　The Shuofang Army

Chapter 33　Cold Gruel

In the first month of the second year of Baoying, Shi Chaoyi, at the end of his road, hanged himself in a wood north of Fanyang. His head was cut off, put in a wooden box, and sent west all the way to Chang'an. Counting from the eleventh month of the fourteenth year of Tianbao, when An Lushan raised his troops at Fanyang, the upheaval had gone on more than seven years. When the news reached Chang'an, people in the city at first hardly dared believe it, and for a good many days after, when people in the street spoke of it, they still sounded as if they were talking of someone else's family.

The one who finished this war was Pugu Huaien. He led the Shuofang troops, with the Uighur Khaghan's horsemen, northeast from Luoyang, taking city after city, receiving surrender after surrender; the rebel generals of Hebei one after another opened their gates and came out and knelt by the road holding their seals above their heads, and before spring opened Hebei was pacified. The court added to his offices, added layer upon layer, until in the end he could not remember them all himself. His daughter had married a Uighur prince some years before, and the prince was now Khaghan, and when he met him had to call him father-in-law.

That year Yisi was in Chang'an, and the monastery's business was several times what it had been. Once the order came, the monastery set up its new offices and drew up its new rules, and every day before light Yisi stood in the courtyard hearing one man on rice, another on firewood, a third on how another child had been left at the monastery gate in the night. Jingjing had taken up the office of Deacon of Charity, in charge of the gruel kitchen, the medicine chest, the gathering of the dead and the taking in of children, and Elder Samaan, his account book in his arms, followed behind Jingjing noting everything entry by entry, till Jingjing hardly dared walk fast.

News of Guo Ziyi Yisi heard mostly from Hou Siyuan. Last year the late emperor had died and the new Son of Heaven had taken the throne; there was a great eunuch in the palace, now in power, who disliked Lord Guo and had spoken a few times before the Son of Heaven, and Lord Guo's command of troops had been taken from him; he held a few fine-sounding titles, and for a time had charge of affairs at the late emperor's tomb, and in the third month, when the late emperor was laid in the tomb, he handed over even that post, and now lived idle in his mansion in the Qinren Ward. Hou Siyuan said the mansion was big, but there were few people at its gate; where once a dozen parties might come of a morning, now not one came in a day. Lord Guo himself was the same as ever: in the morning he got up and ate millet gruel with pickles and a steamed bun, and when he had eaten, sat under the gallery looking at the locust tree in the courtyard. Yisi heard it and kept it in his heart, but did not go to the Qinren Ward. When Lord Guo did not call him, he had never gone.

That summer the palace commissioner Luo Fengxian came back from the north. Luo Fengxian was about forty, white-skinned, soft-spoken, with two deep lines at the corners of his eyes when he smiled. That year the Uighurs were going back to the northern desert, and the court sent men to escort them the whole way; he was one of those who escorted them. On the way back he passed through Taiyuan, and through Fenzhou. Xin Yunjing, the commander at Taiyuan, had an old grudge against Pugu Huaien; he kept him a few days and gave him many presents. Fenzhou was where Huaien was encamped, and Huaien too kept him a few days.

What came after was told in Chang'an in scraps and pieces, roughly like this: Huaien gave a banquet for him, and they drank till midnight; Huaien's old mother came out from the rear hall, pointed at Luo Fengxian and scolded him, saying you swore brotherhood with my son, and turn round and get cosy with Taiyuan—whose side are you on? The next day Luo Fengxian wanted to leave; Huaien said the Duanwu festival was only a few days off, stay one more day and we'll drink and make merry together, and had his horse hidden. Luo Fengxian could not find his horse, and panicked; he thought: if they're hiding my horse, they want my life; and in the middle of the night he went over the back wall, borrowed a post-horse, and rode all the way back to Chang'an. The moment he was in Chang'an, he sent up a memorial saying Pugu Huaien was conspiring with the Uighurs, and the signs of revolt were plain.

The memorial had been in only a few days when Huaien's petition arrived too. It was very long; someone in Chang'an had copied it out, and it was going round the wards from hand to hand. Yisi never saw the whole text; he only heard an old literate believer in the monastery read out a few passages. In it Huaien said he was guilty of six crimes: his first crime, when the Tongluo rebelled, he had fought the Tongluo for the late emperor; his second crime, his son had surrendered to the rebels on the field and then escaped back, and he had beheaded his son with his own hand, so the officers and men would know that one step back meant death; his third crime, he had married two daughters far away to the Uighurs, to make this alliance for the country; and there were several more after that, each longer than the last. He said too that forty-six of his brothers, sons and nephews had died on the field. He said too that he dared not come to the capital: a military governor had gone to court last year at an imperial summons, and this first month had been granted death on the road; your servant is not afraid of death; your servant is afraid of dying not knowing why.

When the old believer had read this far, he raised his head and asked Yisi: "Master Yi, this man—is he going to rebel or not?" Yisi said he didn't know. The old believer said: you fought beside him; how can you not know? Yisi said he had fought some years in the same camp as General Pugu, had seen him fight, had not seen into his heart; not knowing was not knowing.

In the seventh month the court changed the era to Guangde and proclaimed a general amnesty. At the beginning of the eighth month someone came from the palace and said the Sage One wished to see him. Yisi did not know at the time why it should be him in particular; only afterwards did he gather vaguely from Official Pei that it was Commissioner Luo who had raised it before the Sage One, saying the hu monk who had guided Huaien that day at the Xiangji Temple was in Chang'an, an old follower under Lord Guo's command; if the Sage One wished to know what kind of man Huaien was, he might call him in and ask. The Sage One had been Prince of Guangping in those days, and that day at the Xiangji Temple had sat with the centre army; he heard it, and allowed it.

Yisi put on the purple robe. It had been granted five years ago, and he had never once had it on his body; the order said to wear it when meeting officials, and only then did he take it out of the chest. The robe was a little big, gaping at the shoulders, and Jingjing took a needle and took it in for him with two seams, crooked ones. As he sewed, Jingjing said: "Pa, if the people in the palace ask you anything, think it through before you speak." Yisi said he was slow of speech to begin with. Jingjing said: slow, and thought through, aren't the same thing. Yisi looked down at him; the boy was seventeen, and half a head taller than he.

The one who led the way was a young palace attendant who walked very fast, the skirts of his robe flicking. Yisi followed behind him, counting as he went: from the palace gate a hundred and twenty paces north to a gate; through the gate eighty paces east, another gate; then north along a long gallery whose pillars were lacquered red; he had counted to the twenty-seventh when the attendant stopped and told him to wait under the gallery. It was an old habit of his: in a strange place, first fix the road in his head—how you came in, how you went out, where the doors were, where the walls, where you could turn. Several officials stood under the gallery too, waiting for audience, in scarlet robes and green; when they saw all that purple on him, and then saw his hu face, they glanced at one another and all moved aside a little. His waist was empty—no knife or mace might be carried into the palace—and his back was empty too, the shield left at the monastery; his two hands hung at his sides, and feeling there was nowhere to put them, he folded them into his sleeves. Only when he had done so did he remember that Lord Guo stood like this too.

The side hall was not large, and very dim inside; the windows were pasted with paper, and the light coming through was yellow. When Yisi went in, Guo Ziyi was already there, in ordinary dress, standing on the left, hands folded in his sleeves, eyes on the floor; Luo Fengxian stood on the right, closer to the Sage One's seat, a smile on his face. The Sage One sat above, a man in his thirties, his face a little long, his cheeks somewhat hollow. Yisi bowed to the ground.

The Sage One looked at him a while and said: I remember you. That day at the Xiangji Temple I was with the centre army; you came back from the east with a flap of skin hanging off your face, tore it off yourself and threw it on the ground, and a man beside me was sick on the spot. That year the Sage One was still Prince of Guangping, Generalissimo of All the Armies of the Realm; Yisi had seen him in the generalissimo's tent, through several ranks of men, and remembered only his robe. Yisi said yes. The Sage One asked again whether the ambush horsemen in the east that day were the ones he had seen first, and whether he had afterwards gone with Pugu Huaien to attack them; Yisi said yes. The Sage One said: "Luo Fengxian says Huaien is rebelling; Huaien says he isn't; Ziyi has just said Huaien isn't. What do you say?"

Yisi glanced to his left: Guo Ziyi was still looking at the floor, quite motionless. He glanced to his right: Luo Fengxian was looking at him too, the two lines at the corners of his eyes smiling deeper. Yisi said: "Your servant is a hu, and slow in the speech of the Hua; I beg Your Majesty to let your servant speak slowly." The Sage One said: speak.

Yisi spoke word by word, enunciating very clearly. He said: "Your servant and General Pugu are not of one road. When your servant first entered the Shuofang army, General Pugu laughed at your servant in the tent, said your servant was a performer of spear-and-staff tricks; and afterwards on the field he hardly spoke to your servant, and your servant doesn't know what he thinks in his heart. That day at the Xiangji Temple, your servant saw the ambush horsemen in the ditch to the east and sent a brother named Wei back first to the centre army to report; Lord Guo sent General Pugu to attack them; the brother named Wei led General Pugu back and died at the sluice gate, and your servant followed behind General Pugu's horse. The fog had not yet quite lifted; those several thousand men to the east were hidden in the ditch, and General Pugu led his horsemen straight in, and let not one get away. Your servant still doesn't know what he thinks in his heart. Your servant knows only that if he had meant to rebel that day, there was no need to charge into the ditch to the east; he had only to turn his horse's head west, and the rear of the centre army would have been bare."

Yisi should have stopped there, but he thought a moment, glanced to his right again, and said slowly: "Commissioner Luo stayed a few nights in General Pugu's camp, and saw that he meant to rebel. Your servant fought some years at General Pugu's side, and can't see it." He thought a moment more before he brought out the next sentence: "Either Commissioner Luo's eyes are good, or your servant's eyes are bad." And having said it, as if afraid he might not be understood, he added honestly: "Your servant's eye has had an arrow in it." In a corner of the hall a young attendant could not hold back a snort of laughter and hastily covered his mouth. The smile on Luo Fengxian's face did not change in the least. Yisi went on: "Lord Guo sees it that way too. Lord Guo judges men more truly than your servant." When this was said, Guo Ziyi on the left moved, a very small movement: his hands folded in his sleeves, the fingers stirred. Yisi did not see it.

Luo Fengxian stepped half a pace forward, bowed toward the throne, and said softly: "Your Majesty, this old slave makes bold to ask the Great Virtue Yi one question." The Sage One raised his hand. Luo Fengxian turned round, and smiling at Yisi asked: this acting Director of the Palace Administration the Great Virtue Yi holds—who petitioned for it? Yisi said Lord Guo. Luo Fengxian asked again: and this purple robe? Yisi said Lord Guo as well. Luo Fengxian nodded, bowed again, and said toward the throne: "The Great Virtue Yi is an old follower under Lord Guo's command; his office was petitioned for by Lord Guo; his robe was requested by Lord Guo. The Great Virtue Yi says Lord Guo judges men truly; Lord Guo has just said Huaien is not rebelling; and Huaien is a general Lord Guo brought up under his own hand. This old slave is dull of wit; I only listen, and hear that in the Shuofang army, it seems, they are all one family." When he had said it he went back to his place, hands hanging at his sides, and spoke no more.

The hall fell silent. The Sage One looked neither at Yisi nor at Luo Fengxian; he looked at Guo Ziyi, and Guo Ziyi's eyes were lowered. The Sage One looked a while, and said: withdraw, all of you.

Out of the hall, the sun was fierce. Guo Ziyi walked ahead, Yisi behind, all the way out of the palace gate, where Guo Ziyi's attendants led up his horse. Before Guo Ziyi mounted, in front of the people at the palace gate, he turned and said one thing to Yisi: "Great Virtue, go back to your monastery. It's hot." Having said it, he mounted and rode off. Yisi stood a while in the sun, the purple robe on his body, his back soaked in sweat.

That afternoon there was talk in Chang'an. Where it came from no one knew, but by evening the old man who sold vegetables at the monastery gate was already telling people: Lord Guo had sent a hu monk to speak for Pugu Huaien before the Sage One, and the hu monk spoke too hastily and talked Lord Guo into it as well. When he had told it, he lowered his voice and said: "You know who the hu monk was? One from this very monastery of ours." The listeners all turned their heads and looked in through the monastery gate.

On the evening of the audience, Yisi went to the Qinren Ward. He went of his own accord; no one had called him. The men at the gate knew him and went in to announce him, and had him wait outside the front hall; he waited a good half hour before an old servant came out to lead him in.

Guo Ziyi was at his evening meal in the side hall: a small low table, a bowl of millet gruel, a dish of pickles, two steamed buns, the lamp at the corner of the table lighting half his face. On a small stand beside the table stood that bronze basin with the dented rim, holding clear water; Guo Ziyi had washed his hands in it before the meal, and drops of water still hung on its rim. Yisi saw it at once, and his heart eased.

He knelt before the table and said this humble monk had dragged Lord Guo into trouble. Before he had finished, Guo Ziyi had already told the old servant to help him up, had a sitting-couch brought and told him to sit, and had another bowl of gruel brought for the Great Virtue Yi. Yisi declined; Guo Ziyi said it was late—once the ward gates shut, you won't get anything to eat back there; eat, then go. The gruel was brought; Yisi held the bowl and could not swallow it.

Guo Ziyi broke off half a steamed bun, dipped it in a little pickle juice, and chewed it slowly, and when he had chewed it, asked him: "Of what the Great Virtue Yi said today, was one sentence false?" Yisi said no. Guo Ziyi said he knew there wasn't, broke off another piece of bun, and said as he broke it: "You took three arrows at the Xiangji Temple; I remember. On the battlefield, to see a road clearly for me is to save me; in the court, to say a word for me is sometimes to harm me." He said it very evenly, much as he might have said it would be overcast tomorrow; and having said it, he spoke of Pugu Huaien: how Huaien was now at Fenzhou, with troops in his hands and an old mother at home, and people in Chang'an speaking against him; he would not come, and the court sent man after man to persuade him. Here he stopped, bent his head, and took a mouthful of gruel.

He asked too about the monastery, asked how long the rice would last; Yisi said till autumn. He asked whether his son was back and in good health; Yisi said he was well, only thinner. Guo Ziyi said: that's good; and said too that the Great Virtue was a man of religious practice, with much to do at his monastery; palace business ought never to have troubled the Great Virtue. Yisi heard it and thought Lord Guo spoke reasonably, and yet thought too that, coming from Lord Guo's mouth, the words sounded somehow strange. Before he had thought it through, Guo Ziyi had already told the old servant to bring a lantern and see the Great Virtue Yi out, and told him too to take the mansion's card, so that if they met the Jinwu Guard on night patrol on the way, he would have something to show.

Yisi got to the door and looked back once. Guo Ziyi had taken up his own bowl of gruel again. Through all this talk that gruel had long gone cold, a thin skin formed on its surface; he pushed the skin aside with his chopsticks, bent his head, and mouthful by mouthful finished the gruel.

When Yisi came out of the Qinren Ward it was already dark. The old servant carried the lantern all the way to the ward gate, said a few words to the gate guards, put the card in his hand, and only then went back. He led his horse slowly west; the streets were empty, only the hooves of the Jinwu Guard sounding far off. He remembered the twelfth month of the fourteenth year of Tianbao on the bank of the Yellow River, when Lord Guo first saw him and asked his name, and he said the Tang call me Yisi; remembered how in the city of Lingzhou Lord Guo had sent word for him to come after he had eaten, any time before the second watch; remembered the first words that mattered Lord Guo ever said to him: "I don't want you to kill men. I want you to see the road." Today Lord Guo had treated him courteously, had more gruel brought for him, had thought of his road home for him; he could not say what was wrong.

Back at the monastery, Jingjing was still waiting for him under the lamp, and asked what Lord Guo had said. Yisi thought, and said Lord Guo had asked how long the monastery's rice would last. He took off the purple robe, folded it, wrapped it in the blue cloth, and put it back in the chest.

A few days later the court sent a chief minister to Fenzhou to soothe Huaien and urge him to come to court. Earlier someone at court had suggested that since Huaien was Lord Guo's old subordinate, Lord Guo going would be better than anyone; the suggestion was made once, and afterwards no one made it again. Yisi heard this from Official Pei. When Official Pei had told it, he sighed first himself, then lowered his voice and said: things weren't going too well over in Longyou either, by all accounts; dispatch after dispatch from the frontier was sent to the capital, and once they reached the palace, nothing more was heard of them.
