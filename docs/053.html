Volume Nine　The Translation Hall

Chapter 53　Official Pei

One day in the tenth month of the third year of Zhenyuan, nothing was written on Jingjing's board. That morning the line he turned to was As for man, his days are as grass: as a flower of the field, so he flourisheth. For the wind passeth over it, and it is gone. He had picked up the charcoal and not yet set it down when the old servant of the Pei house in the Guangde Ward came knocking at the monastery gate: the master asked Master Jing to come, and the lady Shiyiniang had already gone ahead. Jingjing laid down the charcoal and woke Lingbao, and the two of them followed the old servant out of the Yining Ward.

The Pei house stood in the north-west corner of the Guangde Ward, with two scholar trees at the gate. Official Pei had been thirty years in the Metropolitan Prefecture, rising from outside the ranks to Administrator, and after he retired he grew flowers at home. The chrysanthemums in the courtyard were in bloom, yellow and white, pot after pot set along the gallery, but no one had had a thought for watering them, and the leaves had curled. Inside it was dim, the brazier burning hot. Official Pei lay in bed, worn to a handful of bones, his high brow ridge higher than ever. Shiyiniang sat by the bed holding a bowl of medicine that had long gone cold; she had been holding it all along. Lingbao called Official Pei Great-uncle; as he came in he first knelt before the bed and knocked his head to the floor.

When Official Pei saw Jingjing he sent his son and daughter out, keeping only Shiyiniang, and had Jingjing sit closer. His voice was very low; after each sentence he had to rest for a couple of breaths, and Jingjing bent down with his ear to his lips.

"Your Reverence, I have something for you. Shiyiniang, go and get it from the chest at the head of the bed."

Shiyiniang opened the chest. Lying on top was a small book made of a few dozen sheets of hemp paper folded and pasted together, the paper rubbed soft, the corners frayed.

"This is what I've kept these thirty years," said Official Pei. "In the Metropolitan Prefecture, in the Bureau of Sacrifices, in the Court of State Ceremonial, in the Censorate, in the two counties of Wannian and Chang'an: who handles what, who can be asked a favour and who can't, who needs a bolt of silk first before he'll open his mouth and who'll be offended if you send one, who bears grudges and who remembers kindness—it's all in there. I kept it for my own use at first. The year Master Yi came to the city I ran about the Bureau of Sacrifices for him, a few times, and felt I'd better write it down; and once I'd begun I kept it thirty years. Half the people in it are dead, half aren't in their old offices any more; the rest can still be of use."

Jingjing opened it. Page after page was in Official Pei's upright regular hand: names, offices, wards, with small notes beneath: "Quick-tempered; go early." "Likes chess." "His wife a Buddhist; don't speak of the monastery." "This man can be asked; not asked too much." Some names were circled in red, some ticked in black ink; those ticked were dead.

"That year the Patriarchate's instructions said dealings with the government were to go through one line only, Master Yi and the official surnamed Pei." Official Pei rested a while and went on. "The first time I heard that read out, I was glad and afraid both. Glad that the Patriarchate thought so well of me; afraid because one line is one thread, and if the thread breaks there's nothing left. When Master Yi went, the thread was down to half; now I'm going too, and the half is gone as well. Your Reverence, from now on you mustn't have only one thread; you must have ten. If three of the ten break, there are still seven."

"I'll remember," said Jingjing.

"And another thing. The people in the book—don't go looking for all of them. Look for too many and people will think you're up to something. When you look, look for one at a time. And when you find him, speak of his affairs first, and the monastery's affairs after."

When he had said this he closed his eyes to rest. Jingjing thought he had fallen asleep and was about to rise when he opened his eyes again. "There's an old matter I want to tell you about too. In the spring of the first year of Qianyuan the Metropolitan Prefecture was to give out rice west of the Qujiang Pool, and I'd been fretting for days for fear people would be crushed to death. That morning Master Yi came to see me in the side hall of the prefecture, and said, over and over, only one thing: have the people sit down first, then give out the rice. I thought at the time, this hu monk has something to him. That day I stood in the waste garden from the hour of mao to the hour of shen, watching six thousand-odd people sitting on the grass ward by ward, not one pushing, not one claiming twice, and by the end my legs had gone and I sat down on the grass myself. As I sat down I remembered Master Yi saying that the line in that book, the first line, had come from a question his son asked." He looked at Jingjing. "That year you asked your Pa why the people should sit down first. These thirty years I've run about the government offices for you people, half for your Pa and half for that child who asked. I never said this to Master Yi. If he heard it, he'd probably find me long-winded again."

Jingjing held his hand and asked whether there was anything he wanted the monastery to do for him.

Official Pei thought, and said, "I've been washed. All my life I've believed in your Lord, and I don't regret it. But this family of mine—my wife believes in the Buddha, my son read the Confucian books and sat the classics examination, my daughter married into a Buddhist family. When I die the family will ask the Buddhist monks to do the forty-nine days, and get someone to choose the grave site. Let them do all that. Your Reverence, don't quarrel with them over it. My grave will be on the Shaoling plateau, in the Pei family burial ground. If Your Reverence is willing, when the monks have gone, read a passage for me—the passage Elder Sabrisho read by the well that year when he baptized me—and when you've read it, leave. Don't make things hard for the family. The epitaph probably won't mention you people by a single word; don't go asking them to add one."

"I understand," said Jingjing. He took a cloth bundle from his breast and untied it. It was the monastery's glass bowl, the one used for the offering once in seven days, its circular facets ground by Sasanian hands, shining ring upon ring in the light of the charcoal; and in the bundle there was also a small piece of the offering bread, which he had broken off in the hall before he left. "Sir, today is the day of the offering once in seven. I've brought the bread from the hall."

Shiyiniang rose and barred the door, and stuffed the cracks in the window tight. Lingbao knelt by the bed and held up Official Pei's head. Jingjing read in Syriac, read a sentence and then said it in Tang, and when he came to This is my body he broke the little piece of bread smaller still and laid it at Official Pei's lips. Official Pei could not open his mouth wide. The bread rested on his lips; he worked it with his tongue and got a tiny bit down, and the rest fell on the pillow, and Shiyiniang picked it up with her fingers and put it in her own mouth. Then Jingjing brought the glass bowl to his lips. There was only a mouthful of wine in it; Official Pei wet his lips and did not swallow. Jingjing laid his hand on his forehead. The forehead was cold. He said Lord, have mercy, and at the third time Official Pei, eyes open, looking at him, moved his lips: "Good. Good."

Official Pei died in the night of the third of the eleventh month that year, at seventy. The day Jingjing came back from the Guangde Ward he took up the charcoal and on the board that had stood empty all day wrote the character cao, grass. He did not wipe it off, but left it there until Official Pei was buried.

The Pei funeral was done handsomely. The spirit seat was set in the main hall, and in the front courtyard a dozen-odd monks from the Jianfu Monastery chanted Sanskrit hymns from morning till night. The day Jingjing went with Samaan to condole happened to be the first seventh-day fast. The Pei son came out to meet them in the coarsest mourning, his face his father's face but the brow ridge not so high. He bowed and said, "My father gave instructions before he died that when Master Jing came he was to be received with all courtesy. The fast is being held in the hall; please sit a while in the side room, Your Reverence."

Jingjing said there was no need to go in; they would sit in the side room a while and go. Samaan had his hands tucked into his sleeves and his face was not pleasant to look at. Jingjing glanced at him once, and he lowered his head.

Lingbao did not sit with them in the side room. He was a nephew of the Pei family, and in mourning clothes he knelt before the spirit seat in the row of the Pei kin. When the monks chanted a passage the kin bowed once, and he bowed too. Jingjing, looking through the lattice of the side-room window, saw Lingbao rise with his Pei cousins, kneel with them, and when the chime sounded, touch his forehead to the floor like everyone else. Jingjing took his eyes away and picked up the bowl of water before him.

When they had sat more than an hour, Shiyiniang came out from the back, took the bowl of cold water from his hand, and handed him a bowl of hot. "You can't drink cold."

Jingjing asked whether she was tired.

"Not tired," said Shiyiniang. "I'm kneeling in there, and in my heart I'm saying our scriptures. The monks say theirs and I say mine, and neither gets in the other's way. All my life I've been used to standing between several families—the Chengs, the Peis, the Yis—that's how I've always stood." When she had said it she went back to kneel.

Lady Pei came out once too, past sixty, her hair all white, her eyes swollen with weeping. She came up to Jingjing and said, "The old man believed in yours. I know it. All these years, every offering once in seven, he got up before light and said he was going to the monastery, and I never once stopped him. That Lord of yours I don't understand. I only know that when he came back from your monastery his face was always good." As she spoke she began to weep again, and Shiyiniang came out from inside and helped her back in.

The Pei son asked Lü Xiuyan to write the epitaph out for the stone. The text was composed by a Presented Scholar. It said that Master Pei came from Hedong, passed the classics examination, served as Administrator of the Metropolitan Prefecture, married a lady of such a family, had so many sons—and mentioned the monastery not by a single word. Lü Xiuyan added four characters in one place only. The draft said that in his late years Master Pei "lived at leisure, taking his pleasure in flowers and trees"; in writing it out, Lü Xiuyan added after it in his late years he loved to give. The Pei son looked at it and said, very well, and let it stand. When Lü Xiuyan came to the monastery and spoke of it, he said, "Those four characters are written for those who know. Those who don't will take them for a kind phrase."

He was buried in the twelfth month. When the Pei family and the monks had gone, Jingjing came up with Lingbao and Samaan from the village below the plateau, knelt before the new grave, and, as Official Pei had asked, read the passage Sabrisho had read by the well that year, once in Syriac and once in Tang. He had in his breast a small wooden tablet with a cross written on it, which he had meant to set at the foot of the two young cypresses before the grave. His hand went into his breast, and then he remembered Official Pei's don't make things hard for the family, and did not take it out.

The next spring, the fourth year of Zhenyuan, a dispatch came into the city saying that the registers of Buddhist monks and nuns in the capital would from now on be under the newly established Commissioners of Merit of the Left and Right Streets, the commissioners to be palace eunuchs holding the post concurrently. Samaan read the dispatch twice and raised his head. "The dispatch says only Buddhist monks and nuns; it doesn't mention us. But the people in the city call us monks too."

In the third month two men came from the Commissioners of Merit's office: an inspector, very young, in a blue robe, and a minor clerk with a register under his arm. The inspector said that by the Commissioner's order every monastery in the capital with monks in it was to be examined and registered afresh: how many people, how many rooms, how many mu of land, how many scriptures, how many images, each to be reported clearly. He spread a blank form on the table and told the monastery to fill it in itself.

Jingjing took the brush himself and filled it in item by item. In the column for images—there were no images in the hall, only the cross—he wrote none, and beside it a line in small characters: in the hall, one cross venerated. When he came to the end, the form had a column called "other," and he stopped. The stele in the front courtyard: was it monastery property, a vessel, or something else? He held the brush and thought a long while, and in the "other" column wrote one line: in the front courtyard, one stele, erected first month of the second year of Jianzhong, approved by the Bureau of Sacrifices.

When the inspector had watched him finish, he went to look at the hall, the scripture room, and the stele in the front courtyard, came back to the side room, sat, drank a mouthful of tea, and said with a smile, "In this business of registration every monastery shows a little consideration—money for paper and brushes, money for the legwork. As much as you like. The monastery can see to it as it thinks fit."

Samaan, standing behind Jingjing, said softly in Syriac, "When Official Pei was alive, he stepped in on this kind of thing. It's in the book too: some people get a bolt of silk first."

Jingjing had him bring the ledger, opened it to this year's page, and pointed it out to him: of the monastery's common funds, after the stele money had been taken from them, the column for repairs to the hall was empty, and the column for clothing allowances was empty too; only the column for gruel rice was left. "If this bolt of silk is to be sent, it can only come out of this column." Samaan looked at the column and closed the ledger.

Jingjing turned to the inspector and said, "For the paper and brushes of the registration, whatever the monastery ought to pay, it will pay. How much—please write down a figure, Inspector, and the monastery will deliver that amount to the office, and Elder Samaan will enter it in the ledger. Consideration, the monastery cannot provide."

The smile on the inspector's face did not go away. "Master Jing is a sensible man. The stele the monastery has put up is a fine one; everyone in Chang'an has been to see it. It says six reigns of Sons of Heaven have protected you. With the Son of Heaven protecting you, naturally you don't care about a few coins." He rose and left, and did not leave the register either.

Samaan watched him go and said, "That man has made a note of it."

"I know," said Jingjing.

Half a month later the Metropolitan Prefecture passed on a dispatch quoting a report from the Commissioners of Merit, saying that the West Market was a place where merchants and travellers converged; a hu temple setting up pots in the market to give out gruel gathered crowds in the market and might lead to disorder; it should be stopped at once. Jingjing took the dispatch to the gruel shed in the West Market. The fire under the big pot was not yet out, and the queue stretched half a street—the old, the sick, those carrying children. There was a lame old man on a stick whom he knew: he had drawn rice west of the Qujiang Pool that year.

Jingjing had the fire put out, and himself stood where the pot had been and told people one by one: "Old fellow, it won't be here tomorrow. From now on come to the monastery gate in the Yining Ward. There's little room at the gate; we can only cook one pot a day, the old and sick and those with children first."

The lame old man stood leaning on his stick a long while and said, "Your Reverence, I've drawn gruel from the first year of Qianyuan till today. Thirty years."

"I know," said Jingjing. "Come a little early tomorrow."

That evening the gruel shed in the West Market was taken down. When the queue had half dispersed, someone swore—swore at the hu monks, at whom exactly no one could tell. That night Samaan wrote a line in the ledger: Came today, so many; did not receive, so many. He had kept accounts all his life, and the words did not receive he wrote for the first time.

That same month Jingjing went looking for people. As Official Pei had said, one at a time, beginning with the book. The clerk in the Bureau of Sacrifices noted "this man can be asked" had been transferred to the Eastern Capital the year before last. The usher of the Court of State Ceremonial who "likes chess" he sent his card to by someone; he waited three days, and the answer came back that he was ill and saw no visitors. The recorder in the Metropolitan Prefecture noted "remembers old ties; Master Yi once treated his mother's illness" did see him, and talked with him half an hour in a little room by the gate. He said he remembered Master Yi, remembered that leg of his mother's; but the dispatch rested on a report from the Commissioners of Merit, the prefecture only passed it on, and he, a recorder, had no say. He saw him to the door and added, "If Your Reverence has business later, come and sit again." Jingjing went back to the monastery and beside the three names wrote a line each: transferred to the Eastern Capital; ill, sees no one; remembers old ties, cannot speak. Three threads: two broken, and the one left was slack.

Those mornings he found he was not as he had been. Standing in the middle of the courtyard telling his knots, as he told them what he was thinking of was the line in Samaan's ledger, did not receive, so many; opening the psalms, his eyes were on the scripture and his mind still on that line of figures. Before, it had been scripture first and affairs after; these days the affairs came in first and the scripture was crowded in behind. One morning he simply closed the book and sat, reading nothing, waiting for that line of figures to withdraw of itself. He waited until the window paper turned white; it withdrew, and only then did he open the book again. He turned to the passage where the Lord sends out the disciples: Behold, I send you forth as sheep in the midst of wolves: be ye therefore wise as serpents, and harmless as doves. On the board he wrote the character she, serpent, and beside it the character ge, dove, the two characters crowded together on one board.

Then he went to find Li Su. Li Su was at the Astronomical Bureau, which had nothing to do with monks and nuns. When he had heard Jingjing out he reckoned on his fingers once and said, "The Commissioner of Merit is a palace eunuch holding the post concurrently, and eunuchs' affairs I have no say in. But among the Commissioner's assistant officials there's one surnamed Yang who likes astrology and often comes to the Astronomical Bureau to ask about the auspicious and inauspicious. I'll pass a word on for Your Reverence. Whether it gets through I daren't say."

He went to find Mi Jifen. Mi Jifen was a general in the Shence Army, very old by now. When he had heard it out he said, "That eunuch is above us in the Shence Army, and on the affairs of those above, a subordinate can't well open his mouth. But I know a lot of people in the camp; I'll ask whose man that inspector is." A month later Mi Jifen sent his son Siyuan with word: the inspector was the nephew of an officer in the Shence Army, and that officer came from the same district as Luo Haoxin.

Jingjing went to find Luo Haoxin. Luo Haoxin slapped the table as soon as he heard. "I know that brat! Relies on his uncle being in service with the Commissioner, and goes about angling for consideration everywhere. I'll talk to him." He paused, then said, "Your Reverence, that year I got you a sheet of abuse. This time, count it as my paying you back once."

Each time Jingjing found someone, he came back and added a page at the back of Official Pei's book, writing the man's name, which office he was in, what had been asked of him, and how he had answered. His hand and Official Pei's hand lay together: Official Pei's upright, every stroke standing; his slanting to the right—Pa had said, like a man hurrying on along a road.

Early in the spring of the fifth year of Zhenyuan, Luo Haoxin brought word: the Commissioner of Merit's office had given way. The gruel shed could open—not in the West Market, but in the Yining Ward's ward street, put up beside the monastery gate; it could have two pots; people from the ward could draw, and people from other wards could draw too, but only once the ward headman had checked them.

Samaan said, "That's half."

"Half is still open," said Jingjing. To Luo Haoxin he said, "The inspector's consideration, the monastery didn't give, and won't give hereafter. But the General spoke for the monastery, and the monastery must thank him. The way to thank him: this winter, men of the Shence Army with frostbite are to be sent to the monastery, and the monastery will treat their injuries without charge."

Luo Haoxin said, "That's good! The men in the camp fear frostbite more than anything. Lose a finger to it and you can't draw a bow."

The day the gruel shed went up in the ward street, the ward headman stood by the pot holding the ward's household register. Whoever came, he asked which ward; those of this ward got some; for those of other wards he turned the pages, and if he couldn't find them, shook his head. On the first day his head-shaking sent away a dozen-odd people. One old woman from another ward, carrying a child, stood by the pot and would not leave, and the ward headman said no. Jingjing went over and poured his own bowl of gruel out for the child. Samaan crouched down and asked the child's name. The child said A-Nu, six, come with Granny from the Guangde Ward.

Jingjing straightened up and said to the ward headman, "From tomorrow, my own bowl every day goes to the people from other wards. Whether you check them or not, Headman, is no concern of mine."

The ward headman looked at him a while and did not stop him. Every day after that Jingjing's bowl was poured out for people from other wards. Lingbao saw it, and the next day poured out his own as well. Samaan made a line in the ledger: From another ward: A-Nu, grandson of a certain old woman of the Guangde Ward, six; given from the monks' own portion. When he had written it, he poured out his own bowl too.

That winter the Shence Army sent a dozen-odd frostbitten soldiers, all young men, the chilblains on their hands and feet broken open and running yellow. Jingjing had Lingbao go to the son of Shopkeeper Wang in the West Market to make up medicine, and boiled a chilblain salve, and put it on them one by one. After half a month nine were healed, three had lost toes, and one had lost two fingers. The one who had lost the fingers held out his hand to show Jingjing. "Master, this hand of mine—will it still draw a bow?"

Jingjing thought of Xu Jing. Xu Jing had lost two fingers of his right hand to the cold keeping a beacon tower, and said he'd given the fingers to the beacon fire, and afterward loaded a crossbow just the same. "It will," he said. "I know a man who lost two, and loaded a crossbow all his life just the same. He still lives at the north end of the Yining Ward. If you don't believe me, I'll take you to see him."

In the twelfth month, on the day of Official Pei's second-year rite, Jingjing did not go up the Shaoling plateau again. That morning he wiped the character off his board, opened the roll of Ecclesiastes, turned to And if one prevail against him, two shall withstand him; and a threefold cord is not quickly broken, and on the board wrote the character xian, thread. When he had written it he went into the scripture room, opened the book, turned to the last page, added the inspector's name, and after it a line in small characters: consideration not given; through General Luo's intercession, gruel shed moved to ward street, two pots, those of other wards given from the monks' own portion.

Lingbao, watching beside him, asked, "Brother, this book—will it still be kept on, after this?"

"It will," said Jingjing. "I'll keep it until I can't write any more, and then you keep it on."
