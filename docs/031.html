Volume Six　The Shuofang Army

Chapter 31　Shapur

Mornings at the Patriarchate began with the striking of a board. Before light, someone in the middle of the courtyard took a wooden mallet and struck a long board hung up there—tok, tok, tok—slow first, then fast, then slow again, and when it was done the people of every room got up and went into the church to say the morning praises. Jingjing lived most of a year at the Patriarchate, and not once did he rise earlier than his uncle. Every time he pushed open his door and came out, his uncle was already standing in the middle of the courtyard—from which room he had come, there was no telling—head bowed, tying the knot in his empty right sleeve with his left hand and his teeth: first rolling the sleeve up to the elbow, biting one end, passing his left hand round, a pull, a cinch, another pull—no more than a few breaths, all told—and when it was done, pressing the knot flat with his left hand, raising his head and going to the church. The knot was the same every day, just as tight, just as neat, as if measured with a rule. Once Jingjing went over and said: Uncle, let me tie it for you. His uncle looked at him and said: "I'll tie it myself. I've tied it nine years; if someone else tied it, the whole day this sleeve wouldn't feel right."

They had arrived in the evening, and his uncle, having seen Jingjing and asked one question about the road, had them rest. The next day his uncle first heard Elder Marcus tell of Chang'an. Elder Marcus talked most of the day, and very lively it was, from the old monastery in the Liquan Ward to the charcoal kilns of the Southern Mountains, from the rice carts at the Qujiang Pool to those few buckets of water at the gate of the fire-temple, and at the important places he even stood up and acted it out. His uncle listened throughout, and when he had listened, asked how many baptized Tang the monastery had now; Elder Marcus said a hundred or so, more or less. He asked how many shi of gruel they gave out a year; Elder Marcus said a few hundred, more or less. When he asked a third number and the answer was again more or less, his uncle asked no more, but only said the elder had had a hard journey and should rest first.

From the third day on, his uncle called only for Jingjing. Jingjing brought the register Babai had copied, the monastery's accounts, and his own two rolls of diary, and whenever his uncle asked for a number he unrolled the scrolls to find it. Elder Marcus took no offence; early on the fourth morning he went alone to the burial ground behind the Patriarchate, found his teacher's grave, and said scripture before it. After that he went every day, and sat by the grave half a day at a time, and came back and ate and went to sit there again, and grew sparer and sparer of words with everyone.

Jingjing delivered his Pa's message too, said his Pa had told him to tell his uncle he was well. His uncle asked whether that arrow in his waist still hurt on overcast days; Jingjing said it did; you could hear it when he turned over at night. His uncle said: then he's well. And asked no more.

In those months his uncle called Jingjing into his small room every few days to question him. He asked about Chang'an, about the monastery's people, about the seven elders, about the guard brothers, about the gruel, about the leather-worker's son, about the old keeper of the fire. He asked in extreme detail, asking about one thing over and over, until sometimes Jingjing was confused himself; but his uncle was never confused, and if Jingjing got a number wrong, the next time it came up his uncle would say: last time you said thirty-seven. When it came to the old fire-keeper, his uncle asked what had become of that basin of fire afterwards; Jingjing said it had not gone out; his uncle nodded, and made a note on the paper.

The matters his Pa wanted told face to face, his uncle asked about one by one as well. What he asked about in most detail was the night the elders debated Baaluo: who spoke first, what Sabrisho said, what Narsai said; when Haddad said that cutting a man off had to be decided by a bishop, where everyone looked; where Elder Yonan sat, what he said. Jingjing said Elder Yonan said nothing at all, and kept his head bowed from beginning to end. When his uncle heard this, he stopped his pen, and only after a while went on writing. Of the purple robe, he asked whether Yisi had worn it; Jingjing said not once; on the day he went to be questioned at the Bureau of Sacrifices, he went with it wrapped in blue cloth under his arm. Of the Tang hymns, he had Jingjing sing a passage for him; Jingjing, red in the face, sang the few lines Granny He and the others had sung for decades, and when he had sung them his uncle had him translate them line by line into Syriac, and when they were translated his uncle said neither that they were good nor that they were not. Of the portraits of the five sage emperors, Jingjing said the ones General Gao had sent back then had been lost when Chang'an fell; Official Pei had asked whether they meant to ask for them again, and said he was willing to find out the way to go about it for the monastery. His uncle asked him whose portraits they would be asking for. Jingjing counted them off one by one, and his uncle wrote them down one by one.

When he had done asking, his uncle wrote with his left hand. He wrote slowly with his left hand, the characters leaning to the left. What he was writing was for Chang'an, to be put before the Patriarch, sealed, and carried back; what it said Jingjing did not know, only that he wrote for several months, wrote and tore up and wrote again, and once, writing till midnight, the lamp oil burned dry, and he sat in the dark waiting for dawn.

That summer everyone at the Patriarchate was talking of one thing: the Caliph was going to build a new city upstream. The site chosen was on the west bank of the Tigris, beside a village called Baghdad, most of a day's journey from Ctesiphon. The city, they heard, was round, walls within walls, and in its very centre the Caliph's palace; craftsmen and labourers had been levied from everywhere, tens of thousands of them. Some said too that when the Caliph went to look at the site he had come upon a monastery there, and the old monk of the monastery had told him some of the virtues of the place—not hot in summer, few mosquitoes, waterways open in all directions—and the Caliph, hearing it, had settled on it. Whether this was true no one knew, but when the people of the Patriarchate told it there was a touch of pride on their faces, as though the place had been picked for the Caliph by one of their own.

Before long another thing was going round the city: that the Caliph found brick-burning too slow, and meant to pull down the old palace at Ctesiphon and build the new city with its bricks, and had already sent men to measure it. Others said that a corner had been pulled down, but the walls were too solid; the wages to pull out an old brick and carry it away cost more than burning a new one, and so they were not pulling it down after all. Whether it had been pulled down, how much of it, everyone told it differently. When Jingjing went out to buy flatbread he could see that great arch still standing far off, and could not see that anything was missing. When his uncle heard people speak of it, he said only: that was the Persian kings' palace; it stood several hundred years, and people talk of pulling it down as if it were nothing.

Jingjing asked his uncle whether the Patriarch too would move to the new city. His uncle said: once the new city was built, sooner or later the Patriarch would move there; wherever the Caliph was, those who had business to ask of the Caliph had to be; what the Patriarchate would be like then, and who would have the Patriarch's ear, no one could say now. When he said it his face looked as it always did, and nothing could be read in it.

How his uncle had lost that arm, Jingjing had heard the first time he came to the Patriarchate: that year there had been a bitter struggle at the Patriarchate over the patriarchal throne; his uncle had stood on the side of Patriarch Jacob, and the Surin faction had lost; but the losers had not dispersed; they hired an assassin who crept into his uncle's room at night; his uncle blocked a knife with his right arm and killed the assassin with his left, but the right arm could not be saved. Only now, living there longer, did Jingjing slowly learn that from then on his uncle had not had one peaceful day. He never again slept two nights running in the same room; every evening he decided himself which room he would sleep in that night, and when he had decided, told only the men keeping the gate that night, so that if something urgent came up in the night they would know where to call him; no one else, not even the Patriarch, knew. The Patriarchate was poor too; they ate flatbread and beans, wore old robes, and the ration money of the brothers who kept the gate grew less year by year. And now the new city was to be built, and the Patriarch was to move, and the Surin people, one by one, were edging up to the Caliph's officials again.

Jingjing had asked the oldest elder of the Patriarchate whether his uncle was afraid. The old elder thought, and said: "Whether he's afraid—ask him, and he won't tell you. All I know is that every night before he lies down he writes the next day's business on a wooden board and puts it under his pillow. If something happens in the night, the next day whoever picks up that board will know what to carry on with."

Among the brothers who kept the gate of the Patriarchate there was one named Baluma.

The day Jingjing and the others arrived, it was he who stopped them at the gate. He was about thirty, broad-faced, big-eyed, loud-voiced, with a mouthful of white teeth when he laughed; he came from a village in the marshes to the south, had come into the Patriarchate in his teens, and from the year his uncle lost the arm had kept the gate with him. He treated Jingjing very well. In Jingjing's first month there his stomach was very bad, and every day Baluma got hot water for him from the kitchen, and when he brought it winked at him and said the little deacon from the east had guts made of silk. He was always pestering Jingjing about the east: whether Chang'an really had over a hundred wards, what colour clothes the Tang emperor wore, how much a gatekeeper at the Chang'an monastery got a month.

Jingjing told him that the guard brothers at the Chang'an monastery ordinarily got no money to speak of; they ate and lived in the monastery, and in the years of the gruel, whatever the gruel kitchen ate, they ate. When Baluma heard it, he scraped the old shoes on his feet against the ground a couple of times, and said: "All these years I've followed the Archdeacon, and the Archdeacon is a good man; I won't say he's not." The soles of those shoes were worn through, and had been patched with leather, and the patches had worn through into holes again. He said again: "Nine years, and I've never worn a new pair of shoes."

His mother was sick in the village; several times he had sent money home by someone, all of it borrowed. The family that sold flatbread in the city—he owed them most of a year's bread money; once when Jingjing went with him to buy bread the bread-seller berated him right in front of Jingjing, and he took no offence, but laughed and said: next month for sure, next month for sure. Out of the bread shop he said to Jingjing: little deacon, when you go back east, take me with you. Jingjing thought he was joking, and laughed too.

Early in the eighth month that year, his uncle gave Jingjing a paper folded into a small square and said: this is for your Pa. It isn't sealed. Take it with you when you go back; you're not to open it on the way. Jingjing put it away and kept it next to his body, together with his grandfather's wooden cross.

A few days later, one evening, Jingjing was sitting on the well platform at the far end of the courtyard writing his diary. He liked to write here; it was light, and the wind came off the river and it was not hot. When he looked up, he saw his uncle come out from inside, go to the courtyard gate, say something to the men keeping the gate that night, and, having said it, go back in. Two were keeping the gate that day: one was Baluma, the other an old brother who did not hear well, who had kept the gate all his life and never been one step away from it. What his uncle said Jingjing was too far off to hear. After a while Baluma said a few words to the old brother and went out of the gate carrying an empty oil jar, and as he passed the well platform he winked at Jingjing and said the lamp oil was finished; he was going into the city to buy some. Jingjing nodded and bent to his writing again. It was full dark before Baluma came back.

In the middle of the night Jingjing was woken by a dull thud, like something heavy falling on the ground, and then someone cried out, in the country speech of Balkh. In the whole Patriarchate only two people spoke the country speech of Balkh. Jingjing ran out barefoot, followed the cry past the church, past the kitchen, all the way to the door of the room beside the storehouse. The door was open; the oil lamp inside had been knocked over and the oil spilled all over the floor, little flames licking along the ground, and by their light he saw his uncle lying on the floor, a wound under his right ribs, blood welling out of it and soaking his grey robe black. Beside him lay a short knife, all blood; the window on the river side was open, and a torn strip of cloth hung on the window frame.

Jingjing knelt and held him. The others came one after another behind him, stood in the doorway holding torches; someone was shouting, someone was crying, someone climbed out the window to give chase; Elder Marcus pushed in and knelt behind Jingjing saying scripture, saying it trembling all over. Jingjing heard none of it; he saw only that his uncle's eyes were open, searching for him in the firelight, and having found him, his left hand came up and took hold of his wrist, and held it very tight.

His uncle spoke, in the country speech of Balkh, very softly, very slowly, stopping for breath every few words. Jingjing put his ear to his mouth, and heard him say: "Don't punish him." He drew a few more breaths, and said: "Don't kill him." Blood ran from the corner of his mouth, and he said again: "Everyone's lived in fear following me these many years. Too tired." When he had said these three things, his eyes were still on Jingjing, and his lips moved, but no more sound came. His left hand let go of Jingjing's wrist and felt across to the empty right sleeve, felt the knot, and the fingers stopped on the knot, and did not move again.

When it was light, there was not a quiet place in the whole Patriarchate. The deacon in charge of the gates and the servants called the two who had kept the gate the night before into the middle of the courtyard, had the gatekeeping brothers all stand by to watch, and said: where the Archdeacon slept at night, only the men at the gate knew; if it wasn't this one, it was that one; and sent for a whip. The old brother knelt on the ground, unable to hear a thing, only knocking his head on the ground; Baluma stood bolt upright, his face grey.

Several of the old elders stopped him, and called Jingjing over and asked what the Archdeacon had said at the last. Jingjing stood in the middle of the courtyard with everyone looking at him. He thought a while, and put his uncle's three sentences into Syriac one by one, without changing a word. The courtyard fell silent. The gate deacon asked Jingjing who the "him" was that the Archdeacon spoke of; Jingjing said his uncle had not said who. The gate deacon would have asked more, but the oldest elder waved his hand and said: "You've heard the Archdeacon's words as well. Never mind the whip; shut the men up for now, and we'll see."

That afternoon the elders washed his uncle's body, and Jingjing stood by holding the water. His uncle was very thin, so thin his ribs showed one by one; below his right shoulder there was only a short stump of arm, the scar old, long since healed flat. When he was washed, they dressed him in a new grey robe, the right sleeve empty, hanging over the edge of the bed. One of the elders reached out to tie the knot, and Jingjing said: let me. He rolled the sleeve up to the elbow, and tied a knot with both hands, and when it was tied he looked at it: not tight enough, not neat enough; he untied it and tied it again, and still it was not tight enough. Three times in a row he tied it, and not once did it come near the one his uncle tied himself; at last he stopped tying it, pressed the knot flat with his hand, and stepped back.

After his uncle was buried, the Patriarchate's lamps needed oil. This had been Baluma's charge; Baluma was shut up in a room and not let out, so it fell to Jingjing. He took the oil jar to the storehouse to draw oil, and when he opened the big oil vat, there was only a little left in the bottom. He stood a while beside the vat, and thought of Baluma going out of the gate that evening with the empty jar, saying he was going into the city to buy lamp oil.

That day he went into the city to buy flatbread, and the bread-seller, wrapping it for him, said: that loud-voiced gatekeeper of yours paid off his account last month, most of a year's bread money, every last coin. Jingjing asked what he had paid it with. The bread-seller picked out a silver coin from his money box to show him: newly struck, writing on both faces, not a single image, the letters at its edge still bright. He said he'd had several coins like that, and had asked him himself whether he'd come into money, and he'd said a letter had come from home—his mother's illness was better. Jingjing paid for the bread and carried it back. That night he wrote only two lines in his diary: "The lamp oil was not bought. The bread money is paid." When he had written them, he rolled up the scroll, and sat on his bed all night.

Before light the next day, before the board had been struck, Jingjing went to the river. Baluma had been let out; the elders said there was no proof, and he was not to be shut up any more. He was sitting on a stone by the river, looking at the water. Jingjing went over, stood beside him, and asked him: that day, the lamp oil—where was it?

Baluma did not turn round; his shoulders began to shake first, and he covered his face with both hands, and when he put them down his eyes were red, and his mouthful of white teeth was biting his lip. He told it. In the spring, a man in the city had sought him out at the door of the bread shop, very well dressed, very courteous in his speech, who knew he owed bread money, knew his mother was sick, knew he had followed the Archdeacon nine years. The man said it was nothing else; he only wanted to say a few words to the Archdeacon face to face, but the Archdeacon would see no one; all he needed was to know which room he slept in on a given night, and he would give him a bag of money, half first, the other half when the thing was done. He had agreed, and in the summer the first half came into his hands. "He said it was only a few words," Baluma said. "I knew in my heart it wasn't. I told myself it was, told myself so all summer, and by the end even I believed it."

He asked Jingjing again: those last words of the Archdeacon's—when you translated them, did you really not change one word? Jingjing said he had not. Baluma said: "Then the Archdeacon knew. He knew it was me; that's why he said don't punish him, don't kill him." Jingjing said his uncle had not said who. Baluma said there was no need to say it; those eyes of the Archdeacon's—who could they not see through? When he had said it, he crouched on the stone and wept like a child.

A little greyish white showed in the sky to the east. Jingjing stood there, remembering how his uncle's left hand had held his wrist so tight, remembering his uncle saying everyone had lived in fear following him these many years, too tired. He said to Baluma: "Go. Go today. Don't go back to the Patriarchate, don't go back to the city, and don't go looking for that man." Baluma raised his head and looked at him, his lips moving a few times, and said the other half of the money hadn't been paid yet. Jingjing did not answer him, only looked at him. Baluma bowed his head, nodded, stood up, made Jingjing a deep bow, turned, and went south along the dirt road by the river, very fast, and never once looked back. Behind him, the Patriarchate's board began to sound: tok, tok, tok.

Jingjing went back and told the old elders that the man had gone; his uncle had said: don't punish him, don't kill him. He did not say who the man was, and the elders did not ask. The gate deacon roared at him a long while: what right had a child from the east to decide for the Patriarchate? Jingjing stood there and did not answer back once. When the gate deacon had done roaring, the oldest elder said the boy had not decided anything for the Patriarchate; he had passed on the Archdeacon's words; this matter must still go to the Patriarch to settle.

That afternoon the oldest elder took Jingjing to clear out the room beside the storehouse, and under the pillow they found the wooden board. On the board were a few lines in his uncle's left hand, the business of the next day; the first few items were the Patriarchate's odd jobs, and the last was: the roll for Chang'an—make a fair copy, ask the Patriarch to seal it.

In the evening, the Patriarch had them all summoned. Patriarch Jacob was past sixty; he sat in an old chair with a piece of felt over his knees, looked first at the wooden board, heard the oldest elder tell all about Baluma, and had Jingjing say his uncle's three sentences once more. Jingjing said them once more. The Patriarch sat a while with his eyes closed, and said: "Shapur followed me nine years, and never asked me for one thing. This is the first. Do as he said. The man has gone, so he's gone; from now on no one is to speak of going after him." He had the roll his uncle had written fair-copied, sealed, wrapped in oilcloth, and given to Elder Azarel to keep; and appointed three elders, Azarel, Marya and Samaan, to go east to Chang'an with Jingjing once the summer heat had passed.

On the third morning after Baluma left, the old brother who opened the gate found a man outside the gate of the Patriarchate, lying on the dirt road beyond it, his throat cut, the blood already dry, the money bag on him gone, his belt torn off: it was Baluma. On his feet was a new pair of shoes, oxhide, the soles barely touched by dust, the stitches on the uppers close and even. The old brother squatted beside him, unable to hear, and kept asking: what is this, what is this. Jingjing stood at the gate, looking at those shoes.

When they buried Baluma, there was another quarrel at the Patriarchate. Some said he was the man who had sold the Archdeacon, and could not be buried in the monastery's burial ground; some said he had been killed—if they didn't bury him, were they to throw him in the river? The gate deacon said: throw him in the river. In the end it was the oldest elder again who said: the Archdeacon said not to punish him, and the Patriarch said to do as the Archdeacon said; so bury him. They buried him at the edge of the burial ground, against the foot of the wall, far from the other graves.

What to set up before the grave, they quarrelled over again. A cross—some would not allow it; his name—some were unwilling; his crime—no one was willing to take up the brush for that. At the end of the quarrel no one could say what should be written. Jingjing went to the storehouse and found a wooden stake, sharpened one end, carried it to the grave on his shoulder, set it on the mound, and drove it down with a wooden mallet. Nothing was carved on the stake; it was bare, with only the marks of the knife where it had been shaved. The burial ground was very quiet; there was only the sound of the mallet on the stake, blow by blow, carrying all the way to the river. Elder Marcus sat by his teacher's grave and watched from far off. Later he hardly even went to the grave any more, but asked for a small room in the back courtyard and sat at its door every day in the sun, telling his beads and speaking to no one. At the last blow Jingjing stopped, and laid the mallet on the ground beside the grave.
