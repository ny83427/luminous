# Volume Seven　A-Lian

## Chapter 39　Reunion

> The Law flowed through the ten circuits, and the state was rich in peace; monasteries filled a hundred cities, and every house abounded in Luminous blessing.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

When spring opened in the third year of Dali, Jingjing went on a long journey alone for the monastery for the first time. He went to Lingzhou.

That spring Old Master Jiao had sent a letter by someone, written for him by an old scholar in the city who wrote letters for hire, in Tang characters, extremely courteous—courteous for most of a sheet—and only at the end came to the matter: the Great Virtue Yi's old house had a corner of its roof fallen in; the apricot tree was still there, and had flowered this year; the monastery's lamp he lit every day, and the bronze cross at the foot of the east wall of the old house he went in and wiped every day too; only he was seventy this year, his eyes were dim, his hands shook as he wiped it, and he was afraid of wiping it to ruin; he begged the Chang'an monastery to send someone to look, and to say to whom this monastery should be handed from now on. Yisi in those two years hardly went out; Guo Ziyi was at Hezhong and called him once or twice a year, always on official business; it was Jingjing who read him the letter. When Yisi had heard it, he said neither that he would go nor that he would not; he asked for the sheet, folded it, put it inside his coat, and two days later told Elder Azarel to send Jingjing on the Lingzhou business. Azarel said Jingjing was Deacon of Charity; the gruel kitchen couldn't do without him. Yisi said the gruel kitchen had Shiyiniang, whose accounts were clearer than Jingjing's; let Jingjing go, and come back having entered the people of Lingzhou household by household in a register, and from now on, when the monastery wrote to the Patriarchate, it could say how many households and how many souls Lingzhou had—better than if he went himself. Elder Samaan, listening beside them, did not object, only opened his leather-bound account book and made an entry: The Superintendent ordered the Deacon of Charity to Lingzhou on a tour of inspection; the elders in council approved.

Jingjing went with a convoy of carts taking grain to Lingzhou, and travelled twenty-odd days. The day he reached Lingzhou a light rain was falling; Old Master Jiao waited for him at the monastery gate from the hour of *chen* to the hour of *wu*, and seeing a tall young *hu* monk leading a horse toward him through the rain, stood stunned a good while before he recognized the little child who used to go every day with the Great Virtue Yi to the east of the city to buy sesame cakes; he seized his hand and led him first to the Great Virtue Yi's old house, to see the bronze cross at the foot of the east wall. The cross was wiped to a shine; of the three beads on the round knobs at its four ends, one had been rubbed flat. Jingjing stayed in Lingzhou more than a month. The fallen corner of the roof of Yisi's old house he had Wang the plasterer of the city come and mend; Wang the plasterer still skimped his work, and Jingjing stood at the foot of the ladder counting the tiles one by one, and when he had counted three short, Wang the plasterer went red in the face and climbed up again. He went to see the Shi Six family in the east of the city: old father Shi was dead; the widowed sister-in-law had two nephews with her, the elder fifteen now, apprenticed at a tannery in the city; hearing someone had come from Chang'an, he ran home and stood at the door not daring to come in. Jingjing gave his sister-in-law a bolt of silk Yisi had sent by him, and told the boy one by one Shi Six's name, the year he died, where he was buried; when the boy had heard it, he said that when he grew up he would go to Chang'an, to see his uncle under that crook-necked willow by the sluice gate. Jingjing said good; the monastery would feed him. He went round the households of old believers left in the city one by one too, and entered them in the register: eleven households, forty-odd souls, how many in each household, what the men did, whether the women could recite the scripture, whether the children were baptized—all written down. Old Mother Kang, who sold sesame cakes in the east of the city, had died some years before; the shop was now selling under a Uighur, who baked his cakes thick; Jingjing bought one, took a bite, remembered the thin kind his Pa had taken him to buy every day as a child, did not finish it, and tucked it away to take back. The night before he left, he slept in Yisi's old house; the room was bare; the wooden peg on the west wall was still there, the one for hanging the shield; as a child he had stood on tiptoe trying to reach that shield, and could not. In the small hours he woke once and heard the leaves of the apricot tree in the courtyard rustling in the rain; he lay listening a while and remembered his Pa standing under this tree in those days, standing an hour at a time, and he had not known then what his Pa was thinking.

The Lingzhou monastery was afterwards handed to Old Master Jiao's nephew, surnamed Kang, whose father was a Sogdian who dealt in felt in the city; he was baptized and knew a few Syriac letters; Jingjing taught him half a month, taught him the liturgy for the Lord's Day, taught him to enter people in the register. When he left, Old Master Jiao saw him off to the city gate and asked when the Great Virtue Yi would come back; Jingjing said his Pa was in Chang'an now and hardly went out. Old Master Jiao said: that's good too; when a man is old, he ought to have somewhere he doesn't go out from.

---

Back from Lingzhou, Jingjing stayed in Chang'an less than a year before he went to Luoyang.

This time it was Old Jacob who was ill. Word came from the Xiushan Ward that the old deacon had not been able to get up since winter set in; he coughed at night till every dog in the ward barked; he wanted to see someone from Chang'an, and named whom: he wanted to see Bishop Yonan. The letter was written by Luo Fifteen; Luo Fifteen was twenty-seven or -eight now, drove a cart at the South Market of Luoyang, and wrote Tang characters better than his grandfather; the word "Bishop" in the letter was written especially large. When the monastery's people had read the letter, they all looked at Yonan. Yonan was sixty-four that year, and since coming to Chang'an with the seven elders in the first year of Qianyuan had not left the city of Chang'an in eleven years, had seldom even gone south of the city. When he had read the letter he folded it and said he would go. Azarel said the road would be cold; Yonan said he had crossed the Pamirs. So Azarel had Jingjing go with him, saying that when Old Jacob's business was done, Jingjing was to come back and report how the Xiushan Ward should go on.

It was the first time Jingjing had travelled far with Yonan, the two of them alone.

From Chang'an to Luoyang took eleven days. Along the way Yonan spoke very little, just as at Xizhou; he got up before light and first looked for water to wash his hands; when the inn had no water he went to the well and drew it; when the well was iced over he knocked a hole in the ice and reached in and scooped up water in his hands, and when he had done, both hands were red with cold. Jingjing stood to one side watching, did not step up to break the ice for him or draw the water for him, and when he had finished washing handed him a cloth; Yonan took the cloth, dried his hands, and only then went to eat. He ate only half a flatbread, tucked away the other half, and at the next stop crumbled it for the sparrows at the post station gate. Jingjing watched him do this for eleven days. Before, at Xizhou, at Chang'an, when he saw Yonan wash his hands he thought nothing of it; an old man, with rules of his own. Now that he knew that thing, seeing those hands reach into icy water again, it seemed to him that what the hands were soaking in was something else. Several times he meant to speak, and the words got to the edge of his mouth and he swallowed them again. There were too many things he wanted to ask; he did not know which to ask first; and he thought too: if I ask, what then; if Yonan answers, what then? Yonan seemed to notice nothing; at night on the earthen bed of the inn he talked to him about Old Jacob, about the early Tianbao years when he was bishop and Old Jacob, fifty or so, was in charge in the Xiushan Ward, and came to Chang'an every year at the Fast with a cartload of Luoyang jujubes, jujubes left over from the jujube-paste filling of the Mu family's sesame-cake shop next door, which the people of the Chang'an monastery all loved; and talking, he fell asleep. Jingjing lay beside him listening to him snore, very lightly, as though afraid of disturbing someone.

When they reached the Xiushan Ward, Old Jacob could no longer speak. He lay in a small room in the back courtyard with Luo Fifteen watching over him; when he saw Yonan come in at the door his eyes lit up all at once; he raised a hand, the hand shaking, and drew a cross in the air, and when he had drawn it the hand did not come down but pointed toward the wall; beyond the wall was the Mu family's sesame-cake shop next door; he pointed twice, making sounds in his throat, and could not get it out. Yonan thought he wanted water and had Luo Fifteen bring some; Old Jacob shook his head and pointed again, pointed till he had no strength left, and only then let his hand fall. Yonan knelt by his bed holding that hand, held it two days and two nights, and at dawn on the third day Old Jacob died. Yonan anointed him with oil and said the prayers; the fourteen households of the Xiushan Ward all came and knelt all over the courtyard. He was buried at the foot of Mangshan north of the city, a bank of earth between him and Old Mu's grave.

When the burial was done, Yonan fell ill; he had taken a chill on the road, and then kept two nights' watch, and lay seven or eight days in the back courtyard. Jingjing, while tending him, set the Xiushan Ward's affairs in order: the hall had not yet been rebuilt; the few rooms in the back courtyard leaked; of the fourteen households twelve remained now, two having gone with relatives to Yangzhou; from now on the Xiushan Ward would have no deacon; Luo Fifteen could read and write, but he drove a cart, and once he was out the door it was ten days or half a month. Jingjing sat down with the heads of the twelve households and talked an evening, and they settled that Luo Fifteen would be deacon and drive his cart as before, coming back for the Lord's Day; whenever anyone in the ward had business, they would go first to Luo Fifteen's mother; Luo Fifteen's mother was Old Jacob's daughter, past forty, loud-voiced, and able to keep people in hand. When that was settled, Jingjing entered the twelve households in the register one by one, and when they were entered left each household a roll of the newly translated hymns he had copied out in Chang'an and brought, in Tang, for them to sing from; whatever they could not manage, Luo Fifteen would teach them when he came back.

On the night of Old Jacob's burial, Luo Fifteen's mother told Jingjing that her father had grown muddled in his last years, and kept going on about a woman who had worked next door at the Mu family's in early years; he said that woman kept her accounts in the monastery's letters; he said he had wronged Chang'an, and every time he said it he wept, and she didn't know how he had wronged Chang'an. Yonan was lying in fever in the small room in the back courtyard, and did not hear.

The Mu family's sesame-cake shop next door was still open. The Mu daughter-in-law had remarried, to another sesame-cake seller, surnamed He, a distant cousin of her first husband; the shop was still the Mu shop, the signboard unchanged. Jingjing went to buy cakes; the Mu daughter-in-law knew him—the year she went with Old Jacob to Chang'an to deliver jujube-paste cakes she had seen this little deacon—and flatly refused his money, and pressed on him a packet of jujube-paste cakes, saying they were for that sick old bishop. Jingjing took the cakes and stood at the shop door a while talking with her, asked how the shop had done these years. She said the shop had been started by her father-in-law and had been open thirty-odd years; in the old days when her father-in-law was alive, business was good, and they had employed several helpers; one was a woman from Chang'an who worked in the shop nine years, and then when An Lushan came she left, went south, nobody knew where. She said that woman was strange: next door was your monastery, one wall between, and she baked cakes at the stove and could hear the chanting over there; nine years she listened, and never once went in at the monastery gate; Old Jacob asked her several times and she wouldn't go. She said that woman had been taught by her father-in-law to bake cakes, and the jujube-paste filling her father-in-law taught her too; the woman learned fast, clever with her hands, only she got chilblains on her hands winter after winter; her father-in-law told her to rub them with lard and she wouldn't, said someone had once made up a medicine for her, and when the medicine was used up she wouldn't use anything else. She said too that the woman could read, could read that curly writing of your monastery's, though she knew few Tang characters; she kept the accounts in that writing, writing Tang people's names in it; her father-in-law couldn't read it and told her to change, and she wouldn't, said that was the only kind she knew, and in the end her father-in-law let her be.

Jingjing asked her what that woman was called.

The Mu daughter-in-law thought and said that in the shop they all called her A-Lian; what her surname was, nobody ever asked.

Jingjing, holding that packet of jujube-paste cakes, stood at the shop door until the Mu daughter-in-law thought he meant to buy something else, and asked whether he wanted soup. He said no, thanked her, and went back to the back courtyard. Yonan was still lying in the small room; his fever had come down somewhat; seeing him come in, he asked whether it was cold outside. Jingjing said it wasn't, opened the packet of jujube-paste cakes and set it by Yonan's pillow, and said the Mu family had sent them. Yonan took one, looked at it, said it was the same as it used to be, took a bite, chewed a long time, and said it was the same taste as it used to be too. Jingjing sat by the bed watching him eat. That time he very nearly said it. The words got to his throat; he remembered what Narsai had said under the window, to vow and not keep it is to lie before the Lord; he remembered too what his Pa had said that night under the lamp, that was Yonan's affair. He swallowed the words back, swallowed them till his throat ached. On the road back to Chang'an, Yonan asked him how he had set the Xiushan Ward's affairs in order, and he told him one by one: Luo Fifteen, the twelve households, the hymns; only the thing at the Mu shop he did not tell.

---

In those years most of the monastery's business fell on Jingjing.

Yisi hardly went out. Guo Ziyi was at Hezhong, and later moved his command to Binzhou, and called him once or twice a year, always on official business; he went, stood before the tent, reported, and when he had reported the administrative officer gave him a dispatch which he brought back in his coat; nor did he tell anyone what Guo Ziyi had said to him, and probably Guo Ziyi had said nothing. At the monastery he kept the general accounts and the letters to the Patriarchate, and the rest of the time split firewood in the courtyard, made up medicines at the chest with Elder Ishaq, and taught Lingbao his letters. At three Lingbao could say "Alaha," and said it "Alaaah," like a yawn; Yisi did not correct him, but had him say it, and each time he said it gave him a jujube. Shiyiniang kept the gruel kitchen's accounts, kept the side courtyard's meals, kept Yisi's robes. In Elder Samaan's account book the entries about the Superintendent grew fewer year by year and the entries about the Deacon of Charity grew more year by year; he said that was according to the rule; the Superintendent shouldn't have charge of everything anyway.

Jingjing translated scripture in the west wing, gave out gruel at the gruel kitchen, made up medicines at the chest, the three by turns, and did not get three double-hours' sleep a day. In those years he made several more journeys. Outside the county town of Zhouzhi there was an old monastery set up in the Kaiyuan years by a few *hu* families who had moved from Liangzhou; it had scattered in the upheaval; in the fifth year of Dali someone came from there and said there were still seven or eight households left, who wanted someone from the Chang'an monastery to come and see them. Jingjing went, with Elder Ishaq; Ishaq rode his old donkey, complaining all the way that the donkey jolted; but when they reached Zhouzhi and saw those households living in a mountain gully, the monastery reduced to three earthen rooms with grass growing on their roofs, and still set in the lintel a blue brick carved with a cross, Ishaq stopped complaining of the jolting, got off the donkey, and stroked that brick till the dirt came off it. They stayed half a month at Zhouzhi; Ishaq treated the sick, and baptized too, five children; Jingjing heated the water and carried it over mixed to warm, but the wind was strong in the gully and by the time he got it to the basin it was half cold; the eldest, nine years old, stood by the bronze basin shivering with cold and asked Jingjing why the water was cold; Jingjing said the wind had blown on it; next time he'd bring a charcoal stove. Back in Chang'an he added another page to the register: Zhouzhi, seven households, thirty-one souls.

That register, at first he only entered people in it; then it grew thicker and thicker. The Yining Ward of Chang'an, several hundred souls; the few households left of the old Persian monastery in the Liquan Ward; the Xiushan Ward of Luoyang, twelve households, and two more gone to Yangzhou; Lingzhou, eleven households; Zhouzhi, seven. And there was what he had learned from the *hu* merchants of the West Market: in the city of Liangzhou there had been a monastery, and when the Tibetans came some of its people fled and some stayed, and under the Tibetans still lit the lamp; there was one at Shazhou too, and at Ganzhou; but the road through Hexi was cut, and how the people there were, nobody could say. In the city of Taiyuan there were a few Sogdian households who had moved there from Lingzhou and said the Lord's Day prayers themselves. The *hu* merchants of Chengdu said there were a few households there too, with no elder. At Guangzhou *hu* merchants came by sea, and some on the ships kept the faith and had set up a small building outside the city; Li Su's family had lived in Guangzhou in early years and knew those households; Jingjing asked him to write and ask, and the reply said yes, twenty-odd souls, all merchants from Persia, no elder, reciting the scripture themselves.

One night in the winter of the sixth year of Dali, in the scripture room, Jingjing took this register apart and rebound it, one page to a place, ordered by the Tang circuits. Elder Azarel could not sleep that night, came over with a lamp to look, sat down across from him, and turned the pages one by one; when he came to the page for Lingnan he asked how many circuits the Tang realm was divided into. Jingjing said ten, fixed by the Emperor Taizong: Guannei, Henan, Hedong, Hebei, Shannan, Longyou, Huainan, Jiangnan, Jiannan, Lingnan; now they were divided more finely, but when the old people spoke of them they still said the ten circuits. Azarel asked how many of the ten we held. Jingjing counted page by page—Guannei, Henan, Longyou, Hedong, Huainan, Jiannan, Lingnan—and when he had counted said: seven. Azarel shut the register and held it under his hand, and said that of these places, half the people at the Patriarchate had never heard; if he went back and told them, the people at the Patriarchate would think he was boasting. Jingjing said: the elder isn't going back. Azarel said yes, he wasn't going back; he was old, he couldn't go back; from now on this register was what he looked at on the Patriarchate's behalf; once he had looked at it, the Patriarchate had looked at it. When he had said it, he took up his lamp and left, got to the door, turned his head, and told Jingjing to bind in pages for Hebei, Shannan and Jiangnan too, and leave them empty; when there was something later, write it in. So Jingjing bound in three empty pages, between the others.

Writing to the Patriarchate grew harder and harder too. After Hexi was lost, only two roads were left going west. One went north, out beyond the frontier from Lingzhou and Fengzhou, through Uighur country to Beiting, then west by Suyab to the region of Shash and Samarkand, then south past Balkh into the Two Rivers; this road the Uighurs controlled; in Chang'an the Uighurs ran riot, but outside Chang'an they kept to rules; pay them, and a letter got through. The other went south, by ship from Guangzhou, across the sea of the Tazi, landing at Basra, then north to the Two Rivers; this road was far; once a ship set out it took a year, and if it met a storm the ship sank and the letter sank with it. When the monastery wrote a letter, it made two copies, one to go north and one to go south; it was Jingjing who copied them, and when he had copied them he wrote a line in small characters at the end of each, on one "by the Uighur road," on the other "by the sea road," and whichever arrived first, the Patriarchate would read. In those years replies from the Patriarchate came only twice. Once in the sixth year of Dali, saying Patriarch Jacob was old and his eyes could no longer see; once in the ninth year of Dali, saying Patriarch Jacob had died the winter before, and the newly made patriarch was called Hnanisho. The reply had been sent from Baghdad; the letter said the new patriarch was often in Baghdad, the Caliph's new city; the patriarch had to go there to ask the Caliph's favour. When Yisi read that letter he remembered what Jingjing had told him on coming back from the Patriarchate, that his uncle had said: wherever the Caliph is, those who have business to ask of the Caliph must be. He handed the letter to Jingjing, told him to read it to the elders, and went out himself to split firewood.

In the first month of the seventh year of Dali, Uighurs lodging at the Court of State Ceremonial ran out into the wards to plunder, plundered their way to the Yining Ward, and at the monastery gate stopped several women coming back from the gruel kitchen and pulled at their bundles; Shiyiniang was among them. Jingjing happened that day to be coming back from the West Market, saw it, ran over, stood in front of the women, and spoke to the Uighurs in Uighur. His Uighur he had learned from the old Tocharian believer of the monastery who had dealt in horses in his youth; he had learned little—only a few curses and a few pleas for mercy; he picked the pleas and said: this is the Daqin monastery; that *hu* monk who guided you the year of the Xiangji Temple is in this monastery; he's my father. The Uighurs stood still a moment and looked at each other—whether they had understood or not, who knew—and just then a few receptionists of the Court of State Ceremonial came running from the ward gate, panting, shouting the name of the Uighurs' leader, and the Uighurs threw down the bundles and went off with them cursing; passing the monastery gate, one of them looked up at the cross-shaped hollow chiselled in the lintel and spat on the ground. When Yisi heard of it afterwards he asked what he had said to the Uighurs, and Jingjing told him truthfully. Yisi put down the axe in his hand and said: next time don't bring me up; bringing me up is no use, and it shouldn't be done. Jingjing said that time it was of use. Yisi said there were plenty of things that were of use; what shouldn't be done, shouldn't; and when he had said it, asked whether Shiyiniang had been frightened. Shiyiniang, cutting vegetables in the kitchen, heard, and shouted from inside that she hadn't been frightened; she'd rather like to see whether those Uighurs dared come and grab her bundle again.

When spring opened in the tenth year of Dali, Jingjing was twenty-nine.

The first time he saw that wooden board was one evening at the end of the second month. That day an old beggar came to the gruel kitchen and said that in a lane to the south someone was too sick to get up, lying under the eaves of a shop, and had eaten nothing for three days. Jingjing ladled a bowl of gruel, covered it with a cloth, and followed the old beggar south to find him. The lanes south of the West Market—when he gave out gruel he ordinarily went only to their mouths, not in; that day he went in, turning this way and that, until they came out on a somewhat wider street; the sick man had already been helped inside by a young helper from one of the shops, and lay on the straw at the back of the shop, drinking soup they had given him. Jingjing put down the gruel and felt the man's forehead: not hot; it was hunger; and he was relieved. When he came out, the sun was still more than a pole's height up, and the gong on the market tower began to sound.

The West Market closed to the sound of a gong. Seven quarters before sunset the gong on the market tower was struck, stroke after stroke, three hundred strokes, and when they were done the market gates shut; sellers packed up their stalls, buyers went out. As soon as the gong sounded the whole street was on the move, men with carrying poles, men leading donkeys, men pushing carts, crowding toward the north gate, crowding till the dust in the lanes flew up. Jingjing stood at the door of that shop waiting for the people to pass. It was a very small shop, one room facing the street, a sesame-cake oven at its door, the charcoal in the firebox nearly out, two cakes still stuck to the oven wall, golden-brown; inside, two low tables, the bowls on them not yet cleared. On the doorframe hung a board of white wood with what was sold written on it—cakes, soup, mutton soup, *botuo* noodles—in Tang characters, every stroke written very hard, as if traced over someone else's writing. Below the board, on a nail, hung another, smaller board, written on in charcoal line after line, like accounts.

Jingjing had been watching the people; his eyes passed over that small board and stopped.

What was written on it was not Tang characters but Syriac letters, line after line, right to left, spelling out the sounds of Tang: Zhang San, owes four cakes; Old Wang, owes two soups; so-and-so, paid. Spelled crookedly, a few letters written backwards, but nothing misspelled. At the end of each entry was drawn a letter Nun, like a mark of signature. The Syriac Nun, written at the end, ought to trail its tail down to the bottom of the line; this one flicked up instead, flicked up very high, as if at this point the writer's hand had let go and the pen had run up of its own accord.

The gong was still sounding. People pushed past him in the street; someone bumped his arm, and he did not move. That upward-flicking tail he felt he had seen somewhere, and for the moment could not remember where; halfway through the gong he remembered: in the parchment-bound register of baptisms in the scripture room, below Elder Yonan's thin line of writing, four crooked letters, Alaph, Lamadh, Yudh, Nun. That day he had pressed on that page till his palm was damp, and had thought only that the letters were crooked, and had not noticed the tail; now, remembering, that Nun too, it seemed, flicked up.

"Will the guest have a cake?"

Someone spoke inside the shop, a woman's voice, not loud, with something of a Chang'an accent, and yet not wholly Chang'an, the ends of the words sinking, as if she had lived elsewhere a long time.

Jingjing raised his head. A woman came out from inside the shop, came to the side of the oven, bent and peeled the two cakes from the oven wall, and put them in a bamboo basket. She was fifty or so, not tall, somewhat stout now, her hair more than half white, put up in a knot at the back of her head with a wooden pin, an old apron tied on her, both hands covered in flour, all the way to the wrists. When she had peeled off the cakes she straightened up and looked at him once; her eyes were very dark. Jingjing looked at her, and she looked at him. The look she gave him was not like the look she gave other customers; it was as though she had long known who stood at her door, and only had not thought he would be standing here today. She wiped her hands once on her apron; the flour did not come off; she wiped them again, and said: "The oven's to be banked. These two are all that's left. If the guest wants them, take them."

Jingjing said he did. He took money from his sleeve, counted out two coins, and held them out. She did not take the money, but told him to put it on the table; she took one cake herself, wrapped it in a dry lotus leaf, handed it to him, then turned and went in and brought out a bowl of soup, hot, steaming, and set it on the low table at the door, and said the soup cost nothing; drink it before you go; the wind's getting up outside.

Jingjing sat down at that low table and drank the soup. It was boiled from mutton bones, salty, scalding; he drank it very slowly. She was clearing bowls inside the shop, stacking them one by one, stacking them very neatly, and did not come out again. The gong finished; the people in the street had all gone; the young helper came out from the back and banked the oven. When Jingjing had finished the soup, he stood up and called "Thank you" toward the inside; a voice answered from inside, and no one came out. He took the cake, went to the door, and looked once more at that small board: the few lines right to left, the tail of the last Nun flicking upward.

He went out of the north gate of the West Market; the market gate was already half shut, and the market guard shouted at him. He tucked the cake inside his robe; it was still warm; all the way back to the Yining Ward the cake pressed against his chest, and kept warm.

That night he unlocked the cupboard with the brass key to the scripture room, took out the register of baptisms, turned to the page of the ninth month of the fifth year of Tianbao, and looked at that mark under the lamp, looked once, and again. It was the same. The tail of that Nun had flicked up just so thirty years ago, flicked up just as high, and in thirty years had not changed.

He locked the register away again, took the cake from his robe, and laid it on the table. It had gone cold and hard, and pressed into it was a crease, the mark of his robe's front. He did not eat it, nor throw it away, but found a cloth and wrapped it and put it in the wooden box in his room where he kept the rolls of his diary.

---

For ten-odd days after that, Jingjing said nothing to anyone.

He went to the gruel kitchen every day as usual; the people at the gruel kitchen came and went, and he ladled bowl after bowl. Several times he raised his head and saw, behind the crowd outside the gruel kitchen, a grey-haired woman in an apron, standing far off, not queuing, not leaving; she stood a while, and turned and went off south. He did not go after her. He remembered what the Mu daughter-in-law had said, that the woman got chilblains on her hands winter after winter, that someone had once made up a medicine for her, and when it was used up she wouldn't use anything else. He remembered too what the old granny peeling scallions in the kitchen of the West Market eating-house had said: that *hu* monk was a good man; in winter he brought her medicine. He thought that if he went and told Elder Yonan, it would be telling a man who had vowed: the one you wait for is two wards away; and when Yonan heard it, whether he went or not, kept the vow or not, it would be Jingjing who had sent him down that road. He thought that if he didn't tell, Elder Yonan would go on every evening lighting the lamp at the gate, and when it was lit stand there looking toward the ward gate, looking all his life, and he would watch Yonan looking all his life. Of these two, he could settle on neither. At night he tossed and turned till dawn, and rose and went to the hall and knelt half a pace behind his Pa, and told Alaha he could not settle it, and begged Alaha to settle it for him. Alaha in those days gave him no word. One day in the west wing he asked Gabriel how to put into Tang the line in the passage of the prodigal son where the father sees the son coming back afar off and runs, and falls on his neck—whether to use the word *run*: Tang fathers don't run. Gabriel said the scripture says run, so write run. Jingjing said: and the son? When the son comes up to him, those few words he says, he'd thought them out all along the road, thought them out the whole way, and when he got there, before he had finished saying them, the father had already called for the robe to be brought. Gabriel looked at him and said: what you're asking today isn't how to translate. Jingjing said it was how to translate. Gabriel asked no more, and told him to write it as the scripture had it.

He thought too of going to tell his Pa. Once after supper Yisi was whittling a wooden horse for Lingbao in the courtyard; Jingjing went over and squatted beside him, squatted till his legs went numb; Yisi finished one leg of the horse, looked up at him, and asked whether he wanted something. He said no, and asked how many legs Pa would whittle on this horse. Yisi said four; did that need asking? He said true, and got up and left. After a few steps he heard Yisi behind him say: whatever you have to say, say it when you want to.

At the beginning of the third month, Elder Azarel took Marya, Samaan and some of the other elders to the house of an old believer south of the city; the old man of that house was near his end, and was to receive the Body and be anointed. Elder Ishaq had been asked the day before by a minor clerk of the Metropolitan Prefecture to look at his mother's eyes, had stayed the night at the man's house, and was not yet back. Babai was mending scrolls in the scripture room; Yisi had taken Shiyiniang and Lingbao to the Cheng grave—that day was the anniversary of the Cheng husband's death; Shiyiniang went every year, and every year Yisi went with her. Only a few guard brothers were left in the monastery; Gabriel and Narsai were in the west wing, Yonan was saying the evening office in the front hall, Jingjing was in the gatehouse checking the gruel accounts.

Around the hour of *wei*, someone looked in at the monastery gate: a porter of twenty or so, sweat all over his head and face, standing outside the threshold not daring to come in; seeing Jingjing come out of the gatehouse, he asked whether there was a *hu* master here who could treat the sick. He said his child had been feverish two days, wouldn't take the breast, couldn't even cry; he'd heard that at the *hu* temple in the Yining Ward there was an old master who treated the sick and took no money. Jingjing asked where he lived. The porter said south of the West Market, in the lane behind a sesame-cake shop; the shop was run by an old woman; the old woman had a good heart; when the child fell sick, it was she who told him to come; she said there was someone at this monastery who could treat the sick.

Jingjing stood at the gate and heard him out, and stood without moving. The porter thought he was unwilling, grew frantic, and was about to kneel. Jingjing caught hold of him, told him to wait at the gate, and turned and went in. He walked very fast, across the courtyard, along the gallery, to the door of the front hall, and there stopped again.

With Elder Ishaq away, there were only two left who could treat the sick. One was himself; he had made up medicines with Ishaq these several years; he could write a prescription to bring down a fever, and had felt children's pulses too; if he went, wrote a prescription, got it down the child, and waited with his hand on the child's forehead for the fever to break, and when he had waited came back, the thing would be over; the board would still hang there, and he still need say nothing. The other was Elder Yonan. Elder Yonan had studied some years with an old physician at the Patriarchate; those few medicines in his bag he had not changed in thirty years; a child feverish two days—he could treat that too.

The hall was very dark; Yonan knelt before the cross saying the office, in Syriac, very low, line by line, as though afraid of disturbing someone. Jingjing stood at the door of the hall and listened to him finish one passage, and begin another. He thought that once he took this step in, what came after would not be his to settle; and if he turned and left, what came after would not be his to settle either. He remembered what his Pa had said to Elder Yonan under the jujube tree: if the child asks me one day, I won't lie. He thought that if today he went himself and came back and told no one, that would not count as lying; but it would be close to it.

He stepped in at the door of the hall, went up behind Yonan, waited till Yonan had finished that passage, said "Elder," and said there was a porter at the gate whose child had been feverish two days; Elder Ishaq wasn't here; would the Elder go and see?

Yonan stood up, turned round, and asked where the child lived.

Jingjing said: "The West Market. South side, the lane behind a sesame-cake shop."

When Yonan heard the words *West Market*, nothing showed in his face; he only stood there, and after a while spoke, and said he would go back to his room for his medicine bag. He walked past Jingjing, went a few steps, turned his head again, looked at Jingjing once, and asked why Jingjing didn't go; Jingjing had made up medicines with Ishaq all these years; he could do it too. Jingjing said he hadn't finished checking the gruel accounts. Yonan nodded and asked no more, went back to his room, came out with his medicine bag, and followed the porter out of the monastery gate. The lamp at the gate was not yet lit; it was still light.

Jingjing stood at the gate watching the two of them go out of the ward gate and turn south. He went back into the gatehouse, spread the account book open, checked two lines, could not go on, and shut it.

---

The West Market was not quite as Yonan remembered it.

Since he came into Chang'an in the first year of Qianyuan, in seventeen years he had not once gone into the West Market. When the monastery bought rice and oil, paper and ink, medicines, they all went to the West Market; Jingjing gave out gruel, and the gruel kitchen was set up at the West Market; every one of the elders had been; Ishaq went most often—he loved the roast mutton at a *hu* shop in the West Market, and every time he went came back a size fatter. Yonan did not go. When there was something to buy, he had someone bring it; when someone asked him to go along, he said his legs were bad. In fact his legs were very good; at seventy he still walked with his back ramrod straight, and from behind looked no different from the young deacons of the monastery. Ishaq once joked with him: Yonan, someone in the West Market owes you money. Yonan said it was he who owed them. Ishaq roared with laughter and took it that he was joking too.

Now he followed the porter in at the north gate of the West Market. By the north gate a few new buildings had gone up; the silk shops had moved; a row of medicine shops had vanished and become shops selling *hu* ewers; there were fewer people in the streets than in those days—there had been war; Chang'an had lost a great many people and to this day had not recovered; but the sounds were still the same sounds, hawking, haggling, mules braying, the clang of the smithies, all mixed together, humming, like a pot boiling. The porter walked fast, and Yonan behind him walked fast too, through from north to south; at the south side of the West Market the porter turned into a narrow lane. Yonan knew this quarter; it was not far from that lane, two streets away; first his heart tightened, and after a few steps, seeing clearly it was not that one, it loosened; and when it had loosened, he himself did not know why it should loosen.

The porter's home was behind a sesame-cake shop, a half-collapsed earthen room, in it a straw mat, and on the mat lay a child of a year or so, his face burning red, his lips dry and peeling. The child's mother squatted beside him, wiping his forehead with a wet cloth. Yonan squatted down, felt the child's forehead, took his little wrist and felt the pulse, counted a while, and told the mother to boil water, hot. He fed the child medicine, a little liquorice boiled to a juice, mixed with a small pinch of white powder from his porcelain bottle, and taught the mother how to sponge the child with warm water, where to sponge, for how long, how long to rest after each sponging. His Tang was much better than in those days, but he still spoke slowly, squeezing the words out one by one; the mother listened very carefully, nodding at each sentence. He sat in that earthen room more than an hour; the child broke into a sweat all over, the fever came down a little, he whimpered twice and opened his mouth for the breast. The porter knelt and knocked his head on the ground to him; he pulled the porter up and said he'd come again tomorrow.

When he came out of the earthen room the sun had already sunk westward, and the gong on the market tower had not yet sounded. The porter saw him out and pointed to the back door of the shop ahead, and said that was the old woman who'd told him to go to the monastery; the old woman ran a cake shop; if the Elder was hungry, he could eat a cake at her place; she took no money from monastery folk. Yonan looked where he pointed: a small door, firewood stacked by it, a wet apron draped over the woodpile. He said he wasn't hungry, and walked on, going round to the front of the shop to get out of the lane that way.

It was a small shop, a sesame-cake oven at its door, the charcoal in the firebox glowing red, a few porters sitting eating cakes with soup; on the doorframe hung a menu board, and on a nail below the menu board hung a small wooden board. Yonan was about to walk past; he glanced at the small board, and his feet stopped.

On it, line after line, right to left, were Syriac letters spelling the sounds of Tang: Zhang San, owes four cakes; Old Wang, owes two soups; spelled crookedly, some letters written backwards, but nothing wrong. The Nun drawn at the end of each entry had its tail flicked up.

He had taught these letters. In the autumn of the fourth year of Tianbao, after her baptism, in that little room in the back courtyard of the monastery, he had drawn them one by one in charcoal on a spoiled wooden tablet, Alaph, Lamadh, Yudh, Nun, and she drew them after him, and when she came to Nun her hand let go and the tail ran up. He told her the stroke of Nun should trail downward at the end; she said trailing down didn't look nice, flicking up looked nice; and ever after she flicked it up.

Yonan stood before that board, stood till the young man baking cakes by the oven raised his head to look at him.

"Will the guest have a cake?"

It was a woman's voice, coming from inside the shop. Yonan did not turn round. He looked at the board and listened to that voice come out from inside, one step, two steps, to the door, and ask again: guest? He turned round. At the door stood a woman, her hair more than half white, both hands covered in flour; thirty years, and he knew her at a glance. She looked at him; the look on her face first froze, then settled, like a basin of water held in the hands for many years and now set down steady. She stood like that, looking at his face, the flour falling from her fingers bit by bit onto the threshold. After a while she spoke, her voice very low: "Your face. It's still that face."

Yonan opened his mouth. The Tang he could speak was several hundred words more than thirty years ago, and now he could find not one of them. He raised his hands and showed them to her. They were the hands of a man of seventy: lean, dry, the sinews standing out one by one, the backs covered in spots, the knuckles thick, the lines of the skin like a dried riverbed. She looked at those hands a while, and raised her own hands too: both covered in flour, and under the flour crack after crack, scars from burns, the hard calluses left by chilblains that healed and came again and came and healed.

The two of them stood with a threshold between them, holding up their hands. The young man by the oven looked at one and then the other, and put down the iron hook in his hand.

---

Afterwards she brought him into the shop and sat him at the low table inside. The porters in the shop finished eating, left their money, and went. She told the young man, you mind the oven, and sat down across from Yonan and brought him a bowl of water: hot. Yonan, holding the bowl of water, said she still remembered. She said that year when she was sick he had held down the bowl she was going to drink cold water from, and she'd remembered, and never drunk cold again. Yonan said she wasn't sick now. She said: drink it hot, and you won't be.

Yonan held that bowl of hot water, held it and did not drink. She told him of these years. She told it very briefly, as though she had said it over in her heart many times and knew it by heart and did not need many words. That autumn, after the child was given away, she couldn't stay in Chang'an, and left within days, went to Luoyang, and worked at a sesame-cake shop in the Xiushan Ward of Luoyang; the shopkeeper was named Mu, a good man, who taught her to bake sesame cakes, taught her to make jujube-paste filling; next door to the shop was your monastery, a wall between; she baked cakes at the stove and could hear the chanting over there, chanting in words she couldn't understand; she listened and felt steady in her heart; nine years she listened and never went in; the old deacon over there asked her several times, and she wouldn't go; she said she wasn't worthy. Then An Lushan came, Luoyang was lost, and she went south with the refugees to Xiangzhou, and cooked at an inn in Xiangzhou; the war was all confusion: one day they said Chang'an was recovered, the next that the Uighurs had entered Luoyang again; she stayed at Xiangzhou ten-odd years, and only in the autumn of the second year of Dali came back to Chang'an; and when she came back she brought out all the money she'd saved over the years, rented this shopfront, and baked cakes to sell herself. Yonan asked how many years. She said eight. Yonan reckoned in his heart: eight years, and he two wards away.

He asked her how she had got by. She thought, and said: by the Lord, and by my hands. She spread her two hands on the table, palms up; the flour had dried and stuck in flakes to her palms. Yonan looked at those two hands and remembered how thirty years ago, every time she came to the monastery, she first washed her hands clean at the door before holding them out for him to put on the salve; in those days her hands were covered in chilblains, split open, oozing bloody water. He put out one finger, and in the layer of dry flour on her palm slowly wrote a character: one hooked curve, three dots. She looked down, watching that character come out in her own palm stroke by stroke, and the three beans had all dropped into the bowl. She did not move, but kept her hand spread like that, leaving the character in her palm.

After a while she drew her hands back, wiped them on her apron, stood up, fetched a cake from beside the oven, broke it in two, set one half before him and kept the other in her own hand. Yonan took up the half cake and bit into it; it was just out of the oven, too hot to hold, and inside was jujube paste. As he chewed, what he tasted was the Mu family's taste, the same taste as that year on the sickbed in the Xiushan Ward. He remembered Old Jacob, dying, pointing toward the wall, pointing twice, and himself thinking Old Jacob wanted water.

She watched him eat, her own half in her hand, uneaten. She said the lamp at the monastery gate was still lit; she'd gone to look once, the winter of the year she came back, at dusk; she stood over by the ward gate and saw the lamp lit at the monastery gate, and under the lamp a man standing who by his build looked like him; she stood a while, and came back. Yonan asked why she hadn't come in. She did not answer, but put down the half cake in her hand and said: "I've seen him." Yonan's hand stopped. She said he came to the West Market to give out gruel, pushing a cart, bringing a few people, setting up at the mouths of those streets on the north side, a bowl to each person; she'd watched from behind the crowd a good many times. She said he'd grown tall, taller than Master Yisi, and his eyes were like hers. Yonan said they were like hers. She nodded, and said that some days ago he had come to this shop once, in the evening, when the gong sounded, and stood before that board at the door a long while; she came out and asked whether he'd have a cake, and he bought one; she gave him a bowl of soup, and he sat at that table at the door and drank it, said thank you, and left. She said she knew then that he'd recognized that board.

When Yonan had heard it, he held that bowl of water and did not move. He thought of this evening in the hall, Jingjing standing behind him saying: the West Market, south side, the lane behind a sesame-cake shop; thought of turning his head and asking Jingjing why he didn't go, and Jingjing saying the accounts weren't finished. He put the bowl of water down; a little slopped out onto the table, and she wiped it up with the corner of her apron.

She asked him whether that child still drank cold water. Yonan said he didn't; Yisi saw to it, and he saw to it himself; in winter, writing in the west wing, even with the inkstone frozen, he'd run to the kitchen to pour hot water, and when he'd poured it, cut in a few slices of ginger. When she heard it she bowed her head and smiled, very quickly, and at once put the smile away, exactly as thirty years ago.

---

The gong on the market tower sounded.

The young man at the door was clearing the oven, stacking the leftover cakes one by one into the bamboo basket. Yonan stood up, and she stood up too and saw him to the door. He came to the small accounts board at the door, stopped, looked at it once more, right to left, line by line, and asked whether all these people paid. She said some paid and some didn't. He asked: and those who don't? She said: those who don't, when they come next time, she gives them cakes again. Yonan looked at her; she stood inside the door, flour all over her apron, many wrinkles in her face now, her eyes still dark. He said he would come again tomorrow; that porter's child in the lane to the south was still feverish, and he had to see to him. She said: then come and eat a cake. She paused, and said again: it was she who'd told the porter to go to the monastery about that child; she hadn't thought it would be he who came; she'd been thinking of someone else, someone who could treat the sick—she'd heard people in the ward say that one had made up medicines for some years with a fat elder at the monastery.

Yonan said: "It was he who sent me." She looked at him and nodded, and said: then go back; the ward gates will be closing.

By the time Yonan went out of the north gate of the West Market the gong had finished, and the ward drums had begun after it. He walked very fast; from the West Market to the Yining Ward was two wards; he went along the main street outside the ward walls, past the corner of the Liquan Ward as the drum beat its second round, and as it beat its third he reached the south gate of the Yining Ward, got into the Yining Ward before the ward gate closed, turned through that narrow stretch of street, and saw the monastery gate.

The lamp at the monastery gate was already lit. Jingjing had lit it; these years the lamp at the gate had always been claimed by Yonan in the evening; today Yonan was not there, so Jingjing brought out the oil jug, added oil, changed the wick, struck the slow-match, lit it, and when it was lit did not go in, but stood under the lamp waiting. He saw Yonan coming from the ward gate, stood up straight, said "Elder," and asked how the child was. Yonan said the fever was down a little; he'd go again tomorrow. Jingjing looked at him: on Yonan's cuff was a little flour, white, very plain to see under the lamp.

The two of them stood under the lamp, the ward drum beating round after round. Yonan said: "It was you who sent me." Jingjing said: "The child was feverish, and Elder Ishaq wasn't here." Yonan heard it and nodded, as though he had long foreseen this answer, and as though this answer were what he had been waiting for. He walked past Jingjing, in at the gate, went a few steps, stopped, turned his head, and said he'd go again tomorrow. Jingjing said good; tomorrow's gruel, he'd have someone take a jar to that porter's house. Yonan said she had soup there. Jingjing did not take up this, but turned aside and let Yonan go on to the back courtyard. He stood at the gate watching that back go into the dark; it was held ramrod straight, and at the corner of the gallery a hand came up and steadied itself once on a pillar, steadied once and let go.

That night Yonan washed his hands very slowly. He soaked them in the basin till the water was cold, and only then took them out. The little flour on his hands washed away.

Note: The closing of the West Market by three hundred strokes of the gong seven quarters before sunset follows the *Tang liudian* and *Tang huiyao* juan 86. Guo Ziyi's move from Hezhong to Binzhou in the fourth year of Dali follows *Zizhi tongjian* juan 224. The division of the realm into ten circuits in the early Tang appears in the *Tang liudian*. The Uighur envoys' coming out of the Court of State Ceremonial to plunder the wards and markets in the first month of the seventh year of Dali follows the *Jiu Tang shu*, "Annals of Daizong" and "Account of the Uighurs." The Daqin monastery at Zhouzhi follows the present Daqin Monastery pagoda at Zhouzhi, Shaanxi, and related studies; its Tang history is to be checked. The loss of Hexi: Liangzhou 764, Ganzhou and Suzhou around 766, Shazhou later. The years of the death of Patriarch Jacob II and the succession of Hnanisho II (Ningshu) are roughly 773–775, to be checked; the patriarch's residence at Baghdad was fixed only under Timothy I, and here it is said only that Hnanisho often went to Baghdad. A-Lian's work at the Mu family's sesame-cake shop in Luoyang, the inn at Xiangzhou, and the cake shop in the West Market are the novel's invention.
