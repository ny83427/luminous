# Volume Eleven　Huichang

## Chapter 71　Qushen

> Thereupon the emperor himself wrote the monastery's name-board, and the board bore the dragon's script.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

In the spring of the fourth year of Huichang a prophecy went round the city. It was said a palace Daoist had told the Son of Heaven: *While the fortunes of the eighteen-sons of Li are not yet spent, a Son of Heaven in black robes shall rule the realm.* The eighteen-sons of Li was the character *Li*—meaning the Son of Heaven's house; *black robes* meant those who wore black, and monks wore black; the Daoists said this meant that one day a man in black robes would come to sit in the Son of Heaven's seat. The West Market said that when the Son of Heaven heard this he could not sleep at night, and the next day sent down an edict: monks were not to walk in the streets; a monk leaving his monastery must first report to the monastery's Three Officers, the Three Officers to the inspectorate, and only with the inspectorate's leave might he go out; after the bell, none might spend the night at another monastery.

When the monastery's people heard it, they all looked at the robes they wore. The monastery's robes were black; from Elder Alopen's time they had been black; the brothers of the Patriarchate all wore black. Joseph said the brothers of the Patriarchate wore black because black hid dirt and wore well; it had nothing to do with the Tang Son of Heaven. Lingbao said: that prophecy speaks of monks; but when the Tang look at us, we're the same black as monks.

That year morning devotion numbered twenty; at the opening of spring An Yan died. He died in the first month; in the days before he died he still had himself helped in to stand morning devotion; the last day he did not come; when morning devotion broke up Jingfu went to see him; he lay on his place in the west wing with his eyes open, covered with an old outer robe whose collar was worn through—Jingjing's. He told Jingfu: that year the snow was heavy; the Great Worthy Jingjing came through the snow to the south of the city, stood below the well curb and said a few words, and when he'd said them turned and left, and didn't tell anyone to come back with him; he stood bare-chested in the snow watching the Great Worthy's back go toward the gate of the garden; he ran a few steps and caught up; the Great Worthy looked round and saw him, took off this robe, and put it round him. He said that all his life he'd been following that robe back. When he'd said it, he said he was cold; Jingfu put another quilt on him; he said it wasn't that kind of cold, and when he'd said it closed his eyes. Jingfu laid the old outer robe over him, and it was buried with him.

With An Yan dead, a place came empty on the register. Of the ten who had left, those who could return to the register had all returned; of the rest, some had married, some had homes outside, some had died; there was no one to come back and fill it; so the register had one more empty; Jingfu reported to the inspectorate; the inspectorate's officer ticked the roll, and did not ask who would fill it.

---

That summer the Son of Heaven sent down another edict, that throughout the empire the mountain cells, hermitages, common Buddha halls, charitable wells and village vegetarian halls—all without an authorized name-board and of fewer than two hundred bays—were to be demolished, and the monks and nuns in them ordered back to lay life and entered on the household registers. On the day the edict came down, people in the city said that the little hermitages in the Southern Mountains outside Chang'an, several hundred of them, were all to be torn down.

The inspectorate's men came to inspect the city in the seventh month. The Buddhist monasteries of the city mostly had name-boards, and those with name-boards were not demolished; what was demolished were the little Buddha halls that families in the wards had built themselves, the small shrines with a stone Buddha at the mouths of lanes, the charitable wells with a little hut by the well for chanting scripture. The inspectorate sent several parties, and they inspected ward by ward. The day they came to Yining Ward it was an old palace eunuch who came, past sixty, thin, a little hunchbacked, in a green robe, riding a donkey, followed by two clerks and an officer, looking as he went; at whatever doorway had a Buddha shrine he had the clerks note it down, and when he had looked over one street he looked over the next.

He came along the ward street to the monastery gate; the gruel shed's pot was open, Jingfu by the pot ladling gruel, the queue reaching from the pot to the corner of the ward street. The old eunuch, on his donkey, looked at the pot and at the queue, stopped his donkey, and asked Jingfu: this gruel shed—how many years has it been open? Jingfu said since the Dali years—first in the West Market, moved to the ward street in the fourth year of Zhenyuan—more than seventy years. The old eunuch asked again: how many pots a day? Jingfu said two formerly; since the year before last, one. The old eunuch nodded, and looked at the board over the monastery's gate; the wood was old, the gold of the three characters *Daqin Monastery* had flaked; the old people of the ward said a Son of Heaven had bestowed a board in the Tianbao years; which board it was, and whether the one hanging now was that one, no one could say any more. He asked: does this monastery have a name-board? Jingfu said it did; the name was changed in the Tianbao years, by edict. The old eunuch said: with a name-board, it isn't demolished; he set the donkey going and went off toward the east end of the ward.

Jingfu asked Headman Liu of the ward afterwards who the old eunuch was. Headman Liu said he was an old eunuch of the Commissioners of Merit's inspectorate of the Right Street, surnamed Yang, who had been in the inspectorate decades; he didn't talk much; he was in charge of inspections; when palace people saw him, they all called him Old Yang.

---

The news from Zhouzhi came in the eighth month. In a ravine in the mountains south of Zhouzhi county lived those seven households baptized by Elder Ishaq in the Dali years; the seven had since grown, to a dozen-odd households; in the ravine they had built three earthen rooms, with a blue brick set in the lintel carved with a cross; at the offering once in seven they worshipped in the middle room; the Chang'an monastery sent someone once a year, with bread and scriptures; while Jingjing lived he sent Lingbao, and later Jingfu went. The inspectorate's men came to Zhouzhi to inspect, came to that ravine, saw those three earthen rooms and asked what they were; the mountain people said they were rooms for worship; the inspectorate's men said: rooms for worship are vegetarian halls; no authorized name-board; demolish. They were demolished, and that blue brick carved with a cross was prised from the lintel and thrown into the ravine.

A young man of those households ran to Chang'an to bring word. He ran two days; the straw sandals on his feet were worn through; at the monastery gate he knelt before Jingfu and said the rooms were gone; the cross brick had been thrown away; at night they went down into the ravine to look for it, looked all night, found it; the brick had broken off a corner in the fall; the cross was still there; they stood the brick at the foot of an old oak at the village entrance, steadied it with stones, and at the offering once in seven the dozen-odd households knelt under that tree. He said: Leader, from now on, if we worship under the tree, does it count as worship?

Jingfu said it counted. He said Aluohe does not live in rooms; the rooms are demolished, and Aluohe is still there; kneeling under the tree, you're worshipping. He said he'd come at the end of the month. The young man asked: from now on, will the ones demolished include the monasteries in Chang'an? Jingfu said the Chang'an monastery had a name-board, and those with name-boards weren't demolished; the young man said he'd heard the inspectorate's men say that with name-boards it wasn't certain either.

Before Jingfu went to Zhouzhi, he discussed the bread with Lingbao in the small room in the back courtyard. He said: those dozen-odd households at Zhouzhi used to have one offering once in seven a year when they received bread—the time the monastery sent someone; the one sent was a priest; Uncle Lingbao went; the bread was blessed in their room and broken in that room. Now Uncle Lingbao couldn't walk a hundred-odd *li* of mountain road; if he went himself he couldn't bless; going, he could only lead them in reciting scripture, and when they came to the offering bread, stop, empty-handed. He said he wanted to take bread: blessed in the hall at Chang'an, and he would carry it.

Lingbao, in the corner, did not nod at once. He said: the offering bread does not leave the hall door; this article Brother set in the sixth year of Zhenyuan, after that Kang child died; for more than fifty years it has never once been broken. He said Brother set this article for fear people would carry the bread home as medicine, as a charm, soak it in well water for a child to drink; if you take bread to Zhouzhi, you take it to the place where they worship; their room of worship is demolished; under the oak is their hall; he can see the reason; but the reason he can see isn't written in the rule Brother set. He said he couldn't change this article for Brother.

Old Mother Kang was sitting at the door of the small room that day waiting for He Siniang to go back to the south end of the ward together; she heard, stood up on her bamboo stick, stood in the doorway, and said she'd put in a word. She said: that rule was set because of her; she carried the bread home, soaked it in well water, the child died, and Great Worthy Jing set it; these fifty-odd years, every offering once in seven when she received the bread, she ate it on the spot, and never once carried it out. She said: Great Worthy, ask her, and she says take it. She said: when she carried the bread home she wanted her child to live; those people at Zhouzhi want the bread so they themselves will know they're still of Aluohe's household; the two aren't the same; if Great Worthy Jing were here, he too would see that the two aren't the same.

Lingbao, sitting in the corner, reached out and touched the glass bowl on the table, touched its rim, and said: then take it; he'll enter it in the accounts: *Eighth month of the fourth year of Huichang: the bread leaves the hall door, to Zhouzhi, carried by the leader Jingfu; the old woman of the Kang family thought it right; the priest Lingbao consented.* He said: entered in the accounts, if anyone later says this rule was broken, turn up the accounts and they'll know how it was broken, why it was broken, and who nodded.

Jingfu went out of the city on the twenty-sixth of the eighth month. He carried a bundle on his back; in the bundle was a little clay jar, its mouth sealed with wax, and in the jar the bread Lingbao had blessed at the offering once in seven the day before, broken into twenty-odd pieces, and a little bottle of wine and a few rolls of scripture; he took the road he had taken a dozen-odd times—out of the Yanping Gate, southwest, across the Feng river, past Hu county, to the county town of Zhouzhi, then south into the mountains; the mountain path was narrow, a slope on one side and a ravine on the other; he walked two days, and two blisters rose on the soles of his feet. That ravine he knew; in other years when he came he could see the roofs of the three earthen rooms from far off; this time he reached the ravine's mouth and saw an empty patch of ground, with heaps of earth where mud walls had fallen, overgrown with grass, and a few half-burned rafters thrown beside the heaps. The old oak at the village entrance was still there, and at its foot stood that blue brick, a corner broken off; before the brick were set a few clay bowls with wildflowers in them.

The dozen-odd households all came. Old and young; grandsons and great-grandsons of those seven households Elder Ishaq baptized; some spoke Tang thickly and knew not one word of Syriac, only the one line *Lord, have mercy*, said as four Tang words. Jingfu stood under the oak, and they knelt at the oak's foot, before that blue brick; Jingfu led them in reciting scripture, the Tang scriptures his Master had translated, and when they came to the offering bread he opened the clay jar, took out the bread piece by piece, and put it in a clay bowl. He told them: this bread was blessed in the hall at Chang'an, blessed by the Great Worthy Lingbao; he himself has never had hands laid on him; he cannot bless; he has only brought it; he brought it so you would know that in the hall at Chang'an, at the offering once in seven, there's a portion for you. When he had said it, he handed it round one by one; when he reached an old woman, she took the bread and held it in her palm and did not eat it, and looked up and asked him: is the hall at Chang'an still there? Jingfu said it was. The old woman put the bread in her mouth, chewed, swallowed, and told him: then that's good; their hall is gone; if the hall at Chang'an is still there, that's good.

That day he stayed the night in the ravine, sleeping in a family's kitchen. In the night the wind rose, and he heard the oak's leaves rustling all night. The next day he started back; at the ravine's mouth he looked back; the wildflowers in those few clay bowls had been blown over by the wind, and a little child ran over and set the flowers upright stem by stem.

---

Qushen left that autumn. He was forty-three that year, and since returning to the register had lived fourteen more years in the monastery. His temper was hasty; Jingjing had given him this name—put away excess, put away extravagance, put away extremes—and he remembered it all his life, and his temper was still hasty, and when he was in a hurry his words came out like beans pouring from a sack. These years he had read the accounts for Lingbao in the scripture room and copied scriptures for the monastery; the Tang-language scriptures Jingjing had translated long ago—the *Sutra of Profound Peace and Joy*, the *Scripture on Proclaiming the Origin*, the *Hymn of the Three Majesties*, and a few more—all their copies were in his hand; the rolls in the scripture room were too old, so he copied a new set; the old were put away and the new brought out for use. He copied well, his hand upright, and beside every borrowed character he put the little dot as in the original, not one missed.

Lu Shouchong came to the monastery several times that year. The first time he came with the rope to measure the land; later when he came, it was to look for Qushen. He said he'd heard people in the temple say the Daqin Monastery had a copyist who wrote a fine hand and had read the *Laozi*; he'd like to ask him to the temple to copy a scripture. Qushen went, copied half a month, came back, and told Jingfu: beside the scripture room in the temple he'd seen the Daoist Lu's register from measuring the land; in the register was drawn a map of Yining Ward, and the plot of the Daqin Monastery was circled in vermilion, with *hall, so many bays; courtyards, so many mu; well, one* written beside it; he didn't turn its pages; the register lay open on the table, and when he looked up from copying he could see it. He said too that of the scriptures in the Tongzhen Temple, half he could understand and half he couldn't; the half he could understand had many characters the same as the scriptures the Great Worthy Jingjing translated—non-action, without desire, pure and still, profound and wondrous; copying, he felt as though he were copying his own house's scriptures. Jingfu said those characters were borrowed; when Master borrowed them he put dots beside them. Qushen said he knew; but copying in the temple, there were no dots beside the temple's scriptures; those characters lived in their own house there, lived securely; their scriptures were their own words.

That year the things he said to people got shorter year by year. Once, reading the accounts, he would argue with Lingbao as he read—this entry's wrong, that entry's too detailed; now when he had read them he closed the account book and left. The day An Yan died he stood at the door of the west wing and did not go in; the day the edict on demolishing hermitages came down, he sat alone in the scripture room all night. Jingfu asked him once; he said he was thinking: Uncle An Yan kept watch all his life and died on his place in the west wing; of those on the monastery's register, each year one fewer; the Buddhist monasteries in Chang'an were being torn down, the rooms at Zhouzhi had been torn down; when the day came that even those with name-boards weren't certain—of the people left in this monastery, how many would remain, and where would those who remained go? He said he was forty-three, and he didn't know where he would go.

One day in the tenth month, Qushen left. He left at night; Salang, keeping watch inside the gate, saw him come out with a bundle on his back and asked where he was going; he said: to the Tongzhen Temple; the Daoist Lu's place is short of a copyist; board and lodging provided. Salang asked whether he'd come back; he stood beside the lamp and said he didn't know. Salang did not stop him. He said his grandfather had told him: keeping the gate is keeping those outside from coming in; when those inside want to go out, a gate can't hold them. After that, at every offering once in seven, Salang keeping watch inside the gate often saw a man standing at the foot of the ward wall of Jincheng Ward across the ward street, in a grey cloth Daoist robe, both hands folded in his sleeves, looking toward the monastery gate; when the singing in the hall was done and people scattered, he left. Salang told Jingfu; Jingfu said he knew who it was; he said: if one day that man crosses the street, don't stop him, and don't call him; let him walk up to the lamp by himself.

The next day at light, Jingfu went into the scripture room and saw the scripture cabinet open and its top shelf empty. That shelf held the Tang-language scriptures Qushen had copied—the *Sutra of Profound Peace and Joy*, the *Scripture on Proclaiming the Origin*, the *Hymn of the Three Majesties*, and several more of Jingjing's translations—all newly copied, eleven rolls in all. The old rolls were still there on the shelf below, wrapped in cloth—Jingjing's own hand from long ago; Qushen had not touched them. On the cabinet door lay a sheet of paper weighted down, in Qushen's hand, upright regular script: *The old rolls stay with the monastery; the new rolls go with me. The scriptures are the monastery's; the copying was my hand. I take them—not to sell, not to burn, not to give to anyone. Later, if the monastery needs them, send someone to fetch them.* At the end of the paper he had written his own name, *Qushen*, and when he had written it, put a dot beside it.

Lingbao had He Siniang help him in. Jingfu read him the paper, and when it was read Lingbao stood before the cabinet and reached up to the top shelf, touched the empty board, touched it once, and again. He said: eleven rolls. Jingfu said: eleven rolls. Lingbao said: Qushen copied scripture and didn't miss one dot; he remembered. He said: Qushen's going is his own affair; the scriptures he took, Qushen copied; the old rolls are still here; of the scriptures in the scripture room, not one is missing; only one set of copies is missing. He said: in the monastery's accounts, he'll enter it. Jingfu asked how. Lingbao said: enter it as it is: *Tenth month of the fourth year of Huichang: Qushen leaves the monastery, to the Tongzhen Temple, taking eleven newly copied rolls of Tang-language scripture; the old rolls stay with the monastery.* He said: another place empty on the register. The next day Jingfu went himself to the inspectorate to report Qushen's leaving; the officer asked where he'd gone; Jingfu said to the Tongzhen Temple in Jincheng Ward; the officer looked up at him once and said: your monastery's people go to Daoist temples now, do they; Jingfu said yes; the officer ticked the roll and asked nothing more.

Joseph said Qushen had gone over to the Daoists. Lingbao said what he'd said was that he'd gone to copy books. Joseph said: copying books till he's in a Daoist temple, living in a Daoist temple—if that isn't going over, what is? Lingbao said whether he went over or not, he couldn't see; he knew only that when Qushen left, what he took was our scriptures; of the Daoists' scriptures he took not one roll; and on the paper he left, beside the last character, he put a dot.

---

That evening Jingfu was alone in the scripture room. He wiped the empty board of the top shelf clean, wiped it several times, wiped it till it shone; when he had wiped it, he did not move the old rolls up from the shelf below, nor put anything else in; he shut the cabinet door and did not lock it.

---

Note: In the fourth year of Huichang (844) Daoists presented the prophecy "While the fortunes of the eighteen-sons of Li are not yet spent, a Son of Heaven in black robes shall rule the realm," and Wuzong thereafter distrusted monks; in the same year an edict ordered the demolition throughout the empire of mountain cells, hermitages, common Buddha halls, charitable wells and village vegetarian halls of fewer than two hundred bays and without authorized name-boards, the monks and nuns to be returned to lay life; see Ennin's *Record of a Pilgrimage to China in Search of the Law*, book 4. The Daqin Monastery's name-board was written by Xuanzong; the stele says "the emperor himself wrote the monastery's name-board, and the board bore the dragon's script." Old Yang (Yang Shousu), Qushen and the Tongzhen Temple are the novel's invention. For the seven households of Zhouzhi see Chapter 39.
