# Volume Nine　The Translation Hall

## Chapter 58　Timothy

> When the virtue of Zhou declined, the blue chariot ascended into the west; now that the Way of the great Tang shines, the Luminous wind blows east.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

In the autumn of the second year of Yuanhe, Jingfu broke wind at the morning office.

He was twelve that year and had already shot up to Jingjing's shoulder. The lamps were lit in the hall, the monastery's people knelt in three rows singing the office, and a long chant had just ended and the next not yet begun, and the hall was quiet for just that moment. In just that moment there came a *pff*—very loud, very long, with a turn in it, and a little tail of an echo. A few children in the front row broke first, their shoulders twitching; the old believers in the back row buried their heads lower, and one of them didn't manage it and let a little snort escape and hastily clapped his sleeve over his mouth. Siyuan, kneeling beside Jingfu, stared straight ahead, his face without a trace of colour, but his ears red. Jingjing stood at the front with his back to everyone, paused a moment, raised the tune of the next chant, and sang on. The others sang after him, ragged for a few lines, before they came together.

When the office broke up the children swarmed out of the hall door. A bigger boy running at the back made faces at Jingfu, pinched his nose, and said, "Iron Belly sprang a leak!" Jingfu went scarlet at once. "It wasn't me! It was Brother Siyuan!" Siyuan was just coming out of the hall and heard it. His step faltered; he looked at Jingfu once, bowed his head, and walked off.

That noon Jingjing called Jingfu to his room. Jingfu stood by the door with his hands behind his back and his eyes on the floor. Jingjing took two bowls from the cupboard, his own and the one Jingfu used every day, Pa's, set them on the table, poured a little hot water into each, and told him to sit.

"Let me tell you something from when I was small," said Jingjing. "The year I was ten, in Lingzhou, in the second month, a guest came to our house, an old soldier Lord Guo sent to fetch Pa, with a sword scar on his face, so that when he laughed only half of it moved. Pa and I sat with him at a meal—a basin of mutton broth, a dish of flatbread, a dish of ginger—and in the middle of eating I broke wind, louder than you did today. The night before I'd secretly drunk cold water, and my stomach was bad. That old soldier sat right across from me and heard it plain as plain, slapped his thigh and laughed, and said there'd been a muffled thunderclap under the table, and tomorrow I should go to the camp and beat the drum."

Jingfu's eyes came up and did not go down again. "Master breaks wind too?"

"I do. More than you."

The corner of Jingfu's mouth moved, and he hastily pressed his lips together.

"My face was red then too, and I said to Pa, Pa, it's shameful. Pa first made me eat ginger, and then said: you did it, so you did it; you didn't lay it on anyone else; what's shameful in that? What shames a man is a lie, not wind."

Jingfu's smile stopped. Jingjing said nothing more, but picked up his own bowl of hot water and drank a slow mouthful. What Pa had told him that last night in the dialect of Balkh, he did not tell Jingfu. Jingfu was twelve; he didn't need to know so much. He only needed to know that one sentence.

Jingfu sat with his head bowed, looking at the hot water in his bowl. After a few breaths he stood and went out. At the door he turned his head back. "Master, that old soldier—did he laugh at you afterwards?"

"No. In all his life he never mentioned it again. Later he saw me over the Pamirs to Ctesiphon, and walked back alone, and put my diary into Pa's hands."

Jingfu nodded and ran out. Through the wall Jingjing heard him run as far as the scripture room, stop, and say loudly, "Brother Siyuan, it was me!"

---

That winter Gabriel often sat in the sun in the gallery of the back courtyard. The burn on his left cheek had puckered into a knot with age, and his voice had been gone for years; he could no longer tell stories. The ward's children weren't afraid of that face, and after their lessons sat round him, Jingfu among them.

One day the children pestered him for a story, and Gabriel could not hold out. He took Jingfu's hand and in Syriac told, word by word, the story of the prodigal: the younger son spent all he had, ate the husks the swine ate in the sty, came to himself, and arose and went to his father. When he reached *But when he was yet a great way off, his father saw him*, his voice gave out entirely; he opened his mouth, and only a little breath came.

The children all looked at him. Jingfu waited a moment, then took it up and went on in Tang: "…his Pa saw him, and his heart went soft, and he ran to him and hugged his neck and kissed him and kissed him. And the younger son said, Pa, I've sinned against heaven and against you; I'm not fit to be called your son any more. His Pa paid him no heed, and turned round and called to the people of the house: quick, bring the best robe and put it on him, bring a ring and put it on his hand, and kill that calf we've been fattening, and let's eat and be glad! For this son of mine was dead and is alive again, was lost and is found."

He had studied Syriac seven years and still couldn't get it right, but his Tang was better than anyone's, and when he got to killing the calf the children all laughed. Gabriel leaned against the gallery pillar with his eyes narrowed, listening, nodding at each line, and the burn on his face seemed to smooth out a little.

Before that winter was over, Gabriel died. When Jingjing said the names before light, he said one more: *Gabriel*.

---

In the spring of the third year of Yuanhe a letter came from the Patriarchate.

It came with an Uighur caravan. The road through Hexi had been cut more than twenty years before; the Tibetans held Liangzhou, Ganzhou and Shazhou. Now, going west from Chang'an, you had first to go north, into the Uighurs' country, then west round the Tianshan, before you could join the road to the Two Rivers; so this letter had been nearly two years on the way. It was written on parchment, folded into a long strip, tied with fine cord, the knot sealed with black wax, and the patriarch's seal pressed into the wax. The writer was Timothy.

This patriarch had sat on his throne nearly thirty years. Merchants from the west said he was very learned, that he had disputed about the Way before the caliph for two days and the caliph had not said he was wrong; they said too that his arm was long: he had sent a metropolitan north to the Turks, and sent men toward Tibet as well. In the second year of Jianzhong Jingjing had written him a letter telling him that Hnanisho's name was carved on the stele and the characters on the stele would not be changed; in the twenty-odd years since, there had been no reply.

This letter was very courteous, and inside the courtesy there was something very hard. It said that for many years the Patriarchate had had no sure report from the East; of what the caravans brought, little was true and much false. Of the stele set up in Chang'an, with his predecessor's name carved on it, he had learned, and there the matter rested. Then it came to business. The province of Sina had had its metropolitan's seat empty for decades, and the Patriarchate could no longer leave it empty. He had already consecrated a man, named David, of Merv, and meant to send him by sea, on a *hu* merchant's ship to Guangzhou, and from Guangzhou north; whether he would arrive, he dared not say. When David arrived, those in Chang'an who should have hands laid on them, he would lay hands on; the matters in Chang'an that should be settled, he would settle together with the one who directed affairs in Chang'an. If the one who directed affairs in Chang'an could make the journey to the Patriarchate and set things out face to face, that would be good too. Either would do; let the East choose for itself.

At the end of the letter Timothy wrote a sentence that had nothing to do with business: "I hear that the Patriarchate once had an archdeacon who used to ask everyone who came from the east: is the road still open? When I took office I went through his old files, and found that on the margin of a page he had written a child's name. That child, I think, must be old by now."

Jingjing put the letter down.

That night he did not sleep. The next morning, standing in the middle of the courtyard, he told the knots twice, and at the second time, before the sky had greyed, he wanted to go to the Patriarchate. Once the thought arose it would not be pressed down. He was sixty-two. He had been to the Patriarchate twice in his life. The first time at ten, on a camel's back, tied to the pack frame with rope by Xu Jing. The second time at fifteen; that time his uncle asked him, is the road still open, and he said, it's open, I've walked it once. That summer his uncle died in the room beside the storehouse, his left hand gripping Jingjing's wrist. His uncle was buried at Ctesiphon. Now the Patriarchate had moved to Baghdad, and whether anyone still went to pull the weeds on that grave at Ctesiphon, he did not know. He wanted to go and see that grave once. He wanted to stand before Timothy and tell him, one thing after another, what had happened in the East these fifty years.

First he went to Li Su. Li Su was past sixty that year, had been an official at the Astronomical Bureau for decades, and was still the same: thin, slow of speech, reckoning everything on his fingers. Jingjing showed him the letter. When Li Su had read it he asked, "You want to go?"

"I want to."

Li Su neither said yes nor said no. He got up, pulled a roll of maps out of a pile of books, spread it on the floor, and weighted it with two paperweights. The map showed mountains, rivers, city after city, with names noted beside the cities in both Tang and *hu* letters. He put his finger on the map and spoke of it place by place. "Hexi is the Tibetans'. In Beiting these ten-odd years the Uighurs and the Tibetans have fought several wars, and the cities have changed masters several times. Going west, you can only keep close along the Uighurs' country, go as far as Suyab, and then turn south. All along the road there are Uighurs, Qarluqs, Tibetans, Arabs, and which stretch belongs to whom is different this year from next."

Jingjing crouched at the edge of the map and looked. Some of the place names he knew. He had walked them at ten, and again at fifteen, and in those days these places all flew Tang flags.

"A year going, a year coming back," said Li Su. "You're sixty-two." As he said it his fingers were still reckoning, as if he were working out an account.

"My Pa was fifty-nine when he went."

Li Su glanced at him and rolled up the map. "Then you've already lived three years longer."

Neither of them laughed.

He went back to the monastery, called Lingbao and Siyuan, and told them.

Siyuan spoke first. "Master, Hexi can't be got through; going round by the north takes a year. The water in the north is all cold."

Lingbao was past forty. The accounts were in his hands and the keys at his waist. He looked at Jingjing, and after a few breaths said only one thing: "If Brother goes, I'll keep things here."

Jingfu had heard from outside the door. He was thirteen. He stood in the doorway with one hand on the frame. "I'll go with Master!"

"You won't."

"Why not?"

"If you go, who'll light the lamp?"

Jingfu opened his mouth and said nothing.

Jingjing packed all the same. He sent Siyuan to the West Market to find the Uighur caravan, which was going back in the autumn and could take people. He laid out on the bed one by one the things he would take: two robes to change into, a roll of scripture, a packet of ginger, a packet of *harītakī*, a stack of paper, a brush, a stick of ink. He wrote a reply to Timothy that he meant to deliver in person, a dozen-odd pages. He wrote how many were in the congregation of the Chang'an monastery, how much property the monastery had, how many scrolls, how its dealings with the government went; he wrote of Lord Guo, of Pa, of the stele, of Prajna, of Official Pei, of Narsai's cart, of Baaluo. When he came to the affair of the second year of Zhenyuan he stopped his brush, thought, and wrote it in after all: *There is a government document which says your servant's principles are obscure and his wording loose.* When he had written it he looked at it himself and smiled once.

Those mornings the character on his board was *rao*, to go round. He was reading how Israel came out of Egypt, and God led them not through the way of the land of the Philistines, although that was near, but led them about, through the way of the wilderness.

---

On the day the caravan left, before light, Jingjing went out of the monastery gate. Jingfu wanted to see him off, and he would not let him. "Stay at home and lead the morning office." Jingfu stood by the stone block at the monastery gate, holding the lamp not yet blown out, and said nothing more.

Siyuan led the camel for him all the way to the Kaiyuan Gate. The gate passage was very deep, and walking in it, their footsteps boomed. Outside the gate a great road went west, stretching to where you could no longer see it. The Uighur caravan was already waiting outside the gate, the camels kneeling on the ground one after another, the packs not all loaded yet.

Jingjing stood in the gate passage and did not walk out. He remembered asking Pa, the year he was ten, why he wouldn't go with him to the Patriarchate, and Pa saying that Lord Guo wanted him to watch the road; if he went, there'd be no one to watch it. He hadn't understood then. He had thought the road Pa meant was the road from Chang'an to Ctesiphon. He stood in the gate passage until the caravan's headman had come twice to say it was time to go, and Siyuan beside him, holding the camel, never made a sound.

Then he turned, took from his breast the letter of a dozen-odd pages, and handed it to the caravan's headman. "Take this letter to Baghdad and give it to the patriarch."

The headman took it and weighed it in his hand. "And the man?"

"The man isn't going."

The headman looked at him, probably thought this old *hu* monk a bit odd, asked nothing more, and stuffed the letter into a pack.

When he got back to the monastery it was already noon. He opened the bundle and put everything back in its place one by one. Last of all there was the packet of ginger left. He carried it to the stove and put it on the stove ledge. Later he sent a second letter after the first by the next caravan, very short, only a few lines: *The one who directs affairs in the Chang'an monastery cannot come. If the Metropolitan David arrives, Chang'an awaits him. The affairs of the East are set out in the previous letter. The patriarch asks: is the road still open? The road is still open; only one has to go round. Jingjing, at Chang'an.*

---

David arrived in the twelfth month of the fourth year of Yuanhe. He had drifted nine months at sea on a *hu* merchant's ship, been ill half a year in Guangzhou, and gone north from Guangzhou, over the Dayu Ridge, through Hongzhou, through Xiangzhou—the very road Baaluo had walked back from Lingnan that year. He was past sixty, tall but bent in the back, his beard snow-white, his eyes grey, like a winter sky; Tang he had not a word of. With him had come two young monks, one named Joseph and one named Elijah, both of Merv, in their early twenties, who had held him up all the way and, in the half year he was ill at Guangzhou, taken turns watching over him.

He came in at the monastery gate and first stopped before the stele in the front courtyard, looked at the cross at its head, looked at the Syriac below, and when he came to the line at the foot of the stele—*Yazdbozid, priest and chorepiscopus of Kumdan the royal city*—asked in Syriac: "Where is this man?"

"He died twenty-five years ago," said Jingjing. "He was my Pa."

David looked at him, and looked at the stele again. "Chorepiscopus, it says. And the laying on of hands?"

"The laying on of hands was never done. Chang'an has no bishop; there's no one who could lay hands."

David said nothing more.

On the third day after he arrived, in the little room where business was discussed, before Lingbao, Siyuan, Sabrisho and a few others, he told Jingjing his conditions. He spoke very slowly, putting out the Syriac word by word, as if he had thought it all through long since on the ship.

"The patriarch sent me first to lay on hands, and second to see the monastery in Chang'an. What the Patriarchate has heard these years is that the monastery in Chang'an gives way to the Tang, gives way too much: in the hall they sing Tang songs, in the scriptures they write the words of Buddhists and Daoists, on the stele they venerate the Son of Heaven. The stele I'm not concerned with; the stele was approved by my predecessor's patriarch. The songs in the hall and the words in the scriptures I am concerned with. Before the laying on of hands, the hymn in Tang is to stop in the hall, and everything is to be sung in Syriac. The Tang scriptures in the scripture room, wherever they borrow another house's words, are all to be changed back to transliteration: Aluohe is Aluohe, Mishihe is Mishihe; Heavenly Worthy, non-action, wondrous being—not one is to remain. If these two things are done, I'll lay on hands. If they can't be done, I'll live in the side courtyard and wait."

Sabrisho, listening, had his beard jutting up and down; he probably agreed in his heart and could not well say so aloud. Lingbao looked at Jingjing.

Jingjing thought about it all night. The next morning the character on his board was *rang*, to yield. He was reading that the Lord, being in the form of God, thought it not robbery to be equal with God, but made himself of no reputation, and took upon him the form of a servant. He went to the side courtyard and said to David:

"The hymn in Tang I'll stop for a month, and for a month the hall will sing only Syriac. The words in the scripture room I can't change. Dozens of scriptures, changing them back to transliteration word by word—at my age, I could change till I died and not finish. I'll give the Elder the key of the scripture room. When the Elder has looked, whichever scripture he wants changed, I'll sit with him and change it."

"And after a month?"

"After a month, the Elder will see for himself."

For that month the hall sang only Syriac. At the first offering once in seven, the old believers of the ward sang along, couldn't manage it, their mouths open and unable to follow the tune. At the second, half the back rows were empty. At the third, of the seven households come back from the south of the city, three didn't come; An Yan went to ask, and they said they couldn't understand, kneeling there was like being a guest in someone else's house. The old woman who sold sesame cakes in the South Lane of the West Market came and knelt in the last row, couldn't follow one line, and when worship was over stood up and asked Lingbao, "That one we used to sing, *In the highest heavens, deep reverence and praise*—I've sung it thirty years. Now we're not allowed to sing it?"

"It's the elder from the Patriarchate who won't allow it."

"Where's the Patriarchate?"

Lingbao couldn't answer.

At every offering once in seven David stood at the front and watched the back rows empty, row by row. He had no Tang, but empty benches he could read. At the fourth offering once in seven, when worship was over, he sent for Jingjing to the side courtyard, sat on the folding chair Pa had sat on in his day, and said, "Next time, sing it back again."

"The Elder has seen."

"I've seen," said David. "Before I came, people at the Patriarchate told me the monastery in Chang'an gives way to the Tang. I've been here a month, and what I've seen is that the Tang, in this monastery, are living in their own house. I took the Tang speech away, and they became guests."

---

The laying on of hands was done in the second month of the fifth year of Yuanhe, on a day of the offering once in seven. The hall sang the hymn in Tang again, and the hall was packed; the households come back from the south of the city came too. An Yan stood at the front, and Jingfu stood beside Lingbao. By the rule, three bishops must lay hands together to consecrate a bishop. David was only one. He took from his breast a letter in Timothy's own hand and had Jingjing read it to everyone: *The East is far, and three bishops cannot be had; let David lay hands alone, and the Patriarchate will acknowledge it.*

When Jingjing had read it, he knelt in the middle of the hall, and David stood before him and pressed both hands on his head. Those two hands were very large, very cold, and pressed very heavily. David recited in Syriac the passage for the consecration of a bishop, recited it very slowly, and at the end his hands were still pressing and did not lift. Jingjing, kneeling there, felt the weight of those two hands go all the way down to his knees. He remembered Pa saying, that last night, from now on all of this is yours; remembered the sleeve knot his uncle tied with his left hand; remembered Yonan. Yonan too had knelt in this hall that year, and someone had pressed his hands on his head, and later he broke his vow. David lifted his hands and bade him rise. Then it was Lingbao: David laid hands on him to make him a priest, and as Lingbao knelt, Jingjing saw his shoulders trembling.

David lived in Chang'an a year, in the room in the side courtyard where Pa had lived, and every day went with Jingjing to the hall, to the scripture room, to the gruel shed in the ward street. He had Jingjing translate the Tang scriptures for him sentence by sentence. When he heard *Heavenly Worthy*, heard *non-action*, heard *wondrous being*, his brows knitted tighter and tighter, and in the end he pointed to those small dots and asked what they were.

"Those are my marks. Wherever I borrowed the words of Buddhists and Daoists, I put a dot."

David took the roll and turned it page by page, and in the end could not count them, and put it down. "You've put so many."

"I've been putting them thirty years."

That autumn David went with Siyuan once on the round of the cart for the dead. He couldn't push it, but walked holding the shafts, out beyond the Kaiyuan Gate, and watched Siyuan and two young fellows lift an old man frozen to death off the cart and bury him. He stood in the burial ground looking at those rows of stakes—one row, two rows, three rows, four, stretching away to the foot of the earth slope in the distance—and asked, "How many people is this?"

"I've never counted. It's in Lingbao's accounts. Some thousands."

David asked nothing else. That day, when he got back, he wrote a letter to Timothy in the side courtyard, wrote for two days, and when he had finished sent for Jingjing. "This letter—you're not to read it. What I've written is the affairs of Chang'an; what I've seen, I've written." He kept the letter under his hand and said, "The Patriarchate sent me to take things in hand. I've been here a year and looked for a year, and I can't take them in hand, and I don't want to any more. I'm old. I can't read Chang'an; you can. Go on as you have."

In the twelfth month of that year David fell ill in that room in the side courtyard. He had Jingjing give him the last communion, using the glass bowl. The bowl was ground by Sasanian hands; he knew that kind of circular facet, held it a while looking at it, and said there were bowls like this in Merv too. After he died, Jingjing sent the letter he had written to Timothy with the next Uighur caravan. What was in it, Jingjing never knew.

That year the character Jingjing wrote many times over on his board was *shou*, hand. He was reading what the Dharma King Baolu wrote to his disciple Timothy: *stir up the gift of God, which is in thee by the putting on of my hands.*

---

Note: Timothy I (Timothy I, r. 780–823) was patriarch of the Church of the East; his letters mention sending metropolitans to the Turks, Tibet and elsewhere; Thomas of Marga's *Book of Governors* records that he consecrated one David as "metropolitan of Sina." David's coming east to Chang'an and his laying hands on Jingjing are the novel's invention. The prefectures of Hexi had fallen to the Tibetans before the early Zhenyuan years.
