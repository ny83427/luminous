# Volume Nine　The Translation Hall

## Chapter 58　Timothy

> When the virtue of Zhou declined, the blue chariot ascended into the west; now that the Way of the great Tang shines, the Luminous wind blows east.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

In the autumn of the second year of Yuanhe, Jingfu broke wind at the morning office. He was twelve that year, and had already shot up to Jingjing's shoulder; the lamps were lit in the hall, the monastery's people knelt in three rows, chanting; a long melody had just closed, the next had not yet begun, and the hall was still for that one moment, and in that moment—*pfft*—very loud, very long, turning a corner, with a little echo after. A few children in the front row broke first, their shoulders twitching; the old believers at the back buried their heads lower; one did not bury his in time, and a snort leaked out, and he hastily covered his mouth with his sleeve. Siyuan, kneeling beside Jingfu, looked straight ahead, not a trace of colour on his face, but his ears went red. Jingjing stood at the front, his back to them all; he paused a moment, raised the tune of the next passage, and chanted on, and everyone chanted after him, a few lines ragged before they were together again. When the office broke up, the children rushed out of the hall door in a swarm; one bigger child running behind made faces at Jingfu, pinched his nose, and said: the iron belly's sprung a leak; Jingfu's face went red all at once, and he said: it wasn't me; it was Brother Siyuan. Siyuan was just coming out of the hall, heard this, faltered a step, looked at Jingfu once, and walked on with his head down.

That day at the hour of *wu* Jingjing called Jingfu into his room. Jingfu stood by the door, both hands behind his back, eyes on the floor. Jingjing took two bowls from the cupboard, one his own and one his Pa's that Jingfu used every day, set them on the table, poured a little hot water into each, and told him to sit. He said he'd tell you something from when he was small. He said the year he was ten, in Lingzhou, in the second month, a guest came to the house: an old soldier Lord Guo sent to fetch his Pa, a sword scar across his face, who when he smiled moved only half his face; he and his Pa ate with him: a pot of mutton broth, a dish of cakes, a dish of ginger; in the middle of eating he broke wind, louder than yours today; he'd secretly drunk cold water the night before and his stomach was bad; that old soldier sat right across from him and heard it plainly, slapped his thigh laughing, and said: a muffled thunderclap under the table—tomorrow come to camp and beat the drum. Jingfu's eyes came up, and did not go down again, and he asked: Master breaks wind too? Jingjing said he did; more than you. The corner of Jingfu's mouth moved, and he quickly pressed it shut. Jingjing said his face was red then too, and he said to his Pa: Pa, how shameful. Pa first made him eat ginger, and then said: you did it, so you did it; you didn't blame it on anyone else; what's shameful in that? What shames is the lie, not the wind.

Jingfu's smile stopped. Jingjing said no more, but took up his own bowl of hot water and slowly drank a mouthful. He thought of the night his Pa was dying, and that thing his Pa told him in the country speech of Balkh, how these words were what his uncle had paid his whole life to buy for him; he did not tell Jingfu; Jingfu was twelve and did not need to know so much; he needed only to know that one sentence. Jingfu sat, head bowed, looking at the hot water in his bowl, and after a few breaths stood up and went out; at the door he turned back again and asked: Master, that old soldier—did he laugh at you afterwards? Jingjing said no; all his life he never mentioned it again; afterwards he took me over the Pamirs, to Ctesiphon, and walked back alone, and put my diary into Pa's hands. Jingfu nodded and ran out. Through the wall Jingjing heard him run over to the scripture room, stop, and say loudly: Brother Siyuan, it was me.

---

In the spring of the third year of Yuanhe, a letter came from the Patriarchate. It came with a Uighur caravan; the caravan had come round by the north; the road through Hexi had been cut more than twenty years: the Tibetans held Liangzhou, Ganzhou and Shazhou, and the Tang post stations had fallen into ruin one after another; now from Chang'an going west one had first to go north, to the Uighurs' land, then west round the Tianshan, before one could join the road to the Two Rivers; so this letter took nearly two years to come. The letter was written on parchment, folded in a long strip, tied with fine cord, the knot sealed with black wax, the Patriarch's seal pressed in the wax. The writer was Timothy.

This patriarch had sat on the throne nearly thirty years; Jingjing had heard merchants from the west speak of him: that his learning was great, that he had disputed doctrine before the Caliph, disputed two days, and the Caliph did not say he was wrong; and that his arm was long: he had sent a metropolitan north to the Turks, and sent men toward Tibet too. In the second year of Jianzhong Jingjing had written him a letter telling him that Hnanisho's name was carved on the stele and the characters on the stele would not be changed; in the twenty-odd years since, there had been no answer. This letter was written very courteously, and in the courtesy there was something very hard. The letter first greeted the people of the Chang'an monastery, said the Patriarchate had for years had no sure word from the east; what the merchants brought was little true and much false; said too that of the setting up of the stele in Chang'an he had received a letter in his early years; the stele bore his predecessor's name; he knew it, and that was that. Then it came to the business: the province of Sinistan had been without a metropolitan for decades; the Patriarchate could not leave it empty any longer; he had already ordained a man, named David, of Merv, of good learning and no longer young, and meant to send him by the sea road, down from Basra across the sea of the Tazi on a *hu* merchant's ship to Guangzhou, and north from Guangzhou; he would be a year or two on the road; whether he would arrive, he dared not say. He said that when David arrived, those in Chang'an who should have hands laid on them would have hands laid on them by him; what Chang'an should settle would be settled by him together with Chang'an's leader; he said too that if Chang'an's leader could make a journey to the Patriarchate and lay it all out face to face, that too would be good; either would do; let the east choose.

At the end of the letter Timothy wrote one sentence that had nothing to do with the business: "I hear that in former days the Patriarchate had an archdeacon who in his lifetime would always ask those come from the east: Is the road still open? After I took office I went through his old files, and found on the edge of one page, in his hand, the name of a child. I think that child too must be old now." Jingjing put the letter down.

That night he did not sleep. The next morning he stood in the middle of the courtyard and told his knotted cord twice through, and at the second time through, before the sky was grey, he wanted to go to the Patriarchate. Once this thought rose it would not be pressed down. He was sixty-two; he had been twice to the Patriarchate in his life: the first time at ten, on a camel's back, tied to the pack frame with rope by Xu Jing, travelling half a year; the second at fifteen, with Elder Marcus, half a year again; that time, arriving at the Patriarchate, his uncle asked him: is the road still open? and he said: it's open; I've travelled it once; and that summer his uncle died in the room by the storehouse, his left hand gripping his wrist, his fingers stopping on the sleeve-knot. His uncle was buried at Ctesiphon; now the Patriarchate had moved to Baghdad; that grave at Ctesiphon—whether anyone still went to pull its weeds, he did not know. He wanted to go and look once at that grave, wanted to stand before Timothy and tell him one by one the east's affairs of these fifty years; he wanted once more to hear someone ask him whether the road was still open, so that he could answer: I've travelled it twice.

He went first to Li Su. Li Su was past sixty that year, had been an official in the Directorate of Astronomy for decades, risen very high, and was still the same man: thin, slow of speech, reckoning everything on his fingers; at home he kept a great many books, Tang, Persian, Indian, heaped in one room with only a crack left to walk through. Jingjing showed him the letter; Li Su read it and asked: you want to go? Jingjing said he did. Li Su said neither good nor not good, got up and drew a roll of map out of the heap of books, spread it on the floor, and weighted it with two paperweights; on the map were drawn mountains, rivers, city after city, and beside the cities names written in both Tang characters and *hu* letters. He pointed at the map with his finger, place by place: Hexi is the Tibetans' now; at Beiting a dozen-odd years ago the Uighurs and Tibetans fought several battles, and the city changed masters several times; going west, you can only keep close along the Uighurs' land, go to around Suyab, then turn south; all along the road there are Uighurs, Qarluqs, Tibetans, the Tazi; which stretch belongs to whom differs this year from next. Jingjing squatted by the map looking; he knew some of the place names; he had travelled them at ten and again at fifteen, when all these places flew Tang flags. Li Su said: a year going, a year coming back; you're sixty-two. As he said it his fingers were still reckoning, as if working out an account. Jingjing said his Pa went at fifty-nine. Li Su looked at him, rolled up the map, and said: then you've already lived three years longer. Neither of them laughed.

He went back to the monastery, called Lingbao and Siyuan, and told them. Siyuan said Hexi was impassable; by the north it would take a year; and said: the Master's stomach—the water in the north is all cold. Lingbao beside him was past forty; the accounts were in his hands and the keys at his waist; he looked at Jingjing a few breaths and said: if Brother wants to go, he'll keep watch here. Jingfu heard outside the door; he was thirteen; he stood in the doorway, one hand on the doorframe, and said he'd go with the Master. Jingjing said: you won't go. Jingfu asked why. Jingjing said: if you go, who'll light the lamp? Jingfu opened his mouth and said nothing.

Jingjing packed all the same. He had Siyuan go to the West Market to find that Uighur caravan; the caravan was going back in autumn and could take people. The things he would take he laid out one by one on the bed: two robes for changing, a roll of scripture, a packet of ginger, a packet of myrobalan, a stack of paper, a brush, a stick of ink; at his neck hung his grandfather's wooden cross, and inside his robe was Ahad's knotted cord; those two needed no packing; they were always with him. He wrote a reply to Timothy, meaning to hand it over in person: he wrote a dozen-odd pages: how many souls the congregation of the Chang'an monastery had, how much property, how many scriptures, how it dealt with the government; wrote of Lord Guo, of his Pa, of that stele, of Prajna, of Official Pei, of Narsai's cart, of Baaluo; and when he came to that thing of the second year of Zhenyuan he stopped his pen, thought, and wrote it in after all: There is a government document that says your servant's reasoning is obscure and his words loose. When he had written it, he looked at it himself and smiled.

Those few mornings the character on his board was *rao*, to go round. What he read was Exodus: when the Israelites came out of Egypt, God led them not through the way of the land of the Philistines, although that was near, but led them about, through the way of the wilderness of the Red Sea.

---

On the day the caravan left, before light, Jingjing went out of the monastery gate. Jingfu wanted to see him off; he would not let him, saying: you stay home and lead the morning office; Jingfu stood by the stone block at the monastery gate holding that lamp not yet blown out, and said nothing more. Siyuan led the camel for him all the way to the Kaiyuan Gate. The Kaiyuan Gate was the gate of Chang'an going west; its gateway was very deep, footsteps humming in it as one walked; outside the gate a great road ran west, stretching away out of sight; by the gate stood a stone, carved: from here west to Anxi, so many *li*; the number was very large, so large it looked like a mistake. The Uighur caravan was already waiting outside the gate, the camels kneeling one after another on the ground, the loads not yet all on, the merchants walking among the camels speaking words Jingjing half understood.

Jingjing stood in the gateway and did not go out. He thought of his Pa: that year his Pa stood at the courtyard gate in Lingzhou watching him ride off on the camel, and did not see him even to the mouth of the lane; afterwards he asked his Pa why he hadn't seen him off that day, and his Pa said: see you out, and you wouldn't want to come back. He thought too of something else his Pa said: the year he was ten he asked his Pa why he wouldn't go with him to the Patriarchate, and his Pa said he followed Lord Guo; Lord Guo wanted him to see the road; if he went, there'd be no one to see the road. He had not understood then, and thought the road his Pa meant was the road from Chang'an to Ctesiphon. He stood in the gateway till the caravan's head came over twice to say they were leaving; Siyuan beside him held the camel and did not make a sound. Then he turned round, took from his robe that letter of a dozen-odd pages, gave it to the head of the caravan, and said: take this letter to Baghdad and give it to the Patriarch. The head took it and weighed it in his hand and asked: and the man? Jingjing said: the man isn't going. The head looked at him, probably thinking this old *hu* monk a little strange, asked no more, and stuffed the letter into a pack.

That day when he got back to the monastery it was already the hour of *wu*. He undid the bundle and put the things back one by one where they belonged: the robes back in the cupboard, the scripture back in the scripture room, the paper and brush back on the table; last of all the packet of ginger, which he carried to the stove and put on the stove top. To that letter he afterwards added another, sent with the next caravan; the added one was very short, only a few lines: "The leader of the Chang'an monastery cannot go. If the metropolitan David arrives, Chang'an awaits him. The affairs of the east are set out in full in the former letter. The Patriarch asks: is the road still open? The road is still open; only one must go round. Jingjing, at Chang'an."

---

David arrived in the twelfth month of the fourth year of Yuanhe. He had drifted nine months at sea on a *hu* merchant's ship, been sick half a year at Guangzhou, and gone north from Guangzhou, over the Dayu range, past Hongzhou, past Xiangzhou—the very road Baaluo had walked back by from Lingnan—and by the time he reached Chang'an it was the twelfth month. He was past sixty, of Merv, tall, though his back was bent now, his beard snow-white, his eyes grey like a winter sky. He spoke Syriac, Persian, a little of the speech of the Tazi, and not one word of Tang. Two young monks had come off the ship with him, one named Joseph and one Elijah, both of Merv, a little over twenty, who had held him up all the way and watched over him by turns those six months of sickness at Guangzhou. He came in at the monastery gate and stopped first before the stele in the front courtyard, looked at the cross on its head, at the two dragons, at the Syriac below, and when he came to that line at the foot of the stele, "Yazdbozid, priest and chorepiscopus of Kumdan the royal city," he asked Jingjing where this man was. Jingjing said he had died twenty-five years ago; he was his Pa. David looked at him, looked at the stele again, and said: chorepiscopus, so the letter wrote; the laying on of hands? Jingjing said the laying on of hands had not been done; Chang'an had no bishop; there was no one who could do it. David nodded and said nothing more.

On the third day after David arrived, in the small council room, before Lingbao, Siyuan, Sabrisho and a few others, he told Jingjing his conditions. He spoke very slowly, the Syriac coming out word by word, as if he had thought it out long ago on the ship. He said the Patriarch sent him first to lay on hands and second to look at the Chang'an monastery; what the Patriarchate had heard these years was that the Chang'an monastery accommodated the Tang, accommodated them too much: sang Tang songs in the hall, wrote Buddhist monks' and Daoists' characters in the scriptures, enshrined the Son of Heaven on the stele; he had come and seen; the stele he would not concern himself with; the stele was permitted by your former patriarch; the songs in the hall and the characters in the scriptures he would concern himself with. He said that before the laying on of hands, the singing of the Tang hymns in the hall was to stop, and everything be sung in Syriac; and in the scripture room, of the Tang scriptures, wherever characters had been borrowed from another house, all were to be changed back to transcriptions: Alaha is Alaha, Meshiha is Meshiha; Heavenly Worthy, non-action, wondrous existence—not one to be kept; when these two things were done, he would lay on hands; if not, he would live in the side courtyard and wait. Sabrisho beside them listened, his beard twitching; probably he approved in his heart and could not well say so. Lingbao looked at Jingjing.

Jingjing thought it over all night. The next morning the character on his board was *rang*, to yield. What he read was that line of Philippians, that the Lord, being in the form of God, thought it not robbery to be equal with God, but made himself of no reputation, and took upon him the form of a servant. He went to the side courtyard and told David: the Tang hymns, he would stop for a month; for a month only Syriac would be sung in the hall; the characters in the scripture room he could not change; dozens of scriptures, changed back character by character to transcription—at his age, he'd die before he finished; he would hand the keys of the scripture room to David; David could look, and whichever scripture he wanted changed, he would sit with him and change it. David looked at him and said: and after a month? Jingjing said: after a month, let the elder see for himself.

That month only Syriac was sung in the hall. At the first Lord's Day, the old believers of the ward sang along and could not, their mouths open, unable to follow the tunes; at the second, half the back rows were empty; at the third, of those seven households come back from the south of the city, three did not come; An Yan went to ask, and they said they couldn't understand it; kneeling there was like being guests in someone else's house. The old woman who sold sesame cakes in the south lane of the West Market came, knelt in the last row, could not follow one line, knelt till the worship was over, stood up, and asked Lingbao: that one from before, "In the highest of the heavens, deep reverence and praise"—she knew it; she'd sung it thirty years; is it not allowed now? Lingbao said the elder come from the Patriarchate would not allow it. The old woman said: where's the Patriarchate? Lingbao could not answer. Every Lord's Day David stood at the front and watched the back rows empty row by row; he knew no Tang, but empty benches he could read. At the fourth Lord's Day, when worship was over, he called Jingjing to the side courtyard; he sat on that folding chair his Pa used to sit on, and said: next time, sing them again. Jingjing said: the elder has seen. David said he had seen; before he came, the Patriarchate's people told him the Chang'an monastery accommodated the Tang; he had come and been here a month, and what he saw was that the Tang, in this monastery, were living in their own house; he took the Tang words away, and they became guests.

The laying on of hands took place in the second month of the fifth year of Yuanhe, on the great *Yaosenwen* day, the day of the offering once in seven; the hall sang the Tang hymns again; the hall was packed; those few households come back from the south of the city came too; An Yan stood at the front; Jingfu stood beside Lingbao. By the rule, to ordain a bishop three bishops should lay on hands together; David was only one; he took from his robe a note in Timothy's hand and had Jingjing read it to all; the note said: the east is far, three bishops cannot be had; let David lay on hands alone, and the Patriarchate will acknowledge it. When Jingjing had read it, he knelt in the middle of the hall, and David stood before him and laid both hands on his head; those two hands were very big, very cold, and pressed very heavily; David recited in Syriac the passage for the ordaining of a bishop, recited it very slowly, word by word, and when he came to the end his hands still pressed and were not lifted; Jingjing knelt there and felt the weight of those two hands press all the way down to his knees. He thought of the night his Pa was dying, his Pa saying: from now on, all these things are yours; he thought of the sleeve-knot his uncle tied with his left hand; he thought of Yonan, who long ago had knelt in this hall too, and someone had laid hands on his head, and afterwards he broke his vow. David lifted his hands and told him to rise. Then it was Lingbao: David laid hands on him and made him priest; as Lingbao knelt, Jingjing saw his shoulders shaking.

David stayed a year in Chang'an. He lived in the room in the side courtyard where his Pa used to live, and every day went with Jingjing to the hall, to the scripture room, to the gruel shed in the ward street, outside the Kaiyuan Gate to look at those rows of stakes. He took the keys of the scripture room and looked at those Tang scriptures there, had Jingjing translate them for him sentence by sentence; hearing Heavenly Worthy, hearing non-action, hearing wondrous existence, his brows knit tighter and tighter, and at last he asked Jingjing what those little dots beside them were. Jingjing said they were his marks: wherever he had borrowed a character from Buddhist monks or Daoists, he put a dot. David held that roll of scripture and turned page after page, turned till he could not count them, put it down, and said: you've put so many dots. Jingjing said: thirty years of dots.

That autumn David went once with Siyuan pushing the death-cart. He could not push; he held the handle and walked; outside the Kaiyuan Gate he watched Siyuan and two young men lift off the cart an old man frozen to death, bury him, set up a stake, and write on the stake which ward he died in and which day. David stood in the burial ground looking at those rows of stakes: one row, two rows, three rows, four, stretching away to the foot of a distant earth slope; and asked Jingjing how many people this was; Jingjing said he had never counted; Lingbao's accounts had it; some thousands. David asked nothing else. That day when he went back, he wrote a letter to Timothy in the side courtyard, wrote it two days, and when it was written called Jingjing and said: this letter you're not to read; what he's written is Chang'an's affairs; what he saw, that's what he wrote. He said too: before he came, the Patriarchate's people told him the Chang'an monastery accommodated the Tang too much, and sent him to set it right; he had come, and looked a year, and could not set it right, and did not want to any more; he was old; he could not understand Chang'an; you understand it; carry on as you were.

David died in the twelfth month of that year, in that room in the side courtyard; before he died he had Jingjing give him the last communion, with that glass bowl. He was buried outside the Kaiyuan Gate, beside his Pa. That letter he wrote to Timothy Jingjing sent with the next Uighur caravan; what it said, Jingjing never knew.

---

That year, the character Jingjing wrote many times on his board was *shou*, hand. What he read was the second epistle to Timothy, the Dharma King Baolu writing to his disciple Timothy: stir up the gift of God, which is in thee by the putting on of my hands. As he read it he thought: the Timothy to whom the Dharma King Baolu wrote was a man of a thousand years ago; now the Timothy of Baghdad, bearing that name, had sent a man of Merv across the sea and laid hands on him; the weight of the two hands on his head had pressed down across a thousand years and across a sea.

---

Note: Timothy I (r. 780–823), Patriarch of the Church of the East, mentions in his letters sending metropolitans to the Turks, to Tibet, and elsewhere; Thomas of Marga's *Book of Governors* records that he ordained David "metropolitan of Beth Sinaye." David's coming east to Chang'an and laying hands on Jingjing are the novel's invention. The prefectures of Hexi had fallen to the Tibetans before the early Zhenyuan years.
