# Volume Seven　A-Lian

## Chapter 40　The Letter Comes

> Thereupon one Person of our Trinity, the Luminous Lord Meshiha, folded away His true majesty and came into the world in the likeness of men.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

Yonan saw to that porter's child seven days.

The first three days he went every day; the child's fever broke, rose again, broke again; on the fourth day the child sat up and clutched at his mother's dress for the breast; on the fifth the child smiled at Yonan, showing two little teeth; on the seventh Yonan felt the child's forehead—cool—and shut his medicine bag, and told the porter there was no need for him to come again; if the child was feverish at night again, sponge him with warm water; where to sponge, for how long, he told him once more, and when he had told it had the porter's wife say it back to him, and only when she had it right did he get up. In those seven days, every day when he came out of that earthen room he went round to the shop in front and sat a while. She brought him a bowl of hot water, broke a cake, half for him and half for herself. They did not say much; she talked of the shop: which porter had run up an account again, which *hu* merchant complained her cakes were too burnt, how the son of the old *hu* mutton-seller across the way had married a Tang girl and the old man was so angry he didn't open for three days; he talked of the monastery: Babai's eyes were bad again, Ishaq was fatter again, Lingbao could recite the hymns, and when he came to "on the great earth all again think on peace and concord," *bu an he*, he always said "on the great earth all again think on mending shoes," *bu xie he*, and Yisi didn't correct him either. Talking, they would stop talking, and the two of them would sit listening to the charcoal crackle in the oven. When the gong sounded he got up and left.

In those seven days the monastery's people all knew Yonan went to the West Market every evening and came back with flour on his cuffs. No one asked. Elder Ishaq came back from the minor clerk's house, heard Yonan had gone to treat the sick in his place, slapped his belly and said good, and said Yonan hadn't set foot in the West Market for seventeen years, and now he'd been in, from now on Ishaq could have Yonan go and buy from that roast-mutton shop for him. Babai, mending scrolls beside him, without raising his head said Yonan wouldn't go and buy for him. Ishaq asked why; Babai said he didn't know either; he just thought he wouldn't.

On the seventh day Yonan got to the shop door, turned his head, and said he wouldn't come tomorrow. She was stacking cakes; her hands stopped a moment, then went on stacking, and she said: then see to your own affairs; the shop's here; it won't run away.

---

That night Yonan went to the side courtyard to find Yisi.

Yisi was fifty that year, his hair still cropped short, a great deal of white in the short hair now. After supper that day he was under the jujube tree putting a tail on Lingbao's wooden horse; the tail was a few strands of palm fibre Shiyiniang had pulled from an old broom, and he was binding them one by one onto the horse's rump with fine hemp thread, very carefully. Yonan came over and stood beside him, waited while he bound one strand, and another. Yisi looked up at him once, asked nothing at all, moved a low stool beside him a little to one side, and Yonan sat down on it.

The two of them sat under the jujube tree; the tree was just in bud, the buds very small, their colour not to be seen in the dusk. Yonan spoke first. He told of these seven days: the porter, the child, that lane, that cake shop, that small accounts board, the scars on her hands; her nine years working at the Mu shop in Luoyang right next door to the Xiushan Ward monastery; her eight years back in Chang'an, two wards away. He told it very slowly, sometimes in Tang, sometimes in Syriac, and toward the end it was all Syriac. He said too that it was Jingjing who had come to the front hall to call him that day; Jingjing knew that board; Jingjing had drunk a bowl of soup at that shop; Jingjing could treat the sick himself; Jingjing had not gone, but sent him. When Yisi heard this he raised his head and looked once toward the west wing; the lamp was still lit in the west wing window; he looked once and bent his head again to binding his horse's tail. Yonan said he wanted to write to the Patriarchate and ask to be released from his vow. The hemp thread in Yisi's hand stopped, and he asked: "Is she willing?" Yonan was taken aback, and said he hadn't asked. Yisi nodded and said: ask her first, then write; and when you write, write it as it was; hide not one word; what shames is the lie. When he had said it he bound another strand, and when it was bound tied a knot, and set the wooden horse on the windowsill.

Yonan sat on the low stool and said further that the letter would have to speak of the child, and he wanted the child to write that himself. He said there was too much about that child he didn't know; when he left the child was three months old; the twenty-nine years after were Yisi's raising, and the child's own growing; if he wrote of the child, write as he might, he could write only an infant wrapped in a sheepskin; he did not want to speak for that child; the child could speak, and spoke better than he. Yisi thought and said: this had to be the child's own wish; he would not agree on Jingjing's behalf; Yonan must go and ask himself.

When Yonan had gone, Yisi went to the west wing; Jingjing was still under the lamp revising drafts. Yisi stood at the door and asked him whether, that evening at the beginning of the third month, he had finished checking the gruel accounts. The brush in Jingjing's hand stopped; he said he hadn't finished; he'd checked two lines and couldn't go on. Yisi said that day's accounts Shiyiniang had finished checking for him the next day, and not a coin was off. When he had said it he did not come in, but turned and went back to the side courtyard. Jingjing sat under the lamp, and that night did not revise another word.

---

The next day, Yonan went to the West Market again.

When she saw him come in she was taken aback and said: didn't you say you weren't coming? He did not sit, but stood by the low table and said what he had to say, said it haltingly; there were several words he thought a long time before he found them. He said that when he was young, in the monastery, he had taken a vow, taken it before the Lord: not to marry, not to have a family. He said he had broken that vow, and hidden it thirty years, hidden it so the whole monastery did not know, not even the child she bore. He said he meant now to write to the Patriarchate and tell all this as it was, and ask the Patriarchate to release him from his vow; if it was granted, he wanted to marry her; if it was not, he would keep the vow as before, keep it till death. He said he could not decide this for her; he had come to ask her first; if she said no, he would not write. When he had said it, he stood there with his two hands hanging at his sides, like the man of thirty years ago just come to Chang'an, unable to say one whole sentence of Tang.

She sat by the oven and heard him out, poked the charcoal in the oven with the iron hook—the charcoal crackled once—and said she was fifty. Yonan said he was seventy.

She looked at him, and said that in these thirty years she had never thought of marrying, nor of not marrying; she had only thought that if he came back, she'd look at him once from far off; and if he didn't, she'd take it that he was over there saying his prayers in peace. She said that the winter of the year she came back to Chang'an, by the ward gate, she had seen him standing under the lamp, and she had thought: he's back, he's still saying his prayers, he's standing there, I've seen him, and that's enough. She said she was fifty now, with sickness in her body, coughing at night, scars all over her hands; what he'd be marrying was an old woman who ran a cake shop. Yonan said he knew. She stood up, went to the oven and peeled off a cake, broke it in two, held out half to him, and said: "Write, then. If it's granted, I'll marry you. If not, you'll come and eat cakes as before."

Yonan took the half cake, ate it standing, and only when he had eaten it sat down.

---

That evening Yonan went to the west wing.

Jingjing was alone under the lamp revising drafts. Babai and Gabriel had gone to bed; Narsai had left too; the low stool under the window was empty, and beside it lay a little heap of reed shavings. Yonan stood a moment at the door, came in, and sat down across from Jingjing, in Babai's seat. Jingjing put down his brush and waited for him to speak.

Yonan told how he had gone to ask her, said she had agreed, said he would write to the Patriarchate. He said he meant to write this letter in Syriac, write it himself, but his eyes were not what they had been and his hand shook; if he wrote at length the letters would go crooked, and the Patriarchate's scribes would laugh; he wanted to ask Jingjing to copy it for him. Jingjing said he would. Yonan said further that there was a passage in the letter that had to be about the child; he could not write it; he wanted the child to write it himself—write how he had grown up, write what he thought in his heart once he knew; write as much as he liked; if he didn't want to write, leave it blank, that would do too; when it was written Yonan would not read it, but seal it in the letter, and the Patriarchate would read it, and that would be that.

Jingjing sat there, his hands on the table, motionless. He thought of that step at the door of the front hall: once he took it in, what came after was not his to decide; and now Yonan was handing that passage to him, and what came after was his to decide again. A snuff had formed on the wick; he reached out to trim it and took two tries to cut it, just as that year in the side courtyard trimming the wick across from his Pa—his hand shook. When it was trimmed, he asked Yonan why he would not read it.

Yonan said: they're your words; you say them to the Patriarchate; he'd be beside you; he would only listen.

The letter took five evenings to write.

The first three evenings Yonan dictated and Jingjing copied. Yonan spoke in Syriac, very slowly, sentence by sentence; Jingjing wrote as he spoke, and where something was unclear asked, and Yonan said it again. Yonan began from his coming to Chang'an in the third year of Tianbao: he was thirty-nine that year and became bishop of Chang'an; he wrote of how the monastery was then, of Elder Jihe's coming east, of the edict summoning the seventeen of them into the Xingqing Palace to perform rites of merit, of the Son of Heaven's edict changing the monastery's name; he wrote of the coughing in the back lane of the West Market that second month, of his going with medicine, of a basin of warm water, of four letters and a heart on a wooden tablet; he wrote of her coming to hear the preaching, of her baptism, of those three nights of the Lantern Festival, and of how from then on he dared not look at her; he wrote of her being with child and going away by herself, of the child's being born and on the fifth day her carrying him to knock at Yisi's door; he wrote of baptizing the child with his own hands, writing that line in the register and having her make her mark, of telling Yisi at night under the jujube tree and begging him not to tell the child; he wrote of his recall to the Patriarchate, of how on the day he left it was snowing and he did not go to see her. When he got this far he stopped a long time; Jingjing's pen hung over the paper, waiting; oil was added to the dish once. Yonan said: go on; write how after he went back to the Patriarchate he asked for the lowest office and copied scripture at the Patriarchate, copied for ten years; write how he heard An Lushan had rebelled and Luoyang was lost, and asked of his own accord to go east; write the Pamirs, write Xizhou, write how at night in Xizhou he heard a child's footsteps pass along the corridor. When Jingjing wrote this line his pen stopped, and he raised his head. Yonan did not look at him, but looked out of the window, and said: go on writing; that child is you. Jingjing bent his head and went on writing.

On the fourth evening Yonan did not come. Jingjing was alone in the west wing; he put the stack of copied sheets under a paperweight, spread out another sheet, took up the pen, and sat half the night without setting it down. There were too many things he wanted to write: the lamp at Xizhou, the palace walls at Ctesiphon, his uncle's sleeve-knot, Du Huan at the ferry asking him whether the locust trees of Chang'an were still there, Uncle Duan dying at Xiangzhou, his Pa's eyes, his Ma—Yisi's dead wife, who washed him in warm water in Lingzhou and raised him six years. He wanted to write of the day he opened the register, of writing the two Tang characters *Jingjing* beside his own line and scraping them off again; wanted to write of standing in that lane of the West Market with his head empty; wanted to write of seeing the tail of the Nun on that board flick upward; wanted to write of standing at the door of the front hall, stepping in, and wanting to step back out. He thought half the night, and in the end wrote none of it; toward dawn he wrote a few lines on that sheet:

"The one who writes this is called Adam, born in Chang'an in the fifth year of Tianbao, baptized by Elder Yonan. He who raised me is Yazdbozid of Balkh; his former wife was a Sogdian, who raised me till I was six and died of illness. I call Yazdbozid Pa. Since I came to know this thing, I still call him Pa. Elder Yonan I call Elder. The one who bore me I have seen once in the West Market, and drunk a bowl of her soup, and not called her by any name. I translate scripture in Chang'an and have charge of the charity. Of the things Elder Yonan says in this letter, those I know of are all true. Those I do not know of, I do not bear witness to on his behalf. I beg the Patriarchate to deal according to the rule, and not to give thought to me."

When it was written he read it through once, paused a moment at the words "not to give thought to me," did not change them, folded the sheet, and put it in among the others.

The next morning in the hall Jingjing knelt half a pace behind his Pa, and when worship was over Yisi walked to the door of the hall and stopped, and without turning his head asked whether he had written that passage. Jingjing said he had. Yisi asked whether it was long. Jingjing said not long; a few lines. Yisi nodded and walked out, and at the foot of the steps stopped again and said: "Did you write about me?" Jingjing said he had; he wrote that he who raised him was Yazdbozid of Balkh, and he called him Pa. Yisi stood at the foot of the steps with his back to him, stood a moment, said he was going to split firewood, and went off to the back courtyard. That day from the hour of *chen* to the hour of *wu* the sound of wood being split in the back courtyard was louder than usual, and the wood split was enough for the monastery to burn half a month. Shiyiniang came out to look several times and said: what is this, are you going to split the jujube tree too? Yisi said there was no such thing as too much firewood; it'd be needed in winter.

On the fifth evening Yonan came, and dictated the end of the letter. He said: "I broke my vow, and hid it thirty years. I do not ask to be pardoned; I ask only to lay the truth before you. If it is not granted, I will keep my vow as before, and not change till death. If it is granted, I beg the Patriarch to pray for her; she is the Lord's sheep." When Jingjing had written it, Yonan took the pen from his hand and wrote his own name at the end of the letter, the letters still thin, slanting right, with a small hook where the stroke ended; his hand shook, and the hook trailed a little longer than it used to.

There was one more sheet, A-Lian's. Yonan said that for the Patriarchate to grant his release it must also know whether she was willing; she could not write Syriac, only those few letters; what she had to say, Jingjing should translate for her. The next evening Jingjing went to the shop in the West Market. When she saw him come in, the iron hook in her hand stopped, and she looked at him, and he looked at her. He said Elder Yonan had sent him; the Patriarchate needed a sheet in her own words; she would speak, and he would write it in Syriac for her, and when it was written read it to her, and when she nodded, she would make her mark. She had the young man mind the oven, brought him in to the low table inside, brought him a bowl of soup, hot, and sat across from him. She did not say much. She said she was called A-Lian, of Chang'an, no surname; she was baptized at the monastery in the autumn of the fourth year of Tianbao, baptismal name Susanna, baptized by Elder Yonan; she said she had borne Elder Yonan a son, and that child was well now; she said Elder Yonan wished to marry her, and she was willing; if the Patriarchate granted it she would marry him; if the Patriarchate did not, she blamed no one. When she had said it she watched Jingjing write; Jingjing wrote very fast, and when it was written read it to her in Tang sentence by sentence; when he came to "that child is well now," she said that sentence she'd put in herself; if you think it shouldn't be written, strike it out. Jingjing said he would not strike it out. She nodded, took the pen, and at the end of that sheet wrote stroke by stroke: Alaph, Lamadh, Yudh, Nun; the tail of the Nun flicked upward.

Jingjing looked at that tail, put the sheet away under a paperweight, and waited for the ink to dry. While waiting for the ink to dry, she asked him whether the west wing was cold in winter; he said it was; no brazier was lit, for fear of sparks on the scrolls. She asked: then when your inkstone freezes, what do you do? He said: breathe on it. She asked again whether his stomach still hurt; he was taken aback, and said it had stopped long ago; it hurt when he was small. She said she'd heard the deacon in charge of the charity at the monastery had a weak stomach; everyone at the gruel kitchen knew; they said that *hu* monk deacon wouldn't drink a mouthful of anything cold even in the heat. Jingjing said that was his Pa's doing. When she heard it, she bowed her head and wiped a ring of water off the table with the corner of her apron, and when it was wiped, asked him again: all these years, had the deacon never married? Jingjing said he'd never thought of it; there was too much to do at the monastery. She said: true; and asked no more. The ink was dry; he put the sheet away and stood up to go. She called him back and said: you haven't drunk your soup. So he stood and drank the bowl of soup, and when he had drunk it said thank you and left. She sat at the low table and did not get up to see him out, but watched him go out of the door, and watched him stop a moment before that board at the door before he went.

---

This letter, all the monastery's elders came to know of.

Yonan told them himself. Before the letter was sealed he took it to Elder Azarel and in front of several of the elders gave them the gist of what was written in it; when it came to the child, he said the child was in Chang'an now, and well; who it was, he would not say; if the child wished to say, he would say it himself. Jingjing, keeping the record at the lowest seat, kept his head bowed, and his pen did not stop. He said this letter was to go with the monastery's official letters; he did not want to go behind anyone's back; and he said too that Chang'an had no bishop, and what a bishop should decide was, by the order, to be decided by the Superintendent together with the Warden of the Rule and the elders in council; by the rule his matter should first be discussed here, and when discussed, reported to the Patriarchate.

When Azarel had heard it, he sat there, his fingers telling the beads on his knee, round after round. That day the seven teachers were all there, and the three elders from the Patriarchate too; Jingjing, as Deacon of Charity, by the order sat at the lowest seat in councils and kept the record.

Elder Samaan, his two hands folded in his sleeves, looked out of the window, and after a while took his hands out of his sleeves, opened his leather-bound account book, turned to a page very near the front, and said that when the seven elders came to Chang'an in the first year of Qianyuan, Elder Yonan's name was written seventh, written as former bishop of Chang'an, his status that of a monk, one who had vowed; he said the order said Chang'an would for the present have no bishop, and what a bishop should decide would be decided by the Superintendent together with the Warden of the Rule and the elders in council; but when one who had vowed broke his vow, only the Patriarch could decide; here in Chang'an they could come to an opinion, but could not decide; they could only report. When he had said it, he took up his pen and wrote a line in the account book: Yonan, former bishop of Chang'an, confessed himself to have broken his vow; the elders in council; to be reported to the Patriarchate. Of what Yonan said about the child, he wrote nothing. He folded his hands back into his sleeves and said he had said what he had to say.

Elder Sabrisho stroked that felt-like beard of his and said that by the rule, one who had vowed and broke the vow was to be cut off, and once cut off could not receive the Body again; and by the rule too, there was an article that the penitent the Church should take back; both articles were there; it remained to see which the Patriarchate would use. Elder Marya kept his head bowed, his fingers tapping on his knee, tapping a long while, and said he had preached all his life, preached the prodigal son, preached himself hoarse, and today when it came to one of their own, he did not know which passage to preach. Gabriel said: preach that passage, then; the father, seeing his son coming back from afar, didn't wait for him to come up, but ran out. As he said it the scar on his left cheek puckered, and he looked at Yonan.

Haddad had not spoken all along; only now did he say that he had something to own before the elders; he had known of this long ago; that year at Xizhou, Elder Yonan had a fever at night and raved, crying out a Tang name, cried it half the night; afterwards he had asked Yonan, and Yonan had not hidden it from him. He said he had known it ten-odd years and told no one; that was his fault; if the elders wished to record it, let them record it against him. Elder Samaan glanced at him, and did not record it.

Narsai sat at the head of the lower seats, only one man between him and Jingjing. From the moment Yonan began to speak he had sat with his back straight as a rod, his two hands on his knees, his eyes on the floor. When the others had all spoken, he raised his head, looked at Yonan, and said he had come out of the Patriarchate with Yonan and walked all the way to Chang'an with him, twenty years now; Yonan washed his hands three times a day, ate half a flatbread at every meal, looked east at night; he had watched it twenty years and could not get used to it; that year on the road to Balkh Yonan had held out flatbread to him and he had not taken it; he had thought Yonan kept the rule too hard, so hard it was not like keeping, but like atoning for something. He said today he knew what Yonan was atoning for; atoning was hard too, but atoning and keeping were two different things: keeping is not having broken; atoning is having broken. He said a vow once taken was taken to the Lord, not to the Patriarchate; if the Patriarchate granted it, whether the Lord granted it the Patriarchate did not know, and nor did he. He said he would not stand in the way of this letter; if it should be sent, send it; however the Patriarchate answered, he would do as the Patriarchate said; but in his heart he could not get past it, and could not get past it was could not get past it; he would not say he could. When he had said it he stood up, bowed to Elder Azarel, said he would go back to the west wing first to cut paper—the paper for tomorrow was not yet cut—and went out.

Babai had not spoken all along; now he said he had copied the Patriarchate's canons three times, and for one who had vowed and broken the vow and then told it himself, the canons had nothing written; when he copied them he had felt there was an article missing. Ishaq opened his mouth, kept it open a long while, and in the end took a dry flatbread from his coat, broke off half, and laid it by Yonan's hand.

At last Azarel spoke, and said only one word: "Send."

That evening at supper Narsai came and sat in his usual place, and everyone at the table spoke more softly. Halfway through the meal he put down his bowl and said one sentence: "A vow is taken to the Lord." Having said it, he did not open his mouth again; everyone at the table looked at him; he looked at no one, took up his bowl, and drank the rest of his gruel. The next day in the west wing he sat as usual under the window cutting paper; Jingjing looked up at him once; he did not look up. The paper he cut that day still had edges level as water.

---

The letter went in two copies.

After the road through Hexi was closed, whenever the monastery wrote to the Patriarchate it made two copies of each letter, one to go by the Uighur road and one by the sea road. The copy for the Uighur road was entrusted to a Sogdian caravan going to the Uighur khaghan's court; the caravan's head was surnamed An, an old believer of the Liquan Ward; it went out of the Kaiyuan Gate at the end of the fourth month, north, and out beyond the frontier by Fengzhou. The copy for the sea road was entrusted to Li Su; Li Su's family still had a cousin in Guangzhou who traded by sea; in the fifth month a shipment was going to Guangzhou, and Li Su put the letter inside a family letter to his cousin, telling his cousin to give it to the next ship going to the sea of the Tazi. Jingjing copied these two letters, copied them over two evenings, and when they were copied wrote a line of small characters at the end of each, on one "by the Uighur road," on the other "by the sea road." He copied A-Lian's sheet in two too; those four letters he could not copy, and so he put the original she had marked into the copy for the Uighur road, and at the foot of the other he traced those four letters after her; when he came to the Nun, he traced that tail three times, and none came out like hers, and in the end he wrote a line of small characters beside it: This is a copy; the original mark is in the Uighur road copy.

The day the caravan left the city, Yonan did not go to see it off. He said the morning office in the front hall, and when he had said it went to sit at the door of the west wing and write in the sand tray. That day what he wrote was not *heart*; Jingjing walked past him and glanced down: in the sand tray were two characters, *ruanruo*, weakness; the *ruan* still written all in pieces, the cart radical and the yawn radical parting company.

---

That summer, Yonan did not go to the West Market again.

He said that until the letter came back he would not go; to go would be not to wait for the Patriarchate's word. As before he washed his hands three times a day, ate half a flatbread at each meal, went at evening to light the lamp at the gate, and when it was lit stood under the lamp looking toward the ward gate; and when he looked, he stood longer than before.

But every seven days that porter came once to the monastery. The first time he came carrying a bamboo basket with a cloth over it, stood at the monastery gate, and said the old mother at the West Market cake shop had sent him with it for the old elder who treated the sick. Yonan lifted the cloth: in the basket were four sesame cakes, still warm, and under the cloth a small wooden board with a few Syriac letters written on it in charcoal, spelling the sound of Tang: *re de*, hot. Yonan took that board in his hand, turned it over and looked at the back, and there was nothing on the back; he turned it back and looked at the sound of those two words, the tail of the Nun flicking upward. From then on, every Lord's Day the porter came. In the basket sometimes cakes, sometimes a jar of mutton soup, the jar wrapped in cotton wadding so that when it reached the monastery it was still too hot to hold; the words on the board were different every time. Once it said: it's turned cool. Once it said: the child can walk—meaning the porter's child. Once it said: Old Wang has paid. Once nothing was written, only a hooked curve and three dots drawn. On the days the basket came, if Lingbao was playing at the monastery gate, he would rush to take it. Lingbao was eight that year; his Syriac letters had been taught him by Narsai; that year after a meal Narsai had promised Yisi that when the child learned his scriptures he would come and teach him, and he was as good as his word: from the age of four, every other day, Lingbao went under the window of the west wing to learn a letter, and if he got it wrong Narsai made him write it twenty times in the sand tray. Lingbao knew them all, but could not much spell. Once he rushed to take the basket, ran to the west wing with the small board, and read it letter by letter to Narsai; when he had read it, what it spelled was Tang, and he was taken aback himself, and asked: Elder, this is Tang; how is it written in our letters? Narsai took the board, looked at the front, turned it over and looked at the back—there was nothing on the back—turned it back again, returned it to Lingbao, and told him to take it to Elder Yonan; someone had sent it to Elder Yonan. Lingbao took it and asked Yonan who that person was; Yonan said someone who baked cakes. Lingbao said: someone who bakes cakes knows our letters too; then the people of the West Market all know our monastery's letters. Yonan, hearing it, smiled; and this the monastery's people seldom saw: when he smiled, the wrinkles of that smooth face all crowded to the corners of his eyes.

Shiyiniang went to that shop in the West Market in the summer. She kept the gruel kitchen's accounts, and the gruel kitchen was on the north side of the West Market; one day when she had done the accounts she walked south, saying she was going to buy cakes, came to the door of that shop, saw that board, stood a moment, and went in. She told A-Lian she was Yisi's wife; A-Lian was kneading dough; her hands stopped; she looked her up and down once and said: you're Shiyiniang of the Pei family; you keep the accounts at the gruel kitchen; I've seen you; you write a good hand. The two of them sat down to talk, and talked a good half hour, all of it about cakes: how to mix the jujube-paste filling, how to bank the oven, whether rubbing your hands with lard did any good after all. When she left, Shiyiniang said her little one loved jujube-paste cakes; A-Lian wrapped four in a lotus leaf and flatly refused money, and Shiyiniang flatly insisted on paying; the two of them pushed back and forth a long while, and in the end Shiyiniang pressed the money under that accounts board and ran. Back in the side courtyard she told Yisi she had seen her: that woman had scars all over her hands, spoke slowly, as slowly as you, and her eyes were exactly like Jingjing's. Yisi said he knew. Shiyiniang said: you know, and you never told me. Yisi said he hadn't seen her; it had been thirty years; he remembered only her standing at the side courtyard door with the child in her arms, and that day it was raining. Shiyiniang, hearing it, said nothing more, took out the four jujube-paste cakes, one for Lingbao, one for Yisi, one kept for Jingjing, and the one left over she thought about, wrapped in a cloth, and had Lingbao take to Elder Yonan's room.

Yonan kept all those boards, one by one, stacking them on the windowsill of his room, stacking them till autumn: ten-odd boards.

Once more cakes than usual were sent, and Yonan brought them to the table and, in his old way, broke each into several pieces and passed them round one by one: passed to Ishaq, and Ishaq took one; passed to Babai, and Babai took one; when he came to Narsai his hand paused. Twenty years ago on the road to Balkh he had held one out once, and Narsai had not taken it, and since then he had never held one out toward Narsai again. This time his hand went out and did not draw back, but stayed held out like that. Narsai looked at the piece of cake, looked at the hand, reached out and took it, did not eat it, and tucked it into his sleeve. Everyone at the table saw, and no one made a sound; Ishaq bent his head to his gruel and slurped it noisily. Yonan, eating his cake, still ate only half, and the other half he crumbled and laid under the locust tree outside the monastery gate for the sparrows. Ishaq saw it and said: Yonan, she sent it for you. Yonan said: half is for her. Ishaq did not understand, and asked no more.

In the ninth month of that year something happened at the East Market. A Uighur stabbed a man in broad daylight at the East Market, was seized by the people of the market and sent into the Wannian county jail; the Uighur leader brought a band of men, broke into the county jail, rescued him by force, and wounded several jailers besides. It was told fiercely round the wards: the Uighurs meant to set fire to the city of Chang'an; the Son of Heaven didn't dare do anything. When the monastery's people heard it, no one said anything; they all looked at Jingjing. Jingjing could not sleep those few days; he got up at night and opened that register of the believers of the various prefectures and turned to the last page; on that page were recorded the days the monastery had written to the Patriarchate: fourth month of the tenth year of Dali, one copy by the Uighur road, one by the sea road. He reckoned that the An caravan should by now have passed Beiting, should be at Suyab; the Uighurs breaking a jail in Chang'an—would they stop caravans on the steppe too? He could not reckon it. Yisi saw the lamp lit in his room, came over to look, saw him going through that register, and asked what he was reckoning. He said he was reckoning where the letter had got to. Yisi said: what can't be reckoned, don't reckon; go to sleep. He said that when his Pa was waiting for Uncle's letters, he had reckoned too. Yisi stood at the door and said: I did reckon; reckoning was no use; a letter goes on its own. He went over and blew out the lamp on Jingjing's table.

---

The reply came in the second month of the eleventh year of Dali.

That day there was a spring snow, not heavy, flakes drifting down one by one and melting as they touched the ground. A young deacon who had come back with a Sogdian caravan, sent by the Patriarchate, came in at the monastery gate covered in wind and snow, took an oilcloth packet from his coat, and gave it to Elder Azarel. In the oilcloth packet were several letters: for Azarel, for the monastery, for Yisi, and at the very bottom one with Yonan's name on the wrapper. The Uighur road copy had arrived first; the reply had not waited for a caravan; Isaac had carried it in his coat, changing Uighur post-horses all the way from west of Beiting, and come east with the Uighur embassy that was going to Chang'an; Elder Azarel reckoned the days and said the monastery had never got a letter to and from the Patriarchate so fast.

The deacon said that Patriarch Hnanisho had lived most of this past year and more in Baghdad, the Caliph's new city, with round walls and straight streets. The deacon was called Isaac, twenty-six or -seven, from Basra in the south of the Two Rivers; he had studied scripture five years at the Patriarchate, and the Patriarch had had him come east with the caravan, first to bring the letters and second to see what the church in Chang'an was really like, and when he went back he was to tell the Patriarch face to face. He lodged at the monastery, in the back courtyard, and first thing the next morning ran to the west wing to watch the translating: watched Jingjing write Tang characters, watched Babai read the Syriac scripture, watched Narsai trim pens, and when he had watched asked Jingjing: these Tang scriptures, not one person at the Patriarchate can read them; who's to examine them from now on? Jingjing said Elder Marya examined them: he read a sentence of Tang and put it back into Syriac and read that to the elder. Isaac said: then the elder's examining your Syriac, not your Tang. Jingjing said yes; and so when he put it back he dared not skimp one word. Isaac laughed when he heard it and said the Patriarchate was like this too now: the Caliph's palace wanted the medical books of the Greeks translated, and the translators were all our people. He said there was an old physician in the palace in charge of medicines, one of our people, called Isa, who had learned medicine at the hospital of Gundeshapur; all the Caliph's medicines he made up; when the Patriarch had business to ask of the Caliph, he first went to sit in Isa's house. He said old Master Isa, hearing that in Chang'an there was a monastery where someone had translated the names of medicines into Tang, had made a point of asking the Patriarch what the Tang over there called myrrh. Jingjing said the Tang didn't call it anything; it wasn't in the Tang herbals; the *hu* merchants of the West Market called it *moyao*. Isaac said then he'd go back and tell old Master Isa just that.

Yonan took that letter; the wax seal on the wrapper was new, pressed with the Patriarch's seal; he felt the seal with his fingertip, did not open it, put it inside his robe, went back to his own room, and shut the door. He stayed in the room from the hour of *wu* until dark, and no one went to knock.

After dark the door opened. Yonan came out and went to the door of the west wing. Jingjing was inside putting away scrolls, putting the half-turned drafts in order sheet by sheet and pressing them under paperweights. Yonan stood at the door holding the opened letter, and said: "Read it to her for me. In Tang."

Jingjing's hands stopped. He raised his head and looked at Yonan; Yonan stood in the dark at the door, his face not to be seen, only the few sheets of the letter in his hand to be seen, white. Jingjing put the paperweight in place, stood up, and said he would.

---

A-Lian came the next day at the hour of *shen*.

The porter took word to the West Market; Shiyiniang had him take a message that by the time the reading was done it would be late, and she would stay the night in the side courtyard. When she came, the sun was already sinking west, and the lamp at the monastery gate was not yet lit. She stood before the stone block, stood a while, and did not come in at once. She wore a clean blue cloth jacket, her hair put up anew, the wooden pin changed for another, no flour on her hands, washed spotless. She looked at the bronze oil dish on the stone block, looked at the cross-shaped hollow chiselled in the lintel, bowed her head, and stepped over the threshold. It was the first time in thirty years she had come through this gate.

They were in the front hall. Two lamps were lit in the hall; the wooden cross hung on the east wall. Yonan sat on one side and Yisi on the other; Shiyiniang had come too, and sat behind Yisi; she had asked to come herself, saying that with a woman coming in and the hall all men, if she sat there it would be easier for her. Elder Azarel did not come, but sent Elder Samaan, who sat by the door with his account book open on his knees. Jingjing stood beneath the cross holding the sheets of the letter. A-Lian came in and stopped near the door. Yonan rose and brought her to a cushion, and she sat down, in the place of the last row. Shiyiniang moved over and sat beside her, reached out and took her hand; her hand was ice cold; Shiyiniang held it and did not let go.

Jingjing looked down at the letter. Syriac, line after line, right to left. He knew the Patriarch's hand: very steady, not large, each stroke finished very cleanly. He began to read, reading and turning it into Tang in his head, saying it out sentence by sentence.

"Hnanisho, servant of the Lord, by the Lord's mercy unworthily holding the patriarchal throne, at Baghdad, to our brother Yonan at Chang'an: the peace of the Lord be with you.

"Your letter I have read three times. The first time, I read as Patriarch, according to the rule; the second, as your brother—you and I took our vows in one monastery; you were twenty that year and I eighteen; fifty years ago; the third, as a man as old as you.

"You say you broke your vow and hid it thirty years. The hiding is your sin. To speak it now is the Lord's grace. The canons say that one who has vowed and broken the vow shall be cut off. But the canons also say that the penitent the Church shall take back. I have consulted twice with several bishops, and we have resolved: we do not cut you off; your standing as former bishop of Chang'an is from this day removed; henceforth in Chang'an you are only an old brother, with no charge and no office.

"When a man takes a vow before the Lord, the Lord remembers; when a man is weak before the Lord, the Lord remembers that too. Alaha pitieth our weakness; for he knoweth our frame; he remembereth that we are dust.

"I release you from your vow."

When Jingjing had read this far, he paused. The hall was very still; a lamp-wick sputtered. He did not raise his head, but read on.

"You might have stayed at the Patriarchate and copied scripture in peace into your old age; instead, through fire and sword, you walked ten thousand *li* to the east. I have heard that the people in Chang'an did not scatter altogether amid the fighting, and the scriptures remain; you, there, gave your strength to it too.

"Your letter speaks of the child. There is a passage in the letter whose hand is the same as elsewhere, but whose words are not like those elsewhere; I guess the child wrote it himself. He writes briefly, asks for nothing, and at the end says not to give thought to him. When I read that line, I put the letter down and walked once round the courtyard before I came back. I hear he translates scripture in Chang'an now. May the Lord keep his hands and his eyes. The Church will remember his words; henceforth if he has any matter to write of, let him write it himself; I know his words."

Jingjing's voice did not change; still the same, saying it out word by word, not fast, not slow. But when he had read this passage, his fingers pinched the edge of the sheet once, and pinched a crease into it. In the last row A-Lian raised her head and looked at him. He did not look at her, but read on.

"Greet for me our sister A-Lian. I do not know her face, nor can I speak her tongue. I have read her sheet; those are her own words; I can read them. I hear she knows our letters. I write her name below, in our letters. If she can read it, let her know that in Baghdad there is an old man who prays for her every day.

"I am old, and cannot walk far; the roads of the new city are very straight, and I still often lose my way. The peace of the Lord be with you all."

---

When Jingjing had finished, he turned the sheet over. At the very bottom of the sheet, below the Patriarch's signature, another line had been begun, and on it were written four letters, right to left, Alaph, Lamadh, Yudh, Nun, the tail of the Nun trailing properly downward.

He looked at those four letters a while, came down from beneath the cross, went to the last row, and squatted down before A-Lian. He held the sheet in front of her, pointed to that line with his finger, and said: "Sister, this is your name."

A-Lian bowed her head and looked at those four letters, put out her finger, and traced along those four letters, right to left, one by one, traced very slowly, saying: "Alaph, Lamadh, Yudh, Nun." She said it with a Chang'an accent. When she came to the last letter, her finger stopped on that downward-trailing tail and flicked up once, as if correcting it for him.

Jingjing squatted before her, motionless. He was very close to her, so close he could see the scars on the back of her hand, see the lines at the corners of her eyes, see her eyes; her eyes were very dark. Shiyiniang sat beside her, holding her other hand, holding it till her knuckles were white. When A-Lian had finished saying them, she raised her head and looked at him. Jingjing stood up, folded the letter, turned and went back, held it out in both hands to Yonan, and said: "Elder, it's read."

Elder Samaan, by the door, finished writing that page of the account book, shut it, stood up, and went out first. As he passed A-Lian he stopped a moment, nodded to her, and said something in Syriac; A-Lian could not understand it and looked at Shiyiniang; Shiyiniang could not understand either and looked at Jingjing. Jingjing said the elder had said: peace be with you.

---

Yisi stood up, went to the door, and pushed it open. It was full dark now; there had been a fall of snow outside, a thin white layer on the ground; the lamp at the monastery gate Yonan had lit before coming in; a little snow had fallen on the edge of the bronze dish and melted to water, and the yellow lamplight shone on the stone block before the gate. Someone was standing under the gallery: Narsai; how long he had been standing there, no one knew; snow lay on his shoulders; when he saw the door open and saw Yisi, he said nothing at all, turned, and went off toward the west wing. Yisi turned and said to those in the hall that the ward gates were shut; tonight A-Lian would stay in the side courtyard; Shiyiniang had already made up the bed. Yonan saw A-Lian to the door of the side courtyard, stood a moment at the door, and went back to his own room.

Jingjing did not see her there. He went out alone by the small door at the side of the hall to the locust tree outside the monastery gate. The ward was very dark; the branches of the locust tree were bare, with a little unmelted snow hanging on them. Over in the side courtyard, a wall between, he heard Shiyiniang speaking—could not hear what—and then a slower voice answering, and when it had answered, coughing a few times. He went to the well, let down the wooden bucket, drew up a bucket of water—very cold, a thin crust of ice on the rim of the bucket. He scooped up a double handful and drank it down; scooped another and drank it down.

That night in the side courtyard Shiyiniang and A-Lian slept in one bed in the inner room, with Lingbao between them, still kicking off the covers in his sleep. The two women talked across the child, very softly. A-Lian asked Shiyiniang how old he was when he learned to walk, what his first word was. Shiyiniang said when she married into the family he was already twenty; of when he was small she knew only what Yisi had told her: that he didn't walk till two, walked late, and once he walked he ran, and when he fell he didn't cry; his first word was in the country speech of Balkh: he asked for water, hot. A-Lian laughed once in the dark, and asked again whether that Sogdian mother of his had been good to him. Shiyiniang said Yisi said that wife stuffed his padded coat thicker than she stuffed Yisi's; once Yisi said she played favourites, and she said: the child's small, you're big. A-Lian listened, and asked no more; after a good while, when Shiyiniang thought she was asleep, she heard her say: then that's good. Yisi lay in the outer room without moving, as if asleep; Shiyiniang knew he was not asleep; when he slept he snored, and that night not one snore came from the outer room.

That night Jingjing's stomach hurt half the night; he curled up alone under the quilt biting the corner of it, and did not make a sound. As soon as it was light the next day he came out of his room to go to the hall, and at the monastery gate met A-Lian; she was hurrying back to the West Market to open the shop, and Shiyiniang was seeing her out. The two of them passed each other at the gate; A-Lian stopped and nodded to him; he nodded too; and she went. Shiyiniang glanced at him, asked nothing, turned and went to the kitchen, brought a bowl of ginger broth, and put it in his hands. That day he went to the west wing to translate as usual; Babai asked why his face was so white; he said he hadn't slept well.

Narsai had been there since early that day. On the board under the window lay a sheet of paper, an offcut, covered with characters, Tang characters, all the same character, *vow*, one after another, several dozen of them, written like two crossed spears, just as twenty years ago under the lamp in Xizhou, not one written any better. In the last few lines the character grew smaller and smaller, crowded into a corner of the paper, and the very last was only half written: the upper part, the *zhe* of *break*, was finished; the *yan* of *speech* beneath it was not written. When Jingjing came in, Narsai was folding that paper up and putting it away in his sleeve; seeing him, he said nothing, took up the knife, and went on trimming pens.

Note: In the ninth month of the tenth year of Dali, a Uighur stabbed a man in broad daylight at the East Market and was arrested and held by Wannian county; the Uighur chief Chixin broke into the county jail, freed the prisoner and wounded jailers—according to the *Jiu Tang shu*, "Annals of Daizong" and "Account of the Uighurs" (the Uighur plundering of the wards and markets in the first month of the seventh year of Dali in Chapter 39 is a separate incident). Patriarch Hnanisho II (Ningshu) reigned roughly 773–780 and went to Baghdad; the formal move of the patriarchal see to Baghdad came under Timothy I (to be checked). The treatment of a monk who broke his vow is the novel's invention; the details of the canons of the Church of the East are to be checked. The Uighur road out beyond the frontier by Fengzhou, Beiting and Suyab, and the sea road from Guangzhou to the sea of the Tazi, follow the general routes of the time; the specific caravan journeys are invented. A-Lian's statement, Jingjing's account of himself, and the text of Hnanisho's reply are the novel's invention.
