# Volume Eight　The Daqin Monastery

## Chapter 49　The Stele Is Raised

> Set up in the Great Tang, the second year of Jianzhong, the year being *Zuo'e*, the seventh day of the month *Taicou*, on the great *Yaosenwen* day; at the time the Lord of the Law, the monk Ningshu, had charge of the Luminous congregation of the East.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

After Lü Xiuyan agreed, the monastery's people still came into the hall every day at the hour of *wu*; only the words of the prayer had changed. The stone was brought into the monastery in the tenth month. It was a blue-black stone, quarried in the Northern Mountains; twenty-odd men pushed it on rollers all the way from the Jinguang Gate to the Yining Ward, pushed it a whole day. Stood upright, the stone was a long way taller than a man, more than three feet wide and a foot thick. The old stonemason first chiselled out the stele's head, with two coiling dragons on it, and between the two dragons, as Jingjing had said, a space left empty. Samaan came to look once, walked round the stone, tilted his head back to look at the two dragons, and said the dragons were someone else's too. The stonemason could not understand him, and thinking he found fault with the carving, hastily said this was the rule; stele heads were all like this; of a thousand steles in the city of Chang'an, a thousand had dragons. Samaan did not argue with him, but tilted his head back again to look at the empty space between the two dragons. In that space a cross was afterwards carved, and under the cross clouds, and under the clouds a lotus. Samaan came to look once more, and when he had looked, made an entry in his account book—the day the cross was finished—and went back.

---

Lü Xiuyan came in the eleventh month to write in vermilion. Writing in vermilion meant mixing cinnabar with glue and writing it straight onto the stone; when it was written, the stonemason cut it stroke by stroke following the red characters; a mistake could not be put right. Lü Xiuyan put up a wooden scaffold before the stone and stood on it to write; he wrote very slowly, only a hundred-odd characters a day. It was cold that winter; the cinnabar froze; he had a brazier lit under the scaffold, and after writing a stretch came down to warm his hands. The monastery's people, when they had nothing to do, all came to watch him write; he paid no attention to anyone, but wrote. When he came to the three characters *A-luo-he*, he paused a moment, as though thinking whether to write these three characters heavier or lighter, and in the end wrote them the same as the rest. When he came to the line of signature at the foot of the stele, "Written by Lü Xiuyan, Gentleman for Court Discussion, formerly Administrator of Granaries of Taizhou," he wrote it more slowly than any other line. He wrote twenty-odd days, and when he had written the last line, came down from the scaffold, stepped back a few paces, looked it over from beginning to end, and told Jingjing that the two sides and the bottom were still empty. Jingjing said that was where *hu* writing would be carved, the names of the monastery's people, with Tang names beneath the *hu* writing; the Tang names he begged the Master to write; the *hu* writing his Pa would write. Lü Xiuyan said the *hu* writing he could not do; the Tang names he would write.

---

Yisi wrote the Syriac at the beginning of the twelfth month. He did not stand on the scaffold, but had the stonemason first grind smooth the surface of the bottom section of the stone, carried over a low stool himself, and sat at the foot of the stele to write. His letters were still big and clumsy; written on stone, big and clumsy turned out to be a virtue. He wrote first the date, "In the year one thousand and ninety-two of the Greeks," then "in the days of the Father of Fathers, Mar Hnanisho, Catholicos Patriarch," then Jingjing's name, Adam, and after it the standing the Patriarch's letter had given him; and when he came to himself, he wrote chorepiscopus too, as the Patriarch's letter had it; Samaan watched beside him, and went back and made an entry in his account book: The titles written on the stele all come from the Patriarch's letter; the laying on of hands has not been done. Yisi wrote on: wrote Azarel, Marya, Samaan, wrote Babai and Sabrisho, wrote the monastery's priests, deacons and monks, wrote the households sent in from Luoyang, Lingzhou and Zhouzhi, one name for the head of each household, name after name, writing onto both sides of the stele, more than seventy of them. When he came to the guardian Sargis he paused; Sargis had fallen sick with a winter chill the winter before last and had not lived to the spring; he wrote him in nonetheless. When he came to Yonan he paused too, and wrote "Yonan, monk"; the words "former bishop of Chang'an" he did not write.

Last he wrote himself; it was the last line of writing at the bottom of the stele, and he thought several days before he settled how to write it. What he wrote was: "Yazdbozid, priest and chorepiscopus of Kumdan the royal city, son of the late Milis, priest of Balkh, a city of Tokharistan, set up this tablet of stone." Yazdbozid was his own name; no one in the city of Chang'an had ever called him by it. When he came to "the late Milis," his hand stopped. He remembered the night he left Balkh at seventeen, when his father called him into the room, took the wooden cross from his own neck and hung it on his, without a word, only pressing his hand on his head. That cross now hung on Jingjing's neck. He dipped the brush in the cinnabar and wrote on: Balkh.

---

As Lü Xiuyan finished a section, the stonemasons cut a section after him; all told they cut forty days. The old stonemason and his son had come from the Northern Mountains with the stone. The old one was past sixty, the skin of his hands like old bark; when he cut characters he put his eyes very close, the tip of his nose covered in stone dust; the young one was twenty or so, and his work was to whet the blades, sweep the dust, hand over the mallet. The two of them put up a straw shed before the stele, cut by day and slept in the shed at night, saying they were afraid the stone would crack with frost and must keep watch on it. The monastery sent them two meals a day, one of flatbread and one of broth; the broth Ishaq stewed; the old stonemason drank it the first time, and the next day asked whether there was any more.

The sound of the carving rang through the monastery the whole winter, *ding*, *ding*, neither hurried nor slow, like someone knocking at a door far away. When the monastery's people said the evening office, in the middle of it they would hear a *ding* from the front courtyard. At first they all found it noisy; after ten-odd days they were used to it; one night the old stonemason was sick and rested an evening, and the monastery's people could not sleep instead.

Jingjing went once a day to look, standing by the straw shed watching the old stonemason: one stroke of the blade, and under the vermilion character a white line showed; another, and the white line went deeper; another, and a stroke had come out. When a character was finished, the young stonemason swept away the dust with a bristle brush, and the character was on the stone: blue-black ground, white grooves. What he had written was turning, character by character, into stone. One day the old stonemason came to *A-luo-he* and stopped and asked whether these three characters were a man's name. Jingjing thought and said: you could say so. The old stonemason cut them, ran his finger over the three characters once, and said a man's name should be cut deeper. Jingjing did not stop him.

On the twentieth-odd day of the carving, something went wrong. That day when Jingjing went to look, the old stonemason was squatting at the foot of the stele, his face white, the young one standing beside him not daring to make a sound. The old stonemason had been cutting the character *jing*, luminous, and at the vertical stroke of the *jing*, capital, beneath it his hand slipped, and the blade-point dragged aside once, dragging out a fine groove, not long, half a fingernail, but on the blue-black stone glaring white. The old stonemason said he had cut steles forty years, and a mistake like this he had made only twice; characters on stone, once cut wrong, could not be helped: grind it, and a whole layer has to be ground away, and the whole line of characters goes shallow with it; patch it, and the patching powder isn't the same colour as the stone, and anyone can see it at a glance. He said this time he would take no wages; he had wronged them.

Jingjing squatted to look at the groove. It was cut beneath the *jing* of *capital* in the character *jing*, like a fine whisker grown beside that vertical. He measured beside the groove with his fingernail and asked the old stonemason whether he could cut along this groove and make it into a dot. The old stonemason stared and said the character *jing* had no such dot. Jingjing said: on the stele, in one place—"a luminous star announced the blessing"—let the character *jing* there have an extra dot beneath; when the Tang read it they'll know it's *jing*; one extra dot, they'll take it the writer's hand shook once. Master Lü's hand never shakes, but a carver's hand can; people shake; let it shake once. The old stonemason looked at him, looked at the groove, took up his blade, and cut down along the groove, made it into a small dot, and when it was cut could not himself see that it was a mistake. He said he had cut forty years, and this was the first time anyone had told him to cut a mistake into a character. Jingjing said: that dot wanted to stay there itself.

That night, in the roll in the west wing where he recorded things, he wrote a line: On the stele, the character *jing* has an extra dot; the stonemason's slip; not ground away.

---

In the twentieth-odd days of the twelfth month the characters were finished. The last cut were the nine large characters on the stele's head, "Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom," three lines of three characters, under the cross; the old stonemason cut two a day, took five days, and when they were cut sat in the shed resting a whole day, doing nothing, only looking. The Syriac letters Yisi had written the young stonemason cut; he could not read them, and traced the vermilion strokes one by one; when he came to Yisi's line he asked Yisi what this line said, and Yisi said it said: my father; and the young stonemason cut a little more slowly.

After the New Year, on the fifth of the first month, father and son took down the straw shed, swept the stone dust from the ground, swept it three times, and then sluiced it with water. The old stonemason stood before the stele and bowed to it. Jingjing asked whether he bowed every time he finished; he said yes; Jingjing asked why; he thought and said: once it's cut, it's not mine any more.

---

On the seventh of the first month, Jingjing was up before light.

He went first to the gate to light the lamp, then carried that small lantern to the front courtyard. The stele stood in the very middle of the front courtyard, a ring of space cleared around it; the stone was blue-black, its colour not to be seen by lantern light; only the cut white grooves could be seen, line after line, top to bottom. He raised the lantern and looked from the cross on the stele's head, down to the nine large characters, down to the first line of the text—Behold, the eternal, true and still—and on down, to Taizong, to Alopen, to the sentence he had written himself, Alopen, Great Virtue of the kingdom of Daqin, to the character *jing* with its extra dot in "a luminous star announced the blessing." He squatted down again and looked at the Syriac at the foot and on the two sides of the stele, his Pa's writing, name after name: Yonan, Old Jacob, Old Master Jiao, Sargis, those seven households of Zhouzhi with two dropped in the reciting. A-Lian's name was not there. She had been baptized; her baptismal name was Susanna; his Pa had asked him whether to carve it; he had said carve it; and later his Pa carved it beneath Yonan's name, only the baptismal name, not the words A-Lian; the Tang could not read Syriac, and those who could would not ask who Susanna was. He reached out and touched those few letters; the stone was icy cold.

There were footsteps behind him: Yisi, coming to light the lamps in the hall, passing through the front courtyard; seeing him squatting at the foot of the stele, he stopped. Father and son, one standing and one squatting; Yisi said: it's cold, the stone's cold; don't touch it long. Jingjing said he knew, and stood up. Yisi said again: today you read. Jingjing said Elder Azarel would read. Yisi said: the elder reads the scripture; you read the stele; the stele is yours.

That day was the great *Yaosenwen* day, the day of the offering once in seven. By the monastery's rule, worship was held in the hall; Azarel said worship would be held as usual, and when it was done, everyone would go out to the front courtyard. By daylight the front courtyard was already full of people. The monastery's people stood at the front: elders, priests, deacons, monks, all in clean white robes; of the twenty-four guard brothers of those days nine were left, their hair all white, standing at the very edge; Sargis was gone, and his son stood in his old place, taller even than his father. Behind them the faithful: Sogdian merchants, old Persians, Tang, a few children of mixed Tang and *hu* blood squeezing this way and that until the grown-ups pressed them down. Xu Jing came too, his back a little bent now; as soon as he was in the gate he said he'd watched this stone being brought in, and halfway through saying it looked round and saw everyone looking at the stele and no one listening to him, and so he stopped too. Behind them again were the people of the ward—the vinegar-seller, the shoe-seller, those who came every morning for cakes under the locust tree at the gate—standing at the courtyard gate craning their necks to look in; a few half-grown children had climbed the courtyard wall and sat astride its top, one on each side, like a row of sparrows. Official Pei, leaning on a walking stick, stood in the crowd and did not push forward. Li Su stood in the back row, looked at the sky, looked at the line of the date on the stele, and nodded. Lü Xiuyan did not come.

When the sun showed above the eastern courtyard wall, Azarel went up to the stele and recited a passage of scripture in Syriac; he was old, his voice was not clear, and those behind heard only a humming. When he had finished, he made the sign of the cross, and of those behind some made it and some did not, and some made it halfway, not knowing whether to touch the left shoulder or the right first, and stopped there. Then Azarel turned round and nodded to Jingjing.

Jingjing went to the foot of the stele. He did not read the whole text; the whole text was more than seventeen hundred characters, and by the time it was read the sun would be overhead; what he read was the last passage, the hymn of praise. Those characters he had copied from the fourth month to the sixth, copied who knew how many times, and knew by heart long ago; still he tilted his head back and read from the characters on the stone. He read very slowly; the people of the ward could not understand, but could hear it was Tang, could hear it was fine writing, four characters to a line, like someone walking step by step, and at the end changing to longer lines; even those children on the courtyard wall stopped their noise. When he came to the last few lines, his voice faltered once. He thought of those three wooden boards in the corner of the west wing, of the two graves outside the Kaiyuan Gate with a foot of ground between them, of that year in Ctesiphon, Yonan looking at him without even blinking.

"How broad is the Way, how fine its answering! Forced to give it a name, we set forth the Three in One. The lord can act, the subject can record; we raise this great stele to praise the supreme good."

When he had finished, Marya was the first to weep aloud; when he wept, a few old believers behind wept with him; there was an old Persian, his beard white as wool, who wept and at the same time recited scripture loudly in Syriac, louder than Azarel. The few Tang believers did not know whether to weep or laugh, looked at each other, and one laughed first, and then several laughed, and laughing, their eyes went red too. Samaan tilted his head back and looked at the cross on the stele's head. Shiyiniang, holding Lingbao by the hand, stood among the faithful; Lingbao was fourteen, nearly as tall as his Pa, and stood on tiptoe reading the Tang characters on the stele one by one; when he came to the words "great benefactor," he turned his head to look at his Pa.

Yisi had stood by the stele the whole time; Azarel had called him, and he had shaken his head, and stood beside the Syriac he himself had written. He wore an old grey robe, not the purple; Jingjing had asked him that morning, and he said the purple robe was kept for meeting officials. The passage about himself on the stele he did not go to look at; what he looked at was that line of Syriac at the foot of the stele, his father's name, Balkh. In the crowd someone said softly: that's the Great Virtue Yi, of Lord Guo's command; and those beside him stood on tiptoe to look this way, at the scar over his left brow. Afterwards he moved away from before the stele, through the crowd, to the foot of the courtyard wall, raised his head, and saw a child sitting astride the top of the wall, ten or so, thin, his face red with cold; seeing him look up, the child shrank back in fright, thinking he would be scolded. Yisi took from his coat a sesame cake he had tucked in before coming out that morning, broke it in two, put one half back in his coat, and reached up and handed the other half to him, and said: slowly. The child held the cake as if he had not understood. Yisi said nothing more, but turned and walked away.

---

Toward noon someone came from the Guo mansion: an old servant of sixty-odd, in the blue livery of the Guo mansion, leading a donkey, who stood at the courtyard gate and asked which was the Great Virtue Yi. He bowed to Yisi and said the Lord was ill and could not come; the Lord had sent him to say one thing. He cleared his throat, as if afraid of getting it wrong, and recited it word by word: that a few days ago Official Pei had copied out the stele text and sent it into the mansion; the Lord had had it read to him; one line was written wrong, the line "though trusted even in the bedchamber"; the Lord said: what bedchamber was there in camp? In the tent there was one felt; he slept on it, and the others sat on the ground.

The faces of the few men behind Yisi all changed colour. Jingjing came over too. But Yisi laughed—laughed very lightly, only the corners of his mouth moved, and the scar on his left brow puckered with them; in thirty-odd years following him, the times Jingjing had seen him laugh like this could be counted on two hands. Yisi said: go back and tell the Lord the characters are carved already and can't be changed. The old servant bowed again, and led his donkey away.

Jingjing saw the old servant to the gate, and when he came back Yisi was still standing before the stele, looking at that line, "though trusted even in the bedchamber, he did not set himself apart in the ranks." Jingjing said that line was written from the patent of appointment the Guo mansion's administrative officer issued; of things in camp, those who write steles all write so. Yisi said he knew, and the Lord knew too; in what the Lord said, it wasn't the characters on the stele he was finding fault with. Jingjing asked: then what was the Lord saying? Yisi thought and said: he was saying that in all those years, he never went into the rear tent.

---

After the stele was set up, many people in the city of Chang'an came to look at it. The first month people came every day—believers and unbelievers—those who could read stood before the stele reading it character by character, those who could not walked once round it, touched it, and left. A few scholars came to copy the text, and when they had copied it stood talking it over, saying the writing was fine writing, only who was this Alaha? A monk came once too, stood before the stele half an hour, and when he had looked, gave a cold laugh and left.

Lü Xiuyan came on the evening of the day the stele was set up, before the closing drum had sounded. Jingjing came out to add oil to the lamp and saw a man standing before the stele in the front courtyard, holding a paper lantern: Lü Xiuyan. He said he hadn't come by day for fear of the crowd; when there are many people, everyone looks at the stele, and he looks at the characters; he wanted to see what his characters had been cut into. Holding the lantern, he looked line by line, looked very slowly; at one place he stopped and said this down-stroke was cut shallow; at another he said this hook was cut even better than he wrote it. When he came to the character *jing* in "a luminous star announced the blessing," he stopped and turned to look at Jingjing. Jingjing said the stonemason's hand had slipped while carving, and he had had the stonemason cut along it into a dot. Lü Xiuyan held the lantern close and said he had written characters all his life, and on a stele his characters had never once been wrong by a stroke; on this stele, there was one stroke not written by him. When he had said it he laughed himself, and said: it's just as well; the characters on this stele can count as written by two men; if later people say it's miswritten, say it was miscut; if they say it was miscut, say that dot wanted to stay there itself. Jingjing asked who had said that. Lü Xiuyan said he'd heard it from the old stonemason; before the stonemason left he went to Lü's house once to beg his pardon. When he had looked his fill, he asked whether the Great Virtue Yi was in; Jingjing went to the back courtyard and called Yisi out, and the two of them stood before the stele and said a few words about the White Deer Plain; as they talked the ward drum finished and the ward gates shut; Lü Xiuyan lodged that night in the west wing and left early the next morning.

Samaan went every morning to wipe the stele, with a cloth, from the bottom up; where he could not reach he stood on a small stool, and wiped more carefully than he kept the rule. The monastery's people said: Samaan was the one least willing to set up the stele at first, and now that it's set up, the one fondest of it is he too. Samaan heard it and did not argue, and went on wiping it every day.

---

At the end of the third month a caravan came from the west. In the caravan were two of the Luminous congregation, come out of Baghdad, who had travelled most of a year; entering Chang'an, the first thing they did was come to the monastery to worship, and when they had worshipped they ate in the back courtyard, and as they ate talked of the Patriarchate's affairs, and said Patriarch Hnanisho had died last year. Everyone at the table put down their bowls. The two thought everyone had long known; they looked at this one and that one, grew flustered, and began to stammer, saying Patriarch Hnanisho died last spring; after he died, the Patriarchate quarrelled several months over who should succeed; then they chose one, named Timothy, formerly bishop of a small place in the mountains east of Mosul; some said he bought it, some said he relied on the great officials in the city of Baghdad; some would not accept him, and still didn't.

Azarel asked: last spring? The two said last spring. Azarel said nothing more. Last spring the monastery had been deliberating on setting up the stele; Samaan had read from his account book that line—the Patriarch permits carving a stone; the reigning patriarch's name may be written; that day at the hour of *wu* the monastery prayed for Hnanisho, and Marya said the name Hnanisho with especial weight. By then, Hnanisho was already dead.

That evening a few elders deliberated in that small room in the back courtyard. Samaan spoke first: the stele says Hnanisho was reigning; on the day the stele was set up Hnanisho had been dead nearly a year; what the stele says is wrong; what's wrong should be corrected: grind away those two places and carve Timothy in their stead. Marya said once a stone is carved it can't be ground away; grind it, and the stele's spoiled. Samaan said spoiled or not, it must be ground; what's written on the stele, people will take as true. Sabrisho said the Timothy business was still only the word of two merchants; no letter had come from the Patriarchate; correct it wrongly and it'd look worse still. Babai said Hnanisho was on the stele not only in the Syriac but in the Tang too, "at the time the Lord of the Law, the monk Ningshu, had charge of the Luminous congregation of the East"; to change it, both would have to be changed, and Lü Xiuyan would have to be asked back. Azarel kept his eyes closed. They went on, one word from this one and one from that, and in the end all looked at Yisi.

Yisi sat by the door and waited till they had all spoken before he opened his mouth. He said: on the day the stele was set up, the patriarch we knew was Hnanisho. Samaan said: but he was already dead. Yisi said: we didn't know. Samaan said: not knowing, what's written on the stele is wrong. Yisi said: it's wrong, but it's not a lie. He said that day we prayed for him, truly prayed for him; we wrote his name, truly thinking he was there; what we didn't know, the stone didn't know either; later, when someone looks at this stele and sees this name, he'll know how far, that year, Chang'an was from Baghdad.

Azarel opened his eyes and looked at Jingjing, and the elders all looked at Jingjing too. Jingjing said: no change. He said the stele would not be changed, but a letter would be written; tonight he would write to the new patriarch; the letter would say Chang'an had set up a stele, and what was written on the stele was Patriarch Hnanisho; on the day the stele was set up Chang'an did not yet know the patriarch had changed; the one who wrote the stele was he, and the fault was his; that line on the stele, he begged the new patriarch to allow it to stay; its staying meant remembering how far apart Chang'an and Baghdad were, remembering that Chang'an had prayed for Hnanisho, and prayed for the new patriarch too. He said if the new patriarch would not allow it and said grind it, it would be ground, and he would pay for the grinding, deducted from his own clothing allowance until it was enough. Samaan took his hands out of his sleeves and wrote a line in his account book; what he wrote was: The *papash* Adam proposes that the name of Hnanisho on the stele not be changed; a letter to be sent to the new patriarch; await reply. When he had written it, he said: that accords with the rule.

That night Jingjing in the west wing wrote to Timothy. He did not know this man; he knew only that he lived in Baghdad, had been a bishop in the mountains east of Mosul, that some said he had bought the patriarchate and some said he had not. He spread out a sheet of parchment and wrote of Chang'an, of these hundred and forty-odd years, of the stele, of the name on the stele, of the day Chang'an prayed for Hnanisho; wrote very briefly. At the end he thought, and added one sentence: that the people of Chang'an now prayed for the new patriarch too; the people of Chang'an did not know him, just as he did not know Chang'an; when they prayed, it was his name they said.

---

When they had dispersed, Yisi did not go back to his room. He walked alone to the front courtyard; someone was standing before the stele: Narsai. He had come back from gathering the dead only after dark; his small cart leaned against the foot of the courtyard wall; he stood close before the stele, and by the little light that came in from the lamp at the gate read the Tang characters on the stele one by one, very slowly, his lips moving, saying aloud those he knew and skipping those he did not. The Tang characters he knew were all ones he had carved on the stakes these years: old man, old woman, child, frozen, sick, ward, wall, below. The characters on this stele he could not read all of, but some he knew. Yisi stopped behind him. Narsai came to one place and stopped, put out a finger, and touched a character: the *wang* of "the living and the dead," which he had carved on stakes many times. When he had touched it he turned his head, saw Yisi, nodded, and pushed his cart off toward the side gate.

A little of the light of the lamp at the gate came in and fell at the foot of the stele. Yisi went up to the stele, squatted, and found that line of Syriac, written by himself, written after the date: "in the days of the Father of Fathers, Mar Hnanisho, Catholicos Patriarch." The letters were cut very deep, and a little of the day's dust had gathered in the grooves. Yisi reached out and wiped the dust off those few letters with his sleeve.

---

Note: The end of the stele: "Written by Lü Xiuyan, Gentleman for Court Discussion, formerly Administrator of Granaries of Taizhou." The stele's Syriac date is "the year one thousand and ninety-two of the Greeks"; it calls Yisi "priest, chorepiscopus of Kumdan, son of the late Milis, Milis being priest of Balkh, a city of Tokharistan," and Jingjing (Adam) "priest, chorepiscopus, and *papash* of Sinistan"; there are more than seventy Syriac names on the sides of the stele; in the novel their titles come from Hnanisho's letter, and the laying on of hands is told in a later chapter. The stele was set up on the seventh day of the first month of the second year of Jianzhong (4 February 781), and its text says "at the time the Lord of the Law, the monk Ningshu, had charge of the Luminous congregation of the East"; Hnanisho was already dead, and Timothy I had been elected in 780. Guo Ziyi's "though trusted even in the bedchamber, he did not set himself apart in the ranks" is from the stele. The extra dot on the character *jing* on the stele and the stonemason's slip are the novel's invention.
