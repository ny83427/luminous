# Volume Six　The Shuofang Army

## Chapter 30　Du Huan

> The kingdom of Daqin rules the Sea of Coral to the south and reaches the Mountains of All Treasures to the north; to the west it looks toward the flowering forests of the immortals, and to the east it borders on the long winds and the Weak Water.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

In the second month of the second year of Shangyuan, Jingjing was to travel west again, this time to report to the Patriarchate on the monastery's behalf.

The business in the Liquan Ward was nearly half a year behind them. Yisi had written the Patriarchate a very long letter; he took seven days to write it, and when it was written spent seven more days revising it. In the letter he wrote how the eleven Pure Ones had gathered, how the old keeper of the fire had died, how the Bureau of Sacrifices and the Court of State Ceremonial had questioned him, how the Metropolitan Prefecture had judged and the Ministry of Justice reviewed it: one strangled, one exiled to Lingnan, nine given three years' servitude. He wrote too how many people the monastery had now, how many Tang, how many *hu*; how many *shi* of gruel they gave out in a year, how many dead they had buried, how far the hall had got. There were a few other matters he wrote several times, and each time it came out wrong, and in the end he simply put one line at the end of the letter: Chang'an's affairs cannot be made clear in a letter; I am sending someone to tell you face to face.

Those matters he had gone over one by one with the seven teachers in the side room. The first: Chang'an had no bishop; when someone in the monastery had done grave wrong, who in the end decided whether he was cut off or kept? In Baaluo's case they had talked till midnight and come to no conclusion, and if it happened again, they still would not. The second: the purple robe the Son of Heaven had granted. He had written to the Patriarchate about it in the first year of Qianyuan, and to this day there had been no answer; the robe stayed locked in the chest. The third: the old believers of the ward, at worship, sang hymns of praise in Tang words handed down from early years; Narsai said the Patriarchate had no such rule; Sabrisho said keep singing them for now; and neither had persuaded the other. The fourth: the portraits of the five sage emperors General Gao had sent all those years ago—once the hall was built, were they to be hung, and where? These four matters, Yisi said, they need not decide for the Patriarchate; only make clear what Chang'an was like, and however the Patriarchate settled them, so they would do.

Whom to send, the monastery debated for several days too. The seven teachers were all past fifty, two of them past sixty, and could not stand the wind on the Pamirs. Yisi himself could not get away: the Liquan business had only just passed, the censor would not necessarily let it drop there, and someone in the monastery had to stand firm; the hall had had its roof beam raised last ninth month, and tiles were to go on when spring opened; and besides, he held an acting office as Director of the Palace Administration, and to leave the realm for the lands of the Tazi he would first have to petition the throne, and whether it would be allowed was not known, and even if it were, by the time approval came down the caravan would have been missed.

In the end it was an old man named Marcus who came of his own accord and said he would go.

Marcus was an elder of the old Persian monastery in the Liquan Ward; he had come to Chang'an in the Kaiyuan years and lived thirty years in the Liquan Ward, longer even than Yisi. He was Persian, small, a little stooped, his Tang extremely fluent, with the twang of the West Market in it, and when he haggled over prices he was slicker than plenty of Tang. In the year Chang'an fell he had hidden in the Southern Mountains with an old customer of his, a charcoal-burner, and lived beside the charcoal kiln for more than a year, and came down the mountain only after the recovery, and when he got back found the Liquan monastery reduced to half a room, and its people to a few households. The year before last, when the four monasteries kept the Fast together for the first time, it was he who had led those few Liquan households to it. He was sixty-four that year, and told Yisi that when he was young he had studied scripture at the Patriarchate, and when the old elder who taught him lay dying he had not been at his side; he had heard he was buried behind the Patriarchate, and he wanted to go and say scripture once at that grave; and when he had said it, whether he came back or not would be as the Lord intended.

So there was a man, but Marcus could not write Tang characters, and his Syriac letters were crooked too; whatever the Patriarchate asked him, he could say, but he could not write it down. Sabrisho said: let Jingjing go with him. Jingjing could write both scripts, had travelled the road to the Patriarchate once, and the people of the Patriarchate knew him. Yisi did not agree at once; he sat in the side room half a day, and at last he nodded. Jingjing was fifteen that year, and was to be the clerk at Elder Marcus's side, and his interpreter in Tang besides.

Three old believers went with them too: one a felt-maker, one a camel-driver, and one who in his youth had travelled to Fulin dealing in medicines, whose legs were bad now, but who knew every well along the road. All three were *hu*, and had lived half their lives in Chang'an; they said they were going home for one look, and when they had looked, they would come back to Chang'an: they had grown used to living in Chang'an, and even dying, they meant to die in Chang'an. The five of them went with a Sogdian caravan: more than eighty camels, loaded with silk, going west to exchange it for silver. For the caravan's travel pass the Metropolitan Prefecture first had to issue a document, and then it had to be applied for at the Office of Gates of the Department of State Affairs; Official Pei ran there twice for them, and got the five names added.

---

The day they left the city, Yisi saw them off to the Kaiyuan Gate.

Five years ago Jingjing had gone west once before, that time from Lingzhou, two or three months after Fanyang rebelled, and Yisi had not even come out to the mouth of the lane, but stood in the courtyard gateway, raised his hand, and lowered it again. This time he led Jingjing's horse right out of the city gate, and went on two *li* along the highway west.

Xu Jing came too. He was on the army rolls and could not leave the realm, and could only come as far as the city gate; he pushed a water-skin into Jingjing's hands, and recited the rules of the road again from beginning to end: when you're thirsty, hold a mouthful first, then swallow; at night sleep with your boots on; keep with the caravan, don't go looking for wells by yourself. When he had recited them, he said: you know all this; I'm only saying it for my own sake.

Yisi too gave him several charges along the road. He said: keep the letter next to your body; when you reach the Patriarchate, give it first to your uncle; when your uncle has opened it, he'll pass it up. He said: when the Patriarchate asks questions, Elder Marcus answers; what the elder can't answer, then you speak; you say only what you've seen; what you haven't seen, don't say; what you don't know, say you don't know; the people there would rather you said you don't know than that you spoke well of the monastery. He said: when you see your uncle, tell him I'm well.

Jingjing asked him whether Pa really was well, or only wanted him to say so. Yisi turned his face and looked at him, and said he really was well. Jingjing knew the arrow wound in his waist still hurt on overcast days, and at night when he turned over, through a wall, you could hear him draw breath; but he did not say it. Only now did he notice that he was taller than his Pa; standing by his Pa, he could look down and see the top of his head, the hair cropped very short, and a good deal of white in it already.

Yisi said again: whatever you see on the road, write it down. Jingjing said: Master Cen said, don't write only what ought to be; write what you see. Yisi said: then write what you see. When he had said it he put the reins in Jingjing's hand; his hand rested a moment on the back of Jingjing's hand, and then moved away, and he said one last thing: "Don't drink cold water."

Jingjing had heard those words from the time he was small, had heard them till they were worn smooth, and this time, hearing them, his eyes went hot all at once. He held it back with all his strength, nodded, and swung himself up on the horse.

When he had gone a long way, he looked back; Yisi was still standing by the road under a willow. The willow had just put out a little green, and from far off it was only a faint wash of colour. Yisi did not wave, and Jingjing did not wave either, until the caravan rounded a slope of earth and neither could see the other.

That night they stopped at Xianyang, and Jingjing opened his diary under the lamp and thought of Baaluo. Baaluo had gone out of the Chunming Gate in the twelfth month, east and then south; who knew where he had got to by now. He himself was going west. He thought a while, and did not write it on the paper.

---

Along the way, Jingjing wrote a great deal. His diary, as Master Cen had taught him, was in plain simple literary Chinese, and for characters he could not write he drew a circle.

Crossing the Long Mountains, the snow had not yet melted, and the caravan waited three days at the pass for the snow to open a way. Elder Marcus shook with cold, wrapped himself in felt with only his nose showing, and still his mouth was not idle: "O Lord, thirty years I slept in a warm room in the Liquan Ward and never once gave a thought to this mountain of yours, and you must make me think of it at my age." The old camel-driver laughed till he coughed.

At Liangzhou there were far fewer soldiers in the city than five years before; the men on the walls were thin on the ground, and many of them old soldiers gone white-haired. In these years the troops of Hexi had been transferred east batch after batch to fight the rebels, and once transferred, had not come back. The caravan's leader spent two days in the city asking for news, and came back with an ugly look, saying that in the east Grand Commandant Li had lost another battle at Mangshan, Heyang was lost too, and the capital was in a panic; and to the south the Tibetans in the mountains were coming closer and closer—a few years ago they only came down to rustle cattle and sheep, and now they dared burn even the military farms outside the walls, and further west half the beacon towers along the road had no one manning them. The caravan dared not stay long in Liangzhou, and left early the next morning.

Out of the Jade Gate Pass, he saw that earthen platform again. Five years ago they had sheltered behind it a whole night under arrows; Uncle Duan had taken an arrow in the arm, the head stuck in the flesh, and Uncle Xu had dug it out with the point of a knife, and Uncle Duan never made a sound. Jingjing stood a while at the foot of the platform. It looked the same as ever; when the wind blew, a layer of yellow sand lifted from its top. Uncle Duan had died at Xiangzhou the spring before last; before he died he had asked Yisi something, and Pa had not had time to answer. That night Jingjing wrote in his diary: "Outside the Jade Gate the earthen platform still stands. Uncle Duan hath died at Xiangzhou." When he had written it, he added: "Uncle Duan slept ever with one eye open. I made trial of it, and could not sleep."

---

At the end of the fifth month they reached Xizhou.

The monastery north of the city was in better order than five years before; the wooden cross on the east wall of the main hall was wiped spotless. The woman who used to help with the cooking had aged a little; when she saw Jingjing it was a good while before she knew him, and then she took his hand, and the first thing she asked was the price of rice in Chang'an. Her husband had died beyond Tongguan; a soldier who fled back had brought word of it; no body had been seen, and to this day she did not much speak of it.

That evening the monastery's old believers stewed a pot of mutton for them. Jingjing sat in the doorway of the hall with his bowl and looked at the courtyard. Five years ago in this courtyard he had taught the seven elders to speak Tang; Elder Ishaq said *mo pa*, "don't be afraid," as *mo pa*, "grind and crawl," and said *fan*, "rice," as *wan*, and took his bowl to the kitchen to ask the woman whether the *wan* was ready, and they laughed about it for days. It was here too that Elder Yonan, by the light of an oil-dish lamp, learned to write the character for *lotus*, stroke by stroke, twenty-odd times. Jingjing felt the knotted cord inside his coat, and did not take it out.

---

Past the Pamirs it was summer.

The wind on the Pamirs was as strong as five years before; Jingjing rode with a cloth over his mouth and nose, only his two eyes showing. Five years ago he had crossed this mountain following Uncle Duan's back: where Uncle Duan set his foot, he set his, and so inched his way down step by step. This time there was no Uncle Duan's back in front; where he could set his foot, he had to see for himself. Beyond the pass there were still mountains on mountains, and behind the mountains more mountains.

Coming down, he was terribly thirsty, and when he came to a stream of snow-water he lay flat and gulped several great mouthfuls, the water so cold his teeth ached to the roots. Elder Marcus shouted after him, but it was already too late. That night he squatted outside the tent most of the night.

Early the next morning the old medicine-dealer who had been to Fulin brewed him a bowl of ginger broth, brewed it very strong, so hot it made his eyes stream. The old dealer watched him drink it, shaking his head, and said if your Pa knew, who knows what he'd say. Jingjing said: don't tell him. The old dealer said he wouldn't, and asked Jingjing: and in your own diary, will you write it? Jingjing thought and said he would. And he did write it: "Below the Pamirs I drank snow-water, and rose several times in the night. Pa hath warned me of it, and I forgot."

---

In the eighth month they reached Balkh.

The gate of the monastery south of the city stood open, and under the mulberry tree in the courtyard there was now a low couch, and on the couch sat an old man, badly bent, wrapped even in the heat in an old leather robe, a knotted cord gripped in his hand. Jingjing stopped at the monastery gate. The old man heard footsteps, raised his head, and peered toward him; his eyes had gone cloudy, and he could not see people very clearly. Jingjing went over, squatted before him, and said: Elder.

Elder Ahad put out his hand and felt his face, felt his shoulders, felt all down his arm to his hand, felt it all once, and again, and only then said: "So tall now." He said it in Tang, more slowly than before; there were a few words he had to think a long time to remember, but the word *fan*, "rice," he said right.

The old deacon who mended shoes was gone. The elder said he had died the winter before last, very peacefully: the night before he had mended a shoe, and the next morning he did not get up. The monastery now had a young priest sent from Merv, who led worship in the elder's place. The elder's own legs had given out, but on the Lord's Day he still preached, only sitting.

Jingjing went with the elder to the open ground behind the monastery. His grandfather's and grandmother's graves were kept spotless, not a blade of grass on them, and before the stones stood a small clay bowl of clear water. Beside the two graves there was a third, its earth already settled firm, a cross cut on its stone and a name cut beneath the cross. The elder said that was him; when he was dying he had said he wanted to be buried beside the priest Milis; he had mended shoes all his life, and the priest Milis's shoes he had mended, every one.

Jingjing knelt before the three graves and said the evening praises through once. The elder could not stand, and sat on a stone saying them with him, and halfway through his voice stopped. Jingjing looked round: the elder sat there, gripping his knotted cord, his eyes shut, his lips still moving. When he had finished, Jingjing asked the elder whether he had pulled all these weeds. The elder said he had long since been past pulling them; there was a child in the city who came on the Lord's Day to hear the scripture, and when he had heard it, the elder had him pull the weeds, a flatbread each time, and now the child pulled them more diligently than anyone; on Lord's Days he didn't come to hear the scripture, he still came to pull the weeds. When he had said it he laughed first himself, his beard twitching up and down.

That night Jingjing wrote in his diary: "The three graves of my grandfather, my grandmother, and the old deacon that mended shoes, Elder Ahad keepeth for Pa. I am come again to look on them for Pa."

---

They reached the Two Rivers at the end of the tenth month.

Half a day's road short of Ctesiphon there was a ferry. Here the Tigris made a bend, wide and slow, and along the banks boats were moored row on row, some going upstream, some going down. The boats going downstream went all the way to the end, to the city of Basra by the sea, and at Basra you changed to a great sea-going ship, and could sail all the way to India, all the way to Guangzhou.

The caravan had to cross the river here; the ferry-boats were few, and the eighty-odd camels had to be taken over in several batches, which took two or three days. Jingjing got off his horse and took a bucket to the river for water, and as he drew it he was muttering to himself, in Tang, without himself being aware of it. All along the road he had often done this: when he was alone he talked to himself in Tang, as if he were afraid of losing it on the road. In the middle of his muttering someone behind him asked: "You're from Chang'an?"

It was asked in Tang, with a Chang'an accent, and a touch of the country twang of the Duqu area south of the city. Jingjing spun round, and nearly dropped the bucket in the river.

The speaker was about forty, in the long robe of the Tazi, white cloth wound round his head, his face burned dark red by the sun, but his brows and eyes were plainly the brows and eyes of a Tang. He asked again, and Jingjing said yes, his home was in the Yining Ward. The man sank down at once, squatting right there on the muddy bank, and covered his face with both hands, and only after a good while took them away; his eyes were red, but his face was smiling. He said: "My name is Du, Du Huan, of the capital district; my home is south of the city. In the tenth year of Tianbao I went west on campaign with Commissioner Gao, we were beaten at Talas, and the Tazi took me, and carried me west all the way, and brought me here."

He said that in these years he had travelled a good deal in the lands of the Tazi, and stayed longest in a city called Akula. In Akula there were Tang too, all men taken prisoner in that battle: two silk-weavers from Hedong, and two painters, one named Fan and one named Liu, men of the capital district like himself, whom the Tazi engaged to paint things, and paid well. "When I was in Akula," he said, "I'd often get together with Fan and Liu and speak Chang'an talk; we'd talk of which street had the best sesame cakes, which ward's well water was sweet, and when we'd done talking all three of us would be too hungry for home to sleep. Later I left Akula and went north round the land of Sham for some years, and never heard Chang'an talk again; and a Chang'an child talking, I haven't heard in ten years. Now there's a merchant ship willing to take me; I came down this river from the north, and I'll change to a sea-going ship at Basra and go back by sea to Guangzhou. The ship sails the day after tomorrow. Fan and Liu aren't going; they say they've married over there and have children, and can't go back."

---

That afternoon the two of them sat by the river talking until the sun was sinking in the west.

Du Huan asked about Chang'an, and Jingjing told him. Told him An Lushan had rebelled, Chang'an had been lost, and then recovered; told him the Retired Emperor had come back from Shu and now lived in the Western Palace; the Son of Heaven was the Retired Emperor's son, who had taken the throne at Lingwu and had been Son of Heaven five or six years now. Du Huan asked question after question, and toward the end stopped asking and only listened, and when he had listened said: when he left, there was only one Son of Heaven. Jingjing did not know how to answer that, and did not answer.

Du Huan asked him in turn what a Chang'an child was doing all the way out here, and Jingjing told him about the Daqin monastery, told him about his Pa, about his uncle, about the Patriarchate, and that this time he had come to report. When Du Huan heard it, he said he knew the Daqin law; he had seen its people in Akula; the cities over there worshipped every kind of thing; the Tazi law most of all, but there was the Daqin law too, and the Xunxun law. As he spoke he took from his coat a roll of paper, its edges fuzzy with handling, closely covered with characters. He said he had been taking notes the whole way, writing down whatever he saw; there was a young uncle in his clan, senior to him in generation though actually ten-odd years younger, who from childhood had loved poring over institutions and old records, and he meant, when he got back, to give it to this uncle to read.

He leafed through a few pages, found a place, and read it out to Jingjing: "The men of Daqin are skilled in healing the eyes and the flux; some can see a sickness before it comes, some open the skull and take out the worm." When he had read it, he asked Jingjing very earnestly: can you people of Daqin really open the skull and take out the worm?

Jingjing was taken aback, and could not help laughing, and said he couldn't; he couldn't even cure his own flux; all along the road all he could do was drink ginger broth. Du Huan laughed too, laughed till he slapped his thigh.

When the sun was nearly down, Du Huan asked whether he might borrow the diary for a look. Jingjing handed it over. Du Huan unrolled it very slowly, and when he had unrolled it to the part beyond the Jade Gate and saw "Uncle Duan slept ever with one eye open. I made trial of it, and could not sleep," he stopped and asked who Uncle Duan was. Jingjing said he was an old soldier in his Pa's camp, who had escorted him west once, and had died at Xiangzhou the year before last. Du Huan asked no more, and repeated the line silently to himself once.

He rolled up the diary and gave it back to Jingjing, unrolled his own paper, took out a brush, dipped it in ink, and wrote a line at the end of the roll, and when he had written it showed it to Jingjing: "Winter of the twentieth year of Tianbao, at a ferry on a great river in the land of the Tazi, I met a boy of the Daqin monastery of the Yining Ward of Chang'an, aged fifteen, his Tang speech very good, who drinketh ginger broth well."

When Jingjing read it, his face reddened first, and after a while he said softly to him: Master Du, it hasn't been Tianbao for a long time; the Retired Emperor passed on the throne that very year, the era was changed, and now it's Shangyuan. Du Huan said that in Akula he had heard from merchants that the era had changed, but to what, the men who said it couldn't say themselves; all the way he had written Tianbao such-and-such a year, and when he got home he would change them all at once. When he had said it, he did not change that line either.

Jingjing remembered how five years ago, on the road to Ctesiphon, Uncle Duan had squatted beside him and asked whether he could put down a line for him on that paper. He unrolled his own diary and wrote a line at the end of it too: "At a ferry on the great river I met Du Huan of the capital district, ten years fallen among the Tazi, long without hearing the speech of Chang'an, who will take ship back to Guangzhou." When he had written it, he showed it to Du Huan as well. Du Huan read it, rolled up the diary, and handed it back to him with both hands.

---

Early on the third morning, Du Huan's ship sailed.

The ship was going downstream, and with the current it went very fast. Jingjing stood at the ferry watching the ship grow smaller and smaller. Du Huan stood at the stern looking toward the bank the whole time, and as the ship rounded the bend he raised his hand and waved once. Jingjing raised his hand too, and by the time he remembered to put it down the ship had long since rounded the bend; behind him Elder Marcus was calling him, saying the caravan was moving.

---

That evening they entered Ctesiphon.

The great arch in the city was still there. The Patriarchate was still by the river on the east side of the city, its earthen walls still as low and as thick. At the courtyard gate stood two men in grey robes holding short staves; one of them, about thirty, broad-faced and big-eyed, stepped forward and stopped them when they came, with a voice you could hear half the street away, asked where they had come from, and when he heard Chang'an asked whether Chang'an really had over a hundred wards, then turned the wax seal on the letter over and looked at it, and only then stood aside. Jingjing learned afterwards that this man was called Baluma.

His uncle was still in that small room. The room was as small as ever, the walls still white, the wooden cross still nailed on the east wall; the wooden table, the mat, the oil lamp, the lamp lit. His uncle sat behind the wooden table; his hair was all white—five years ago it had been only half white, and now there was not a black hair left. His face was longer and thinner, his cheekbones higher, his eye sockets deeper, and those brown eyes were still exactly like his Pa's. His right sleeve was still empty, tied in a knot at the elbow, tied very tight, very neat.

Jingjing went up to the wooden table, knelt, and made his bow. His uncle looked at him, this time not so long as the last, and spoke, still in the country speech of Balkh:

"Is the road still open?"

Jingjing raised his head and said: "It's open. I've travelled it twice now."
