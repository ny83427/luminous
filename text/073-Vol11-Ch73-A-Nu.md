# Volume Eleven　Huichang

## Chapter 73　A-Nu

> Scriptures were translated, monasteries built, a ferry for the living and the dead.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

In the seventh month of the fifth year of Huichang, the lamps of the city's monasteries were all lit later at night than in other years.

The two decrees of the fourth month—under forty, under fifty—had sent those returned to lay life out of the monastery gates batch after batch, wearing newly bought turban caps, carrying bundles, going back to their native places to be registered. In the fifth month there was another: those over fifty without a certificate from the Bureau of Sacrifices were also to return to lay life; foreign monks without a certificate from the Bureau of Sacrifices were also to return to lay life and be sent back to their own countries.

People in the West Market said that at the Zisheng Monastery there was an old monk from Japan who had lived some years in Chang'an and had been ordered back to lay life in the fifth month. In Tang clothes and a turban cap, with his few chests of scriptures, he had been escorted east by men the government sent; he was to take ship from Dengzhou and go back to his country.

The monastery's people heard it, and each lowered his head and went on with his own work. Joseph and Elijah were men of Merv; the register said they had come from the Patriarchate. Those nights the two of them sat with a lamp lit in the west wing talking in Syriac until midnight.

By the seventh month more talk came out of the city: this was not the end; in the palace a great one was being deliberated. The monasteries of the realm were to be torn down, down to two left on each street of the two capitals, thirty monks left in each, and all the rest torn down. Bronze images melted to cast coin, iron images melted to cast farm tools, the monasteries' lands confiscated, their slaves freed, the monks and nuns returned to lay life, not one left. The Secretariat-Chancellery was deliberating, the chief ministers were deliberating; they had been at it more than a month and had not yet settled it.

That year nineteen stood at the morning.

---

Lü Shao came on the evening of the fourteenth of the seventh month.

He was thirty-five that year and had been a copyist in the Secretariat more than ten years, copying documents, making fair copies of draft decrees, with a fine regular hand. He had never been promoted and never demoted, and in the Secretariat they called him Copyist Lü, and had for ten-odd years. In the year of the Sweet Dew he had hidden seven days at the monastery, and after that, every year at the winter solstice and the New Year, he came once to the monastery with a packet of cakes, stood a while before the stele looking at his great-uncle's name, talked a few words with Jingfu, and left. He didn't believe; he had said so several times. But whenever something touched the monastery and he knew of it, he always came to say a word. When the gruel shed borrowed rice in the third year of Kaicheng, it was he who wrote the petition for Jingfu; in the first year of Huichang, when the Mi aide-de-camp's son would give no more, Jingfu went to wait for him at the Secretariat gate, and he took two strings of cash from his own salary for the gruel shed.

That day when he came it was no festival. The ward gate was not yet shut, it was still light, he carried no cakes, and the sweat on his face was not yet dry.

"Director, there's something I must tell you and the Great Worthy Lingbao," he said. "Inside. With the door shut."

Jingfu led him into the scripture room. Lingbao had not gone home when the morning broke up that day; he was sitting in the sun at the scripture-room door. He Siniang helped him in and sat him down.

Lü Shao stood and would not sit.

"I'll say it and go," he said. "Today in the Secretariat I made a fair copy of the draft of a memorandum, one the Secretariat-Chancellery is presenting to the Son of Heaven. It deliberates the tearing down of the monasteries. The draft's been changed several times. The earlier copies weren't mine; today's was. When I'd copied it I handed it in. Tomorrow it goes up to the throne."

Jingfu shut the door.

"The memorandum is a dozen-odd sheets, all about the Buddhist monasteries. How many to keep in the two capitals, how many in the provinces, how many monks to keep in those kept; the timber of those torn down to repair government offices, bronze images to cast coin, lands confiscated, slaves registered as households—article by article, very detailed," said Lü Shao. "At the very end there's only one sentence that speaks of you."

"What sentence?"

"When I copied that sentence my hand stopped. I read it again, learned it, for fear of getting it wrong, and on the way recited it over in my head a few times."

He stood straight and recited it word by word:

"*As for the shrines of Daqin, the Magi and the like: the Buddhist teaching having been reformed, heterodox doctrines may not alone survive. Their people are all to be ordered back to lay life and sent to their native places as tax-paying households; if they are foreigners, they are to be sent back to their own countries to be taken in charge.*"

No one in the scripture room spoke.

Lü Shao recited it again, more slowly.

"I don't understand your teaching. I only know characters," he said. "Going by the characters, it says the Buddhist monasteries are all to be torn down, and *hu* temples like yours can't be left alone either. The monastery's people—Tang men returned to lay life, sent to their native places, to be tax-paying householders; foreigners sent back to their own countries, handed over there. Whether the Daqin Monastery is to be torn down, this sentence doesn't say. But if the Buddhist monasteries are all torn down and *hu* temples may not alone survive, then it means torn down."

"When?"

"The memorandum goes up tomorrow. If the Son of Heaven approves, it's an edict. Once it's issued—probably next month."

Jingfu looked at him. "Saying this, what crime do you bear?"

"Leaking a palace matter that should be kept secret—by the code, strangling. Judged lightly, a year and a half's penal servitude," said Lü Shao. "I know. I thought it over on the way. I'm a copyist. Ten-odd years I've copied, and of all I've copied not one thing has touched me. This time, copying, I remembered that winter, a sword cut on my arm, coming over the wall, and your monastery's people carrying me into a room."

He wiped the sweat from his face.

"I don't believe in your Lord. But that morning your Director opened the gate and said, he's inside. I remember. I owe that sentence."

He saluted with crossed hands, turned, and went.

Jingfu saw him to the monastery gate. Lü Shao stopped before the lamp by the gate.

"Director, I haven't been here today."

"I know. I won't tell anyone you came," said Jingfu. "But if someone asks me, I won't say you didn't come either."

The corner of Lü Shao's mouth moved.

"You people of this monastery—even at a time like this you're still like this."

He left.

---

That night Lingbao did not go back to the south end of the ward. He had He Siniang take Liu'er home first and stayed the night in the scripture room himself. He lay down until the second watch, couldn't sleep, sat up again, and sent someone to the west wing to fetch Joseph.

Joseph came. "Great Worthy, what do you want me for?"

"Read me the accounts."

"Which book?"

"Elder Samaan's. From the beginning, as far as we get."

"In the dead of night—what do you want the accounts read for?"

"I can't sleep," said Lingbao. "My eyes can't see. I want to hear how this monastery got through, before."

Joseph lit the lamp, carried Samaan's nine ledgers out of the scripture cabinet, and began reading from the oldest.

The second year of Baoying: Elders Azarel, Marya and Samaan arrive in Chang'an; for the welcoming feast, so many *dou* of flour, so many sheep; the Guo household's kitchen sends a basket of steamed cakes. The first year of Guangde: the Tibetans enter the city; a hundred and forty-odd old and weak of the ward taken in; two sacks of rice to ransom the gate. The Dali years: translation of scriptures, the gruel shed, the register of baptisms. The year the stele was set up: forty days' food for the stonemason father and sons; "Master Lü would not accept"; "'what is asked' changed to 'what is thanked.'"

Joseph read slowly. The Syriac letters were small, and in Samaan's later books the hand shook; he leaned to the lamp and made it out letter by letter, and when he had made it out read a line and then said it in Tang. Lingbao sat against the wall listening, and sometimes said, "That entry I remember," and sometimes, "That entry I didn't know."

When he came to the fourth year of Zhenyuan, it was the pages of the gruel shed. That year the Commissioners of Merit made the registers; the monastery gave no consideration; the gruel shed in the West Market was stopped and moved to the ward street, two pots. Samaan had written several pages: the rice, the firewood, the numbers who drew gruel, those who "did not receive." After followed a line, and Joseph read it out:

"*From another ward: A-Nu, grandson of a certain old woman of the Guangde Ward, six; given from the monks' own portion.*"

"Stop there," said Lingbao. "That line I remember."

Joseph raised his head.

"That winter the gruel in the ward street wasn't enough to go round. By the rule, the ward street's gruel was given only to people of this ward; those from other wards got none. An old woman came carrying a child, from another ward, couldn't get any, and stood by the pot. Brother poured his own bowl out for the child. The next day, I poured out mine too," said Lingbao. "I was in my early twenties then, and when I'd poured it out I went running errands in the West Market on an empty stomach. What that child looked like, I don't remember. Elder Samaan asked his name and entered it in the accounts. Only today have I learned he was called A-Nu."

Joseph turned on a few pages and read another line. It was from the twelfth month of that year, the accounts of the dead collected: Elder Narsai pushing the cart, the stakes outside the Kaiyuan Gate, which Samaan had recorded one by one.

"*Twelfth month, nineteenth day: Granny Yang of the Guangde Ward, frozen to death by the gruel shed in the ward street, about sixty; collected by Narsai; stake, second row. Her grandson A-Nu had before this been taken by a palace eunuch as adopted son.*"

Lingbao, against the wall, raised one hand.

"Read it again."

Joseph read it again.

"Taken as adopted son—taken by a palace man," said Lingbao. "In the Zhenyuan years the eunuchs of the palace took adopted sons—some bought from Lingnan and Min, some carried off from poor families in the wards. Once carried off, they were gelded and took their adoptive father's surname. The adoptive father was in the palace, and the adopted son went into the palace too, as a little yellow-gate, and grown up became a palace official. In those days, of the poor families of the wards, some wept and some didn't; some took their children there themselves, saying that following a eunuch there'd be a mouthful of food."

His hand went back to his knee.

"That old woman—A-Nu's granny—came every day that winter carrying the child to draw gruel. Later the child was carried off by a eunuch, and she still came alone, came several times, and in the twelfth month froze to death by the gruel shed."

"Great Worthy, that was more than fifty years ago."

"I know," said Lingbao. "On that page, A-Nu was of the Guangde Ward?"

"The Guangde Ward."

"Granny Yang?"

"Granny Yang."

Lingbao sat there, his hand on his knee, his fingers tapping, tap by tap.

"Last year, when the hermitages were torn down, the old palace official who came to the ward to inspect—Headman Liu said his surname was Yang, that the palace people call him Old Yang, that he's been decades at the Right Street Commissioners of Merit's inspectorate," he said. "Jingfu told me that old official sat on his donkey and asked how many years the gruel shed had been open, asked how many pots a day, and when he'd asked, looked at the pot and left. An official inspecting hermitages—he'd only to count his Buddha niches. What would he ask about the gruel shed for?"

"Great Worthy, Yang is a great surname. In Chang'an there are tens of thousands named Yang."

"I know," said Lingbao. "Tomorrow have Jingfu go and ask Headman Liu where that Old Yang is from."

---

Jingfu went early the next morning.

At first Headman Liu was unwilling to say. "Palace officials' affairs—what would a ward headman know?"

Jingfu asked again. Headman Liu drew him behind the ward gate.

"I only heard it from the old people of the ward too. They say Old Yang was a boy of the Guangde Ward; his family was poor, and in the Zhenyuan years a eunuch surnamed Yang carried him off to be his adopted son. He was only six that year. He grew up in the palace, became a palace official, and the second half of his life has been at the Right Street Commissioners of Merit's inspectorate, risen to administrative assistant. His name is Yang Shousu," said Headman Liu. "The old people of the ward still remember his child-name. What it was, I don't remember."

Jingfu came back and told Lingbao.

"Then it's him," said Lingbao.

That night they deliberated in the scripture room. Joseph was there, Elijah was there, the Shi brothers at the door.

"Uncle Lingbao, you want to go and find him," said Jingfu.

"Yes."

"Find him to do what—beg him?" said Jingfu. "Old Yang is an assistant at the inspectorate. The memorandum is the Secretariat-Chancellery's deliberation; the edict is the Son of Heaven's. What can one palace official of the inspectorate do? And even if he could do a little—if he said a word for a *hu* temple and the palace people heard, if Zhao Guizhen's lot heard, an old official past sixty would lose forty or fifty years' career, and very likely his life too."

Lingbao said nothing.

"Our monastery's rule: we don't beg favours of the government, we don't go by back doors," said Jingfu. "The Master was like that, and Master Yi was like that too. With Lord Guo the Director, Master Yi never once in his life asked a favour."

"I won't beg him," said Lingbao. "I'll only go and open that page of the accounts and let him look at it."

"How is that different from begging?"

"It's different. To beg is to ask him to do something. To open the accounts is to let him know something. Once he knows, whether he does anything is his affair," said Lingbao. "That bowl of gruel—Brother poured it, I poured it. When we poured it we didn't know who the child would be, and the child didn't know who we were. When Elder Samaan kept the accounts, he recorded the truth; when he recorded it, he didn't know that one day someone would come to turn up this page either."

His hand pressed on the ledger on the table.

"This page has been in the scripture cabinet fifty-odd years. I'll take it out—not to collect a debt for that bowl of gruel; the gruel, once poured, is poured. I'll go to tell that man: when he was small he ate a bowl of gruel in the Yining Ward; who poured that gruel; and the monastery that poured it is to be torn down next month."

"Then I'll go," said Jingfu. "I'm the Director. The monastery's business—I go."

"You won't. I'll go," said Lingbao. "That bowl of gruel—Brother poured it first, and the next day I poured mine too. You weren't born yet. You're the Director; if you go, it's the monastery going to beg the inspectorate's assistant. If I go, it's a blind old man going to show another old man a page of accounts."

He drew the ledger toward his breast.

"I'm seventy-eight. If I go, and Old Yang has me beaten out, I'm not wronged. If you go and are beaten out, the monastery will have no Director after."

Joseph, beside them, said, "Great Worthy, what if Old Yang takes it for a trap? He'll think the monastery has dug into his past and is using what happened fifty years ago to threaten him."

"Then let him think so," said Lingbao. "I can't be sure either. I'm only a man who can't see, taking a ledger and going once."

---

The Right Street Commissioners of Merit's inspectorate was in an office building west of the Imperial City, with two clerks guarding the gate.

Lingbao went on the seventeenth of the seventh month, He Siniang holding his arm, the ledger in his breast wrapped in a piece of blue cloth.

"Looking for whom?" asked the clerk at the gate.

"For Assistant Yang."

"Who are you?"

"Of the Daqin Monastery, Yining Ward. Yi Lingbao. Once the monastery's priest; now a registered householder."

"Assistant Yang doesn't see outsiders. Go home."

"I won't go," said Lingbao. "I'll wait at the gate. Whenever Assistant Yang comes out, I'll speak then. If he doesn't come out, I'll wait till the ward gates shut, go home, and come again tomorrow."

He waited at the gate four hours. He Siniang found him a stone to sit on. He sat holding the blue cloth bundle in his arms. The sun moved from the east to overhead, and slanted on toward the west. People went in and out of the gate, in green robes, in blue robes; as they passed him their steps slowed a moment, and went on. No one paid him any heed.

After noon a minor clerk came out from inside. "Assistant Yang says you're to come in."

---

Yang Shousu saw him in a small room.

In the room there was a table with registers piled on it, stack upon stack; against the wall a cabinet, and registers on the cabinet too. The window was small, the room dim, a lamp lit on the table. Yang Shousu sat behind the table, past sixty, thin, a little stooped, in that green robe, his face beardless, white, wrinkled, small eyes under drooping lids.

He Siniang helped Lingbao in.

"Let the lady wait outside the door," said Yang Shousu.

He Siniang did not move, but looked down at Lingbao.

"Go," said Lingbao.

She went out and drew the door to behind her.

Only the two old men were left in the room.

"Please sit, Great Worthy." Yang Shousu's voice was thin and hoarse.

Lingbao sat down on the stool before the table, set the blue cloth bundle on the table, untied it, and took out the ledger. He couldn't see; the night before he had had Joseph put a fine hemp cord at that page. He felt for the cord, turned to the page, turned the ledger round, and pushed it to the other end of the table.

"Assistant Yang, look at this page. It's in Syriac letters; you can't read it. I'll read it to you."

He read it once in Syriac, then said it again in Tang:

"*From another ward: A-Nu, grandson of a certain old woman of the Guangde Ward, six; given from the monks' own portion.*"

Yang Shousu made no sound.

"This was in the winter of the fourth year of Zhenyuan," said Lingbao. "That year the Commissioners of Merit made the registers; the monastery gave no consideration; the gruel shed in the West Market was stopped and moved to the ward street of the Yining Ward. The gruel wasn't enough to go round; the ward street's gruel was given only to people of this ward, and those from other wards got none. An old woman of the Guangde Ward came carrying a child to draw gruel, couldn't get any, and stood by the pot. My brother Jingjing—the one who wrote the stele—poured his own bowl out for that child. The next day I poured mine too. Elder Samaan asked the child's name and entered it in the accounts. It's this line."

He reached for the ledger, turned on a few pages, found the place where another cord lay, and pushed it across again.

"This page is from the twelfth month of that year."

"*Twelfth month, nineteenth day: Granny Yang of the Guangde Ward, frozen to death by the gruel shed in the ward street, about sixty; collected by Narsai; stake, second row. Her grandson A-Nu had before this been taken by a palace eunuch as adopted son.*"

"Elder Narsai was the one who pushed the monastery's cart for the dead. That winter he pushed several dozen out of the Kaiyuan Gate. This old woman—he pushed her out and buried her outside the Kaiyuan Gate, second row of stakes. On the stake was written: Granny Yang of the Guangde Ward," said Lingbao. "That stake was still there a few years ago. Whether it's still there this year, I can't see any more. I don't know."

When he had finished, he put both hands back on his knees.

Yang Shousu kept his head bowed over the two pages. Of the Syriac letters he could read not one—curving strokes, small and dense, line after line. He reached out—his hand white and thin, the nails cut very even—and pressed one finger at the beginning of the line, then moved it to the end, and pressed.

He closed the ledger, closed it very gently, wrapped it in the blue cloth, and pushed it back to Lingbao's hand.

Lingbao found the blue cloth bundle and drew it back into his arms.

"The second row of stakes," said Yang Shousu. "On which side outside the Kaiyuan Gate?"

"Northward, against that earth slope. The second row, counting from the east end, the tenth or so—I don't remember exactly," said Lingbao. "The writing on the stakes is Elder Narsai's. Decades—I'm afraid the wind and rain have worn it smooth."

Yang Shousu asked no more about that.

"Great Worthy, the matter of the edict is the Secretariat-Chancellery's affair, the Son of Heaven's affair. The inspectorate can't touch it," he said. "Fifty-odd years I've been in the palace, the second half of my life at the inspectorate. What the inspectorate can touch and what it can't, I know."

Lingbao sat and listened.

"The Great Worthy has come today, and I have heard. What the Great Worthy has said, I will remember," said Yang Shousu. "Please go back, Great Worthy. The sun's strong outside. Have that lady of yours hold your arm, and walk slowly."

Lingbao stood, saluted with crossed hands, and turned toward the door. He could not see; he walked slowly, reached the door, put out his hand to find the door frame, found it, and stopped. He turned his head back.

"Assistant Yang, that bowl of gruel—once poured, it's poured. I didn't come today to collect on it."

"I know."

---

He Siniang was waiting outside the door and took his arm. They came out of the office and into the street, and the sun was already slanting west.

They had gone a few steps when Lingbao said, "He didn't promise."

"He didn't promise, and he didn't have you beaten out either."

"No," said Lingbao. "When I was small, Pa took me to the Guo mansion. Pa said that if Lord Guo didn't send for him, he never went. Today I went of my own accord; no one sent for me."

---

That evening Yang Shousu sat in that little room until after the ward gates had shut, and the lamp on the table had oil added twice.

A minor clerk of the inspectorate came to tidy up and saw him sitting behind the table with a sheet of paper spread before him and nothing written on it. He had a brush in his hand; the ink on its tip had dried.

"Does the Assistant want anything else?"

"No more. Go home."

The clerk withdrew. Yang Shousu sat on, sat a while longer, bent his head, and blew out the lamp.

Note: In the seventh month of the fifth year of Huichang (845) the Secretariat-Chancellery set out in a memorandum the arrangements for destroying the monasteries: on each of the Left and Right Streets of the two capitals two monasteries were to be kept, with thirty monks each, and the rest destroyed; "as for the shrines of Daqin, the Magi and the like: the Buddhist teaching having been reformed, heterodox doctrines may not alone survive. Their people are all to be ordered back to lay life and sent to their native places as tax-paying households; if they are foreigners, they are to be sent back to their own countries to be taken in charge"—see *Tang huiyao* juan 47 (wording follows the common text; to be checked). Leaking a great matter that should be secret was punished by strangling, and one not great but that should be secret by a year and a half's penal servitude; see the *Tang lü shuyi*, "Zhizhi." Ennin, as a foreign monk without a certificate from the Bureau of Sacrifices, was ordered back to lay life in the fifth month of the fifth year of Huichang and returned east from Chang'an; see his *Record*, juan 4. Tang eunuchs were mostly sent as tribute from Lingnan and Min, and palace eunuchs often took adopted sons; see the "Eunuchs" chapters of the two *Tang Histories*. Yang Shousu and Lü Shao are invented.
