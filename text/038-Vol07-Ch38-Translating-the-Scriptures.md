# Volume Seven　A-Lian

## Chapter 38　Translating the Scriptures

> The Law flowed through the ten circuits, and the state was rich in peace; monasteries filled a hundred cities, and every house abounded in Luminous blessing.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

At the start of spring in the first year of Dali, the elder Azarel raised a matter after supper.

He said the monastery's scriptures must be translated into Tang.

Everyone at the table put down their bowls. It was not a new thing; ever since Alopen came to Chang'an in the Zhenguan years, the monastery had had scriptures in Tang writing. But those scriptures had been translated more than a hundred years ago, and who had translated them no one could now say. The characters were Tang, but the meaning often went round corners: in places it borrowed Buddhist words, in places Daoist words, and in places borrowed no one's words but just sat there stiffly, so that reading it was like chewing sand. The old believers of the monastery had read it all their lives and read it smoothly, and did not notice. Newcomers read it and could not get through it.

"Who will translate?" asked the elder Samaan.

Azarel did not answer, but looked at Babai.

Babai was past sixty that year, a little bent, his fingers still long and thin, his fingernails still black underneath. He thought and said: "I'll see to the Syriac. For the Tang, someone has to write."

When he had said it, everyone turned and looked at Jingjing, sitting at the end of the table.

Jingjing was twenty, in the middle of drinking ginger broth, and with a mouthful not yet swallowed, he choked.

Yisi had happened to come back from Shuofang that day and was sitting beside Azarel. He glanced at his son and said to the elders: "His Tang is better than mine."

He paused, and said again: "He says so himself."

---

The place for translating was the innermost room of the east wing.

The room faced west, and in the afternoon the sun slanted in on a long narrow table. The table had been bought from a silk shop in the ward that had gone under; it was old, the lacquer worn off at one end, showing the bare wood. On the table were an inkstone, brushes, a pile of paper, an earthen jar of water, a dish of ginger slices.

Babai sat at the east end of the table. Before him lay the Syriac scroll, parchment, its edges curled, held down with two paperweights. He read a sentence aloud in Syriac, and when he had read it explained the sense in his half-raw Tang, mixed with Syriac.

Gabriel sat beside him. His left cheek was still wrinkled, his left eye still a slit, but his right eye was very bright. His task was to pick out the hard words. When Babai came to a word he thought would not translate well, he reached out and rapped the table once and said: "This one."

Jingjing sat at the west end of the table, brush raised, listening, thinking, writing.

Narsai was there too.

He had never mastered Tang characters in the end. The characters he wrote still looked like two spears crossed. But he came every day, and when he came did not sit at the table, but on a low stool under the window, a board across his knees, cutting paper and trimming pens for them.

He had a small knife, its blade no longer than a finger, thin, its handle ground from bone and gone yellow. When he cut paper he laid the sheet on the board, set the point of the knife against a bamboo rule and drew it from one end to the other, and with one stroke the edge was straight as the surface of water. When he trimmed a pen he held the reed in his left hand and the knife in his right and pushed outward stroke by stroke, the reed skin falling off sliver by sliver onto his robe.

He took a long time to trim one pen. When he had finished, he flicked the tip with his thumbnail, listened, and only then laid it by Jingjing's hand.

He sat there and said not a word. But every time Jingjing finished writing a sentence and read it aloud, he raised his head and listened. When he had heard it, sometimes he nodded, and sometimes he did not.

When he did not nod, he would say: "Something's missing."

"Missing what?"

Then Narsai would recite the original in Syriac, word by word, like knocking in nails. When he had recited it he said: "There's a *because* here. You haven't written it."

Jingjing looked at the sentence he had written, and indeed there was no *because*. He felt that in Tang there was no need for *because*; the sense was clear. But he did not argue with Narsai. He added the *because*. With it added, the sentence took a bend and did not read smoothly.

When Narsai heard it, he nodded.

Jingjing waited until he had bent his head to his pen-trimming again, and quietly straightened out the bend. Sometimes he could straighten it, and sometimes he could not.

---

The first thing they translated was a hymn.

It was the one the monastery sang at every service, sung in Syriac since Alopen's time. Its opening line said: glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace to men. Jingjing had heard it since he was small, heard it twenty years, could sing it, but had never thought what it should be in Tang.

Babai read a line and explained a line. Gabriel rapped the table. Jingjing wrote. Narsai cut paper.

God was called Alaha. This name was in the old scriptures already, from the sound of the Syriac. Gabriel said: this word is good, it's our own sound, not borrowed from anyone. Babai said: good. Jingjing wrote it down.

The Messiah was called Mishihe. That too was in the old scriptures. Jingjing wrote it down.

When they came to the Holy Spirit, they stuck.

The old scriptures had several names for it. Some transliterated the sound, four characters, knobbly to say, so that a Tang listener could not remember it after one hearing. Some borrowed a Buddhist term, and Gabriel shook his head as soon as he saw it.

"No," he said. "That's someone else's house."

Jingjing knew what he was thinking of. Ten years before, in Xizhou, Jingjing had said those words himself, and Gabriel had remembered them to this day.

The three of them sat facing that blank line all afternoon.

Babai explained the passage three times. The Holy Spirit—that word in Syriac was at root breath, wind, the breath that comes out of a man's mouth. Gabriel recited a verse from the Gospel of John: The wind bloweth where it listeth, and thou hearest the sound thereof, but canst not tell whence it cometh, and whither it goeth.

Jingjing listened, and said nothing.

He thought of years ago, walking with the caravan across the gravel desert, the wind blowing from the north at night, setting the tent cloth going *puh*, *puh*. He thought of the white poplars in the city of Xizhou, their leaves rushing in the wind, their shadows swaying back and forth on the walls. He thought of earlier still, in Lingzhou, the wind blowing off the Yellow River, setting the window paper going *puh*, *puh*, and Pa tying the strings of his sheepskin coat and pushing the door open to go out, and the cold wind rushing into the room all at once, and the lamp flame bending, and not going out.

The wind cannot be seen. But when the grass bends one way, a man knows which way the wind is going.

He thought of what Rectifier Cen had told him that year in the felt tent: don't just write *ought*; write what you see.

He took up the brush and wrote two characters.

淨風. *Jingfeng*. The Pure Wind.

Gabriel leaned over to look, looked a long time. "Wind?" he said.

"Wind," said Jingjing. "A clean wind."

Babai read it once in his lopsided Tang: "*Jing—feng*." When he had read it he smiled and said: "Easy to say."

Under the window, Narsai stopped the knife in his hand. He raised his head and looked at Jingjing, looked a while.

He nodded once.

That line was afterwards settled as *the merciful Father, the Bright Son, the Pure Wind King*. Tang mouths said it easily, and remembered it at one hearing.

---

Translating scripture was very slow work.

One hymn, no more than a few dozen lines, took them more than a month. When it was done Jingjing made a fair copy, took it to the front hall, and read it to everyone at the service. Some of the old believers nodded when they heard it, some frowned, saying it was not as good as the old one; they were used to the old one. The young ones heard it, said it over after him once, and when they had said it, said: this time we understand.

Jingjing went back to the east wing, noted down the lines the old believers had frowned at, and revised them again.

When they were revised, he read them again. And revised again.

Later, as they translated more, they went a little faster, though not much faster. One scripture took a month or two if it was short, more than a year if it was long. Babai's eyes grew worse year by year, until in the end he had to bring the parchment so close to read it that his nose nearly touched it. The hard words Gabriel picked out grew more every year; sometimes he picked out one word and the three of them argued three days. The paper Narsai cut grew straighter every year.

Winter was the hardest.

There was no brazier in the east wing, for fear of sparks landing on the scrolls. The ink in the inkstone grew a skin of ice after a little writing. Jingjing wrote a few characters and then had to bring the brush tip to his mouth and breathe on it to soften it before he wrote again. Babai stamped his feet with cold, read a line and stamped, and at the important places stamped louder.

The water in the earthen jar froze. Jingjing was thirsty, looked at the jar of ice, and did not touch it.

He got up and went to the kitchen, poured a bowl of hot water, cut a few slices of ginger and dropped them in, brought it back, set it by his hand, and wrote a few characters and drank a mouthful.

Gabriel watched him drink and asked him: "Your pa isn't here—and still you won't drink cold water?"

Jingjing said: "My stomach is here."

---

In those years a young man often came to the monastery.

He was a Persian, two or three years older than Jingjing, surnamed Li, named Su. When his forebears had come to Chang'an and what they had done, some said they were Persian nobles and some said not; he himself never spoke of it. He spoke little; at the service he stood at the very back, and when it was over he left and did not talk much with anyone.

He understood the stars.

He could look at the sky and tell you when the sun would rise tomorrow, whether the moon would be full or waning the day after, how many days more before some star would rise in the east. At first the people of the monastery did not believe him; later they checked him time after time, and every time he was right, and they believed.

That year the monastery was troubled over something. Which day was Easter had to be reckoned by the moon: the first full moon after the spring equinox, and the first Lord's Day after the full moon. The Patriarchate had a method of reckoning written in a Syriac calendar, but that calendar was reckoned by the sky over the Two Rivers, and used in Chang'an it was a little off every year, off enough that no one felt easy.

The elder Azarel asked Li Su to look at it.

Li Su came, spread the Syriac calendar and the Tang calendar side by side on a table, and compared them, reckoning on his fingers. He reckoned three days, and on the fourth came with a sheet of paper on which were written the dates of Easter for the next ten years, line by line, the Tang date on the left and the Syriac date on the right.

Jingjing held the paper and looked at it a long time. He asked Li Su what would be a good name in Tang for the Lord's Day that came round every seven days.

Li Su thought and said: "In the Tang calendar there are the Seven Luminaries. Sun, Moon, Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus, Saturn—seven days, round and round. The Lord's Day falls just on the Sun's day."

Jingjing said: "The Sun's day won't do. Tang people will hear it and think we worship the sun."

Li Su laughed too. He said: "Then call it *once in seven days the offering*. Seven days, one offering."

Jingjing remembered those words. Later he wrote them into the stele.

Before long, Li Su stopped coming often. People said he had gone into the Astronomical Bureau, watching the stars in the palace and reckoning days for the Son of Heaven. Some years after that, it was said he had become an official, and his office grew greater and greater. He was at the Astronomical Bureau many years, and lived longer than most of the people at this monastery. But that is for later.

He still came now and then, still stood at the very back, and left when the service was over. Once Jingjing ran after him to the gate and asked him what it was like watching the stars at the Astronomical Bureau.

Li Su stood under the lamp at the gate, thought, and said: "They're the same stars as here."

---

Every year the elder Azarel had to write a letter to the Patriarchate telling how the church in the east was faring. Later it was always Jingjing who drafted this letter for him.

One year Azarel had him list every monastery in the east, writing each one clearly—how many people, how many elders.

Jingjing sat in the east wing, spread out the paper, and began with Chang'an.

Chang'an, the Yining Ward, one. Luoyang, one, said to be still there, few people now. Lingzhou, one; Pa had repaired it. He remembered the edict Emperor Suzong had issued that year to re-establish the Luminous monasteries in Lingwu and the four other commanderies, and wrote those five commanderies down too. Xizhou, the one north of the city; Xu Jing still kept it, and the year before last had written that a dozen more people had joined. Shazhou, the caravans said there was one; he had never been, and did not know.

When he had written this far he stopped his brush, thought, and counted them over again.

At the eleventh, he could count no further.

He folded the paper and gave it to Azarel. Azarel read it, said nothing, and put the paper in the letter.

Many years later Jingjing wrote on the stele the words *the Law flowed through the ten circuits; monasteries filled a hundred cities*. What he wrote of was the time of Emperor Gaozong, before he was born. Whether there had truly been a hundred cities then, with a monastery in every one, he did not know. He searched all the monastery's old records, and could not find an exact number.

What he had seen with his own eyes in his life was a dozen or so lamps.

---

Sometimes Yonan came to the east wing too.

He did not sit at the table, nor under the window, but on a rush cushion by the door, with a sand tray on his knees, writing characters by himself. What he wrote was still that one: *heart*. He wrote it and smoothed it out, smoothed it out and wrote it.

When he came, no one in the room sent him away, nor greeted him. When Jingjing tired of writing and raised his head, he would sometimes see him by the door with his head bent over his writing, the sun on that smooth face of his, so that he looked like a young man sitting there. Only the hand that held the bamboo stylus was old.

One day, when Yonan had finished writing a *heart*, he raised his head and asked Jingjing: "*Weakness*—how do you write it in Tang?"

Jingjing was taken aback. He wanted to ask Yonan what he wanted those two characters for, and did not.

On a scrap of leftover paper he wrote the two characters 軟弱, *ruanruo*, *weakness*, and handed it over.

Yonan took it and looked at it a long time. He put the scrap by the edge of the sand tray and copied it. 軟 had many strokes; he wrote it five or six times, and it came out all in pieces, the cart radical and the yawning radical parted company. 弱 he wrote better.

"弱," he read, "is like two men."

Jingjing looked. The character 弱 had two halves, left and right, exactly alike, side by side, both bent over.

"Like two men kneeling," said Yonan.

He folded the scrap and put it inside his robe. The next day, when Jingjing looked at his sand tray, what was written there was *heart* again.

---

Year by year the scrolls piled up on that long table in the east wing.

After the hymn came a scripture of the creation, after that one of the life of the Messiah, after that one on how to pray. When one was finished Jingjing made three copies, one kept at the monastery, one sent to Luoyang, one to be taken to the Patriarchate when a caravan went west. Later when people asked Jingjing how many scriptures he had translated in all, he reckoned and said: thirty or so. That was many years later.

In the spring of the tenth year of Dali, Babai's eyes could no longer make out the parchment. When he read he had Jingjing hold the scroll for him and point to the line, and he followed that finger line by line. He read more slowly still, and sometimes stopped halfway and thought a long time before going on.

Gabriel's hair had gone half white.

Jingjing was twenty-nine. His writing hand was steadier than in those years; the brush came down without trembling. But at the important places he still had to stop, breathe on it, drink a mouthful of ginger broth, and then write.

That afternoon they came to a hard place and stuck again. Babai read it three times, Gabriel rapped the table three times, Jingjing's brush went up and down, down and up.

The sun slanted in, slanted to the west end of the table, and shone on the back of Jingjing's hand.

Under the window, Narsai finished trimming a new pen. He wiped the knife on his robe, wiped it clean, and put it away in his sleeve. He flicked the pen tip with his thumbnail, listened, stood up, went to the table, and laid the pen by Jingjing's hand.

Then he went back under the window, sat down, and took up another sheet of paper.
