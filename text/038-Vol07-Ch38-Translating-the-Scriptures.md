# Volume Seven　A-Lian

## Chapter 38　Translating the Scriptures

> He hung up the luminous sun to break open the dark realm, and the devil's lies were all destroyed.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

When spring opened in the second year of Yongtai, Elder Azarel raised a matter after a meal. He said the monastery's scriptures must be translated into Tang afresh.

Everyone at the table put down their bowls. The matter was nothing new; since Alopen came to Chang'an in the Zhenguan years the monastery had had scriptures in Tang, but those scriptures had been translated more than a hundred years ago, and who had translated them no one could now say. The characters were Tang, but the meaning often went round corners; in some places it borrowed Buddhist words, in some Daoist words, and in some places it borrowed nobody's words and just sat there stiff and hard, so that reading it was like chewing sand. The monastery's old believers had recited them all their lives and knew them by heart and did not notice; newcomers could not get through them. Azarel said the order required that three men be trained every year who could preach in Tang; in these years not one had been trained, and it was because there were no proper Tang scriptures; Tang who wanted to learn picked them up and could not understand them.

Elder Samaan asked who would translate. Azarel did not answer, but looked at Babai. Babai was a little over sixty that year, a little stooped, his fingers still long and slender, his nails still black underneath; he thought, and said he would read the Syriac, but someone had to write the Tang. When he had said it, everyone turned their heads and looked at Jingjing, sitting at the end of the table. Jingjing was twenty that year, and in the middle of drinking ginger broth; a mouthful went down the wrong way, and he choked.

Yisi sat beside Azarel. He was newly married, and in these days the monastery's people, looking at him, all thought there was a better colour in his face, and that he said a few more words than before. He glanced at his son and said to the elders: "His Tang is better than mine." He paused, and said: "He says so himself." Everyone at the table laughed, Elder Ishaq loudest, and when he had laughed he slapped Jingjing on the back and said: your Pa's made a joke for the first time in his life.

---

Before the translating began, the elders debated three evenings over one thing: whose words to translate with.

A good many words in the old scriptures were borrowed. God was called Alaha in one place in the old scriptures, but in several places Tianzun, the Heavenly Worthy, which was the Daoist name, and the Tang understood it at once; and in one place it was written Buddha, and when Gabriel turned to that place his face went dark. Narsai said: to borrow another's words is to live in another's house; live there long enough, and the house is theirs, and you become theirs too; Alaha is Alaha, Meshiha is Meshiha; if the Tang don't understand, teach them to understand; you can't change the Lord's name to someone else's for the sake of making them understand. Sabrisho said the rule did not say borrowing was forbidden, nor that it was allowed. Elder Ishaq said that when he treated people in the ward, the old women asked him: this Alaha you people worship, is that the Lord of Heaven? He said yes, and the old women were reassured; had he said no, they wouldn't even have taken their medicine. At this, they all looked at Jingjing.

Jingjing did not say much that day. He said Granny He had recited Alaha forty years and in her heart still thought of the Lord of Heaven—Elder Narsai had heard that too, by the well; but Granny He had also said that in forty years of reciting she didn't know what she was reciting. He said his thought was: the Lord's names in our own sounds, Alaha, Meshiha, not one character changed; the rest, whatever could be said clearly in the Tang's own words, say in the Tang's words, and where Buddhist or Daoist characters were used, make them say our meaning, and where they couldn't be made to say our meaning, better not use them. When Narsai had heard it, he said: so, half living in our own house and half in another's. Jingjing said yes, Elder; he had no other way; the Tang lived in Tang houses; he had to go to their doors to speak. Narsai said no more, and Elder Azarel said: so be it; translate first, and when it's translated we'll see.

---

The place for the translating was fixed in an empty room of the west wing of the back courtyard. The room faced west; in the afternoon the sun slanted in and fell across a long table. The table had been bought from a silk shop in the ward that had gone under; it was old, the lacquer worn off at one end showing the bare wood; on it were an inkstone, brushes, a stack of paper, a clay jar of water, and a dish of ginger slices.

Babai sat at the east end of the table, the Syriac scripture spread before him, of parchment, its edges curled, held down by two paperweights. He read a sentence, read it in Syriac, and when he had read it explained the meaning once in his half-baked Tang mixed with Syriac. Gabriel sat beside him; his left cheek was still puckered, his left eye still a slit, but his right eye was very bright; his charge was to pick out the hard words; when Babai came to a word Gabriel thought would not translate well, he reached out and rapped the table and said: this one. Jingjing sat at the west end of the table, brush in hand, listening, thinking, writing. Every time a passage was done, Jingjing had to take it to Elder Marya to be examined; Marya was Elder of Preaching, and the order said Tang scripture must pass his examination before it could be read out; but Marya could not read Tang characters, so Jingjing put the translated Tang back into Syriac sentence by sentence and read it to him, and Marya listened hoarsely, and wherever the meaning had gone out of shape, he raised his hand. When that hand went up, Jingjing had to go back and rewrite.

Narsai was there too. His Tang characters had never come right after all; what he wrote still looked like two spears crossed; but he came every day, and when he came he did not sit at the table but on a low stool under the window, a board across his knees, cutting paper and trimming pens for them. He had a small knife, its blade no longer than a finger, thin, its handle of bone ground yellow. When he cut paper, he laid the paper on the board, set the knife-point against a bamboo rule, and drew it from one end to the other; one stroke, and the edge was level as water. When he trimmed pens, they were the reed pens Babai wrote Syriac with; he held the pen in his left hand and the knife in his right and pushed outward stroke by stroke, and the reed skin fell flake by flake onto his robe. He took a long time over each pen, and when it was done flicked the nib with his thumbnail, listened, and only then laid it by Babai's hand. The brushes Jingjing wrote Tang with he did not know how to dress, so he washed Jingjing's inkstone clean for him and ground the ink.

He sat there and said not a word, but each time Jingjing finished a sentence and read it out, he raised his head and listened, and when he had listened, sometimes nodded and sometimes did not. When he did not nod, he said: "Missing." Jingjing asked him what was missing, and Narsai recited the original in Syriac, word by word, like driving nails, and when he had recited it said: there's a "because" here; you haven't written it. Jingjing looked at the sentence he had written, and indeed there was no "because"; he thought Tang did not need "because," the meaning was clear without it; but he did not argue with Narsai, and put in the "because." With it in, the sentence went round a bend and did not read smoothly. Narsai heard it and nodded. Jingjing waited until he bent his head to his pen-trimming again and quietly straightened out the bend; sometimes he could straighten it, and sometimes not.

---

The first thing they translated was in fact not scripture but the names of medicines.

That was before the translating began. Elder Ishaq had charge of the monastery's medicines; the medicine chest had been put together by Yisi in his twenties, drawer after little drawer, each with a label pasted on it, half in Syriac and half in Tang; the Tang Yisi had written, crookedly, and a good many could no longer be read. These two years the giving of medicine had passed to Jingjing, and every time Jingjing went to make up a prescription he had to call Elder Ishaq to read the labels. Last winter Elder Ishaq had gone out of the city to see a patient, and Jingjing was alone; a child in the ward was coughing badly; he went by the labels to get coltsfoot flower; the few Tang characters on the label had been written by Yisi twenty years ago, the ink had faded, the character *kuan* was only half there; he took from the wrong drawer, the one beside it, false hellebore. Luckily the child's mother, before boiling it, brought it to show Pei Shiyiniang; Shiyiniang had seen coltsfoot in the countryside and said this wasn't it, and so that packet never got into the child's mouth. When Elder Ishaq came back and heard of it, his face was white half the day, and he said: you get the writing on this chest translated clear first, then go and translate scripture; a word of scripture translated wrong can still be corrected; a medicine taken wrong, and the person's gone.

So Jingjing translated the medicine chest first. Drawer by drawer, Elder Ishaq said in Syriac what medicine this was, where it came from, what it treated, and Jingjing went to Shopkeeper Wang in the West Market to ask what the Tang called it. Some were easy to find: apricot kernel was apricot kernel, liquorice was liquorice. Some were hard: there was a tree resin from the Two Rivers that the *hu* merchants of the West Market called *moyao*, myrrh; Shopkeeper Wang went through the whole *Xinxiu bencao* and it wasn't there, and so they wrote it as the *hu* merchants said it. And there were several that even the *hu* merchants had no Tang name for, and Jingjing could only write them by their sound, drawing a small circle beside them to show this was a *hu* sound. Those labels took him three months to replace, and when they were replaced, he copied out a medicine register from them, one medicine to a line, the Syriac name on the right, the Tang name on the left, what it treated written beneath. The one who copied the medicine register was Pei Shiyiniang; her characters were more upright than Jingjing's. Elder Ishaq leafed back and forth through that register, and in the end said this thing mattered more than his own book of prescriptions.

---

The translating of scripture began with a hymn. It was sung at every service at the monastery, had been sung since Alopen's time; its first line said glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace to men. Jingjing had heard it since he was small, heard it twenty years, could sing it, but had never thought what it should be in Tang. What they first pieced together were two lines: "In the highest of the heavens, deep reverence and praise; on the great earth, all again think on peace and concord." Seven characters to a line, set out the way Tang poems are; Babai read it twice and said it was long; Jingjing said when the Tang sing songs, seven characters is just right for a breath.

God was Alaha, the name already in the old scriptures, from the sound of the Syriac. Gabriel said this word was good: our own sound, borrowed from no one; Babai said good; Marya heard it and did not raise his hand. Messiah was Meshiha, also in the old scriptures. Before the order came down in the second year of Baoying, Babai had pieced together a few Tang hymn lines with some Tang believers—"Alaha made heaven and earth; sun and moon and stars are all lamps he lit"—and this time they brought it out too; Marya had Jingjing translate it for him line by line, and when he had heard it said the meaning was not wrong, only the characters bent round when sung, and told Jingjing to rearrange the characters to fit the original tune. Jingjing rearranged it for half a month, until those bending characters ran smooth; Babai sang it once, Elder Ishaq heard it at the door, and came in and said it was better than before.

When they came to write the Holy Spirit, they stuck.

The old scriptures had a name for it, *liangfeng*, cool wind, fixed by those who translated more than a hundred years ago, taking the root sense of the Syriac word, which was breath, wind, the breath a man breathes out of his mouth. Gabriel read a line from the Gospel of John, and Jingjing put it into Tang on the spot: the wind bloweth where it listeth, and thou hearest the sound thereof, but canst not tell whence it cometh, and whither it goeth. Babai said since the old scriptures called it cool wind, keep it as it was. Jingjing took up the brush and wrote it, and when he had written it took it home, and at night under the lamp revised another passage. Pei Shiyiniang, beside him with nothing to do, drew out an old scripture from under his stack of drafts to look at: the *Xutingmishisuo Sutra* that had come down in the monastery over a hundred years. She could read; she read a few lines, and when she came to the Lord's name—the old scripture wrote it *Yishu*, "shifting rat"—she read it twice and burst out laughing, and said: whose rat is this, moving house? Jingjing laughed too, and said that was written by sound by people a hundred-odd years ago, who didn't know what *shu* meant in Tang. Shiyiniang read on, and came to a line, "The cool wind came upon a virgin, named Moyan," and stopped laughing; she stopped there, and asked what this cool wind was. Jingjing said it was the Holy Spirit, that breath that comes from the Lord. Shiyiniang thought a while and said she didn't understand your doctrines, but when a Tang hears *cool wind*, what he thinks of is autumn, is the graveyard, is the draught that creeps in at the door-crack at night; it makes you feel cold to hear it. When she had said it, she drew up her own shoulders first.

Jingjing looked at those two characters a good while. All his life what he feared most was cold: cold water, cold wind, cold food; from the time he was small his Pa would not let him drink cold water, and at twenty he still dared not. He remembered following the camel train over the Gobi many years ago, the wind blowing from the north at night till the tent cloth flapped; remembered the white poplars in the city of Xizhou, their leaves rustling in the wind; remembered Lingzhou, the wind coming off the Yellow River, his Pa tying the strings of his sheepskin coat and pushing open the door, the cold wind rushing into the room, the lamp flame leaning once and not going out. He remembered too what Master Cen had said to him that year in the felt tent in the back courtyard: don't write only what ought to be; write what you see. He took up the brush and meant to write the character *jing*, pure; the tip touched the paper and stopped again. He thought of those eleven young men in white in the Liquan Ward who called themselves the Pure Ones, *jingzhe*; thought of the character *keep* the butcher's son had written square and true on the wooden board. This character *jing*—someone had taken it and gone and poured water on another household's fire. He held the brush suspended, held it a while, and then after all struck out the character *liang*, cool, and beside it wrote *jing*, pure, and when he had written it, added a small character beneath: *feng*, wind—so that it went joined, and not alone.

The next day he took it to the west wing; Gabriel leaned over to look and asked: wind? Jingjing said: wind; clean wind. Babai read it once in his crooked Tang: "*Jing—feng*." When he had read it he laughed and said it was easy to say. Jingjing translated it for Marya as clean wind; Marya thought a moment and did not raise his hand. Under the window, Narsai stopped the knife in his hand, raised his head and looked at Jingjing, looked a while, and nodded once. Later this line was settled as "Compassionate Father, Luminous Son, Pure Wind King," and the Tang found it smooth to say and remembered it on first hearing.

---

Translating scripture was slow work. A hymn of only a few dozen lines took them more than a month, and when it was done Jingjing made a fair copy and took it to the front hall and read it out to everyone at a service. Of the old believers who heard it, some nodded and some frowned and said it wasn't as good as the old; they were used to the old; the young ones heard it, read it through with him once, and when they had read it said: this time we understand. Granny He was past seventy that year and hard of hearing; when Jingjing had finished, she took hold of his sleeve and had him lean down to her ear and read it again, and when he had read it she said: forty years I've recited it, and today for the first time I know what these lines say. Jingjing went back to the west wing and noted down the lines the old believers had frowned at, and revised them again, and when they were revised read them again, and revised again. The settled scriptures he copied in three: one to stay at the monastery, one to wait for someone from Luoyang to take back to the Xiushan Ward, and one meant to wait for a caravan going west to carry to the Patriarchate. But that year the road through Hexi was cut, and the caravans going west either went round through Uighur country or did not go, and that copy, wrapped in oilcloth, lay a year on top of the cupboard with no one to take it.

That year when Jingjing went back to the side courtyard at night he often took the day's drafts with him and revised them under the lamp in the outer room. When Pei Shiyiniang had done her sewing, she sat across from him and made fair copies for him. She copied slowly, reading each sentence aloud as she copied it, and wherever it did not run smoothly she stopped her brush and asked him: would a Tang say this? Once she was copying "all men are *zui'e*, sinful and wicked," and she said when the Tang say a man has *zui*, they mean he's broken the king's law and been taken off by the yamen; tell an old woman in the ward she has *zui*, and she'll fly at you. Jingjing thought all night and changed it to "all men have faults"; when he had changed it, he felt it was too light, and changed it back, and added a sentence beneath explaining that this *zui* meant a man being cut off from Alaha, which was different from the *zui* of the king's law. When Shiyiniang copied this line, she nodded and said put that way, she understood. After that, every time Jingjing finished a passage he first took it home and read it to her, and only what she understood did he take to Elder Marya to be examined.

Yisi by then was mostly asleep. This year he had charge of less in the monastery; the gruel kitchen he had handed to Jingjing, the outside business too he had Jingjing go along on with Official Pei, and himself kept only the general accounts and the letters to the Patriarchate. He slept early and rose early, went to the hall before light, and when worship was done came back and split firewood in the courtyard, and when the wood was split went to the West Market to look in on the gruel kitchen, and came back to eat at midday. The midday meal Shiyiniang cooked, and there were Tang dishes; once she made a bowl of vinegared celery, and Yisi took a mouthful and it was so sour his eyes shut; when he had finished it he said: make it again. Jingjing, translating in the west wing, sometimes heard Shiyiniang over in the side courtyard scolding Yisi, scolding him for not knowing to stand aside when he split wood, so the chips flew all over her; Yisi did not answer back, and when she had done scolding said: next time I'll stand aside. Jingjing, listening, wrote a character wrong on the paper, struck it out and wrote it again.

Winter was hardest to bear. No brazier was lit in the west wing for fear sparks would fly onto the scrolls, and the ink in the inkstone grew a skin of ice after a little writing; every few characters Jingjing had to bring the brush-tip to his mouth and breathe on it, and write again when it had softened. Babai stamped his feet with cold, read a sentence, stamped once, and at the important places stamped louder. The water in the clay jar froze; when Jingjing was thirsty he looked at the jar of ice, got up and went to the kitchen to pour a bowl of hot water, cut in a few slices of ginger, brought it back and set it at his hand, and wrote a few characters and took a sip. Gabriel watched him drink, and asked: your Pa isn't even in this room, and still you won't drink cold water? Jingjing said: my stomach is.

That winter they came to the passage where the Lord, after his death, went down into the place of the dead. The Syriac word meant the place where the dead stay: no light; once a man goes there he does not come out. The old scriptures did not have this passage; when Babai had explained it, the few men in the room all looked at Jingjing. Jingjing first wrote *diyu*, hell, and having written it shook his own head: *diyu* was a Buddhist word, and a Tang, hearing it, thought of cauldrons of oil, mountains of knives, King Yama sitting in his hall judging people; that was a place of punishment, and the place the scripture spoke of was not for punishing, it was darkness, the place everyone must go, and once there, there was no more sound. Gabriel said then write it by sound; Jingjing said write it by sound, and the Tang would read it like a spell and not know what it was when they'd read it. Babai said what do the Tang call the place the dead go? Jingjing said they called it the Yellow Springs, the Nine Springs, the Dark Netherworld, beneath the springs; at each one he named, Babai shook his head, saying the Yellow Springs had water, and the place in the scripture had no water. Narsai, listening under the window, trimmed his pen a long while, and suddenly said something in Syriac: that place was a mansion, *fu*, a seat of government, with gates and a bar on the gates, and the Lord went there and smashed the gates open. Jingjing heard it and wrote the character *fu* on the paper, then added *an*, dark, in front of it, and read it once: *anfu*, the dark realm. Babai read it once and said good: it has gates. Marya that day, hearing Jingjing put it back into Syriac, when he came to this, raised his hand; Jingjing thought he would have it rewritten again, but Marya only pressed his hand once in the air, as if pressing shut a door, and said: that's it. Jingjing went back and circled the two characters *anfu* in the draft, and when he had circled them wrote a line beside them, saying the Lord went there like the sun shining in: "He hung up the luminous sun to break open the dark realm." When he had written it he read it to Shiyiniang, and Shiyiniang said this line made her heart feel lit when she read it; and she said that the winter her mother-in-law died at the monastery, when they buried her, she had thought: a person is buried, and underneath it's dark; she'd been afraid her mother-in-law would be afraid of the dark. That night Jingjing revised no other line.

---

One evening, when the others in the room had all gone, Narsai still sat under the window trimming pens. Jingjing put away the drafts on the table, and when he had put them away asked him: that day the Elder said to Pa, you never vowed—what did it mean?

Narsai stopped the knife and looked at him a while before he said: "Your Pa is a priest; a priest has taken no vow not to marry; his former wife died, he takes another; the rule allows it, and the Lord allows it. Those who have vowed are different. A monk, the day he enters the monastery, kneels before the Lord and vows; what's vowed is vowed: not to marry, not to have a family, not to have things of his own, all his life. To vow and not keep it is to lie before the Lord." When he had said it he bent his head and went on trimming his pen, trimmed twice more, and said: "I have vowed."

Jingjing stood there holding that stack of drafts. He remembered under the lamp in Xizhou Narsai writing the character for *vow*, writing it like two crossed spears, and when it was written saying: once you vow, you must keep it; one who can't keep it ought not to vow. He had been eleven then and had not understood. He thought too of Elder Yonan; Elder Yonan had come out of a monastery; the Patriarchate's bishops all came out of monasteries. He said nothing at all, locked the drafts in the cupboard, and left.

---

That year a young man came to the monastery, a Persian, two or three years older than Jingjing, surnamed Li, named Su. His family had lived in Guangzhou in early years, and his forebears had held office there; he knew the stars and the calendar, his name had reached the capital, and early in the Dali years he was summoned to the capital by edict. When he entered Chang'an, the first thing he did was come to the Yining Ward, and he lived at the monastery some days waiting for the papers from the Directorate of Astronomy. He lodged in the back courtyard, and did not sleep at night, but often climbed onto the roof of the scripture room, wrapped in a felt, and lay with his face to the sky, looking, and only came down at midnight. One night Yonan went up too, and the two of them sat on the roof ridge; Yonan pointed to the western sky and told him that when he was young, in the monastery at Ctesiphon, he had looked like this at night too; the stars seen there were the same as here, only a little lower. Li Su asked him how much lower; Yonan held out a hand, four fingers together, and measured, and said: this much. The next day Li Su took a bamboo pole and spent half a day measuring shadows in the courtyard, and when he had done told Yonan the elder's measure was right; it was about that much. He spoke little, stood at the very back during worship and left when it was over, and did not talk much with anyone; but he understood the heavens, and could look at the sky and say when the sun would rise tomorrow, whether the moon would be full or waning the day after, how many days more before such-and-such a star would rise in the east; the monastery's people did not believe it at first, and then it checked out time after time, right every time, and they believed.

In those years the monastery was in difficulty over one thing. Which day Easter fell on the Patriarchate had a way of reckoning, written in the Syriac calendar, using the years and months of the Two Rivers; brought to Chang'an, it had to be changed into the months and days of the Tang calendar. When Yonan was bishop, Yonan had reckoned it; when Yonan left, Yisi reckoned it; Yisi reckoned it for some years and said each time he reckoned it he had a headache three days; the last two years he had been away with Lord Guo, and those who reckoned it at the monastery had changed several times, and every year it came out a day or two off, and everyone felt uneasy. Elder Azarel asked Li Su to look at it. Li Su came, spread the Syriac calendar and the Tang calendar on one table, compared them, reckoning on his fingers, reckoned three days, and on the fourth brought a sheet of paper with the dates of Easter for the next ten years written on it line by line, the Tang month and day on the left, the Syriac on the right. Jingjing, holding the paper, asked him how, in the Tang calendar, the day of worship could be recognized. Li Su said the *Xiuyao jing* had the seven luminaries—sun, moon, Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus, Saturn—turning once in seven days; in the Sogdians' almanacs the sun's day was marked with the character *mi*, honey, and the Sogdians called that day the *mi* day; the Sogdian merchants of the West Market all had such almanacs at home. Jingjing said marking it *mi* was all right, but don't say "the sun's day" out loud; the Tang would hear it and think we worship the sun.

The next year the papers from the Directorate of Astronomy came down, and Li Su went into the palace to observe the stars and reckon days for the Son of Heaven, and did not come often. Now and then he still came, stood at the very back during worship, and left when it was over. Once Jingjing ran after him to the gate and asked him what it was like observing the stars in the Directorate of Astronomy. Li Su stood at the gate under the lamp, thought, and said: they're the same stars as looking here.

---

In the second month of the second year of Dali, Pei Shiyiniang bore a son.

The child was born at night, after most of a night's labour. Yisi stood outside the door most of the night, Jingjing stood with him, and Elder Ishaq boiled water on the stove, pot after pot. Just before light a cry came from the room, a very loud cry, so loud the dogs next door all began to bark. The old midwife came out and said it was a boy, a sturdy one; Yisi nodded and did not go in; first he knelt down at the door, knelt on the frozen ground, and recited a passage of scripture in the country speech of Balkh. Jingjing stood behind him and listened. Never in his life had Jingjing seen his Pa kneel and recite scripture so urgently, one word chasing the next, as if afraid that if he were slow someone would snatch it away.

On the third day after the child was born, two aunts came from Shiyiniang's family, to give the child the third-day bath by Tang custom. The third-day bath was an old Tang rite: a basin of fragrant water is heated, coins, jujubes and scallions are scattered in it, the old midwife chants as she washes, and when the washing is done the relatives add money to the basin. Narsai heard of it, came and stood at the door of the side courtyard, and said that before baptism the child must not be washed in another religion's water; scallions and jujubes scattered in the basin were what the Tang begged of their gods. Yisi, inside the door, looked at Narsai, looked at the two aunts, and did not know whose side to take. Shiyiniang sat up on the bed and said: we won't scatter anything in the basin, and we won't take the money; but the child has to be washed; born three days, there's blood and birth-grease on him; wash him, and he's clean. Narsai stood a while and said nothing more. The water in that basin Jingjing went to the kitchen to heat; he boiled it and let it cool to warm, carried it over, tested it with his elbow, added a dipper of cold, tested it again, and only then carried it into the room. The two aunts watched this *hu* monk squat by the basin and test the water with his elbow, glanced at each other, and later said to Shiyiniang: that elder son of yours has hands finer than a woman's.

On the eighth day after the child was born, he was baptized. Those days fell in the Great Fast; the monastery's people ate no meat, touched nothing rich; but baptism did not wait. By the Patriarchate's rule, a baptism needed a godfather, who made the vows for the child, and when the child grew up the godfather was to teach him the scriptures. Yisi had Jingjing be godfather. Jingjing held the child and stood by the bronze basin; the water in the basin he had heated himself, mixed till it no longer scalded the hand, tested three times with his elbow. Elder Marya asked the child's name; Yisi looked at Jingjing and said: you're the godfather; you name him. Jingjing looked down at the red wrinkled little face in his arms, thought a while, and said: call him Adam, the same as me. Adam was his own name; everyone in the monastery knew it. Yisi stood still a moment, and after a while nodded. Marya scooped up water and poured it on the child's head, and the child cried, cried louder than when he was born.

When the baptism was over, Elder Samaan caught Jingjing at the door of the hall and said: you've been godfather, and from now on the child's learning of the scriptures is yours; but your own office is still "for the time being." The order says you're to be ordained when you come of age; by the Patriarchate's rule a deacon must be a full twenty-five; you're twenty-one this year, four years short; and even when you're of age, to be ordained deacon a bishop must lay on hands; Chang'an has no bishop; even if the Patriarchate sent a chorepiscopus, a country bishop, a country bishop can't ordain a deacon; it has to be a city bishop. As he said it his two hands were folded in his sleeves, and he looked at the bronze basin in the hall, and said he had entered the matter in the accounts, and when he next wrote to the Patriarchate he would put it in. Jingjing said he wasn't in a hurry. Samaan said: you're not in a hurry; he was; the rule was his charge, and with a piece missing from the rule he couldn't sleep at night.

The Tang name was given by Pei Shiyiniang: Lingbao. She said that when she was small her family had kept a copy of the *Lingbao Scripture*, a Daoist one, and her father said the meaning of the two characters *lingbao* was the most spiritual treasure between heaven and earth; she didn't understand Daoism, but she loved those two characters. When Narsai heard that the child's Tang name was taken from the characters of a Daoist scripture, he frowned, came and stood a moment before Yisi, said nothing, and left. Two days later he could not hold it in after all, and stopped Yisi after a meal, and said: the child is baptized, named Adam, but his Tang name is the name of a Daoist scripture; when he grows up and people ask him what Lingbao is, and he says it's a Daoist scripture—what is that? Yisi thought and said that when Alopen's generation translated the scriptures they called the Lord Tianzun, the Heavenly Worthy; the stele had not been set up, the scrolls were all in the cupboard, and the elder had seen them; he said he wasn't saying it was good to borrow a Daoist name; he was saying that when the child's mother didn't know Alaha, she knew these two characters; she had given the child the best two characters she knew, and he couldn't make her take them back. Narsai said: that's living in another's house. Yisi said yes, living in another's house: the walls are theirs, and the lamp lit inside is ours. Narsai looked at him a while and said: Jingjing taught you that. Yisi said yes, he'd heard it at the west wing door the year before last. Narsai snorted and left, went a few steps, turned his head and said: when the child learns his scriptures, he would come and teach the Syriac letters. Yisi said good. When Jingjing later heard Shiyiniang tell of it, he thought of those three evenings when the elders debated the translating, of what Narsai had said and of what he himself had answered; he had thought at the time that his Pa, sitting to one side, had taken in none of it.

---

When the baptism was over, Jingjing went to the scripture room to make the entry in the register.

The monastery had a register of baptisms that had existed since the Zhenguan years; it had been replaced several times, and this one had been begun early in Tianbao, with a parchment cover, its pages already yellowed. Everyone baptized at the monastery had a line written in it: the year and month, the name, the parents, the godfather. This register used to be in Yonan's keeping; when Yonan left, Yisi kept it; these two years, with Yisi away, it had been given to Babai; Babai's eyes were bad now, and so this time he had Jingjing write it himself. Jingjing turned to the last written page and wrote in Syriac: In the year one thousand and seventy-eight of the Greeks, in the month of Shebat, baptized one male infant, named Adam, Tang name Lingbao. Son of Yazdbozid and Martha. Godfather, Adam. The two Adams written in one line—he looked at them, and did not change it. When he had written it he blew on the ink and waited for it to dry.

While waiting for the ink to dry, he leafed back through the register. He wanted to see his own line, to see who had baptized him, who his godfather was; he had never looked. As he leafed, he turned the years of the Greeks into Tang reign years in his head, leafed back past the second year of Zhide, past the fourteenth year of Tianbao, past the tenth year of Tianbao, to the year one thousand and fifty-eight, which was the fifth year of Tianbao, and found the month of Teshri; Teshri was the ninth month of the Tang calendar.

That line was in Syriac letters, written very thin, each letter slanting a little to the right, with a small hook where the stroke ended. Jingjing knew this hand: he had seen it under the lamp in Xizhou, seen it in that sand tray at the door of the east wing, seen it in the drafts of Elder Yonan's letters to the Patriarchate. That line said: "In the year one thousand and fifty-eight of the Greeks, in the month of Teshri, baptized one male infant, named Adam. Son of A-Lian. Received by Yazdbozid and his wife." Below that line there was a mark of signature, four Syriac letters, crooked, stroke by stroke, as if written by someone just learning to write: Alaph, Lamadh, Yudh, Nun.

Jingjing stood in the scripture room, his hand pressed on that page, pressed till his palm was damp. The ink of his own line had long since dried. He read that page through again, read the words "Son of A-Lian," read the four letters beneath, read back to the word "Adam." Of those baptized at the monastery in the ninth month of that year, there was only this one male infant. Adam was his name. A moment ago he had been holding another Adam, standing by the bronze basin, saying: call him Adam, the same as me.

He dipped the pen in ink again, and in the empty space beside that line wrote two Tang characters: Jingjing. When he had written them he looked at the two Tang characters beside that line of Syriac, one horizontal and one vertical, one slanting right and one standing square; however he looked at them, they did not look as though they belonged to one family. He thought again of what Narsai had said that evening under the window: to vow and not keep it is to lie before the Lord. He thought that Elder Yonan had vowed. He thought again of the woman who made that mark, Alaph, Lamadh, Yudh, Nun, four crooked letters, someone just learning to write, at the rear door of the hall, holding the pen held out to her, writing her own name, and when it was written, leaving the child and going away. He did not know what she looked like, whether there were chilblains on her hands, whether, standing in the last row of the hall, she had looked toward the bronze basin. He thought a while, took the small paper-knife from his sleeve, and scraped the two Tang characters away bit by bit, scraped very lightly, afraid of tearing the paper, and when they were gone pressed the fibres of the paper flat with his finger.

He closed the register, put it back in the cupboard, locked it, and went out to help clear up the things in the hall. When they were cleared, he ate supper; at the table Elder Ishaq said Lingbao's cry was loud, he'd surely have a fine voice for chanting scripture, and everyone laughed, and Jingjing laughed too.

---

That night Jingjing went to the side courtyard.

Yisi was not yet asleep; he sat under the lamp in the outer room working at accounts; in the inner room Shiyiniang and the child were asleep. Jingjing stood a while at the door, went in, and sat down across from Yisi. Yisi raised his head and looked at him, and put down his brush, as if he had long been waiting for him.

Jingjing said that today making the entry in the register, he had leafed back, back to the ninth month of the fifth year of Tianbao. He said that line was in Elder Yonan's hand; he knew it. He said beneath it was a mark, four letters: A-Lian. He asked his Pa who A-Lian was.

Yisi sat there, his two hands on his knees, and only after a good while spoke. He spoke very slowly, in the country speech of Balkh, which those asleep in the inner room would not understand; he spoke as slowly as when Jingjing was small and he was teaching him his letters. He said A-Lian was a woman who washed bowls at the stove of an eating-house in the West Market; that year she fell sick, and Elder Yonan went to treat her; afterwards she came to the monastery to hear the preaching and was baptized; later still she was with child, and went away on her own; on the fifth day after the child was born she carried him to the door of the side courtyard and begged him to take him as his own. He said he took him; his former wife took him from her, washed the child's body in warm water, and raised him six years, raised him till she died of her illness. He said that on the day the child was baptized, A-Lian stood in the last row of the hall, and when the baptism was over, Elder Yonan took the register out and had her make her mark. He said that that night Elder Yonan came to the side courtyard and told him under the jujube tree: the child was Yonan's.

Jingjing listened throughout, and when it came to this, his hands clenched. Yisi said again that Elder Yonan had begged him that night not to tell the child; when the child grew up, when one day Yonan himself came back, he would tell him himself. He had promised; but he had also told Yonan that if one day the child asked him, he would not lie. He said: "Today you've asked."

The room was very quiet; from the inner room came the sound of the child turning over; Shiyiniang murmured in her sleep, and then there was no sound. Jingjing sat there looking at the lamp; a snuff had formed on the wick; he reached out to trim it, and took two tries to cut it off. When it was trimmed, he asked his Pa: Elder Yonan has been back nine years—why has he never said? Yisi said he didn't know; he had never asked; that was Yonan's affair. Jingjing asked again: why didn't Pa say either? Yisi said he had promised Yonan; if you didn't ask, he wouldn't say; he had thought too that if you never asked all your life, he would never say all his life, and you would be his son all your life, and that too would be good. Here he stopped, and after a while brought out the second half: "That part was my own wish. Not all for Yonan's sake."

Jingjing bowed his head and looked at his own hands. His hands were big, the fingers long, the nails cut very even; writing Tang characters had worn a callus on his middle finger. He remembered Elder Yonan under the lamp in Xizhou sitting across from him learning to write the character for *lotus*, writing one character twenty-odd times, and when it was done saying to him: the way you write, you're like someone. He remembered the first time he met Elder Yonan in Ctesiphon, Yonan looking at him, looking a good long while without even blinking. He remembered how in these ten years Elder Yonan had every evening been quick to claim the lighting of the lamp at the gate, and when it was lit had stood there looking toward the ward gate. He remembered asking his Pa when he was small why his eyes were dark when Pa's were brown, and Uncle's were brown, and Pa saying: like your mother's side.

He asked: "Pa, when you told me my eyes were like my mother's side—was that a lie?"

Yisi said: "No. You're like A-Lian."

Jingjing looked at him. Yisi's face under the lamp was half light and half dark, the scar on his left brow quite still. Jingjing looked at the scar, and asked one thing more: "Pa, in your life, have you ever lied?"

Yisi said: "I have. Not about this."

He did not go on, and Jingjing did not ask further. The two of them sat under the lamp until the oil was nearly gone; Jingjing stood up and added a little oil to the lamp-bowl, and when he had added it did not sit down again, but stood there and said: "I'll still call you Pa."

Yisi raised his head and looked at him. He wanted to say something; his lips moved and did not get it out; he only nodded once. Jingjing pushed open the door and went out. It was very cold outside; the ward drum had long since finished; he walked along the foot of the wall back to his own room, and passing the monastery gate saw the lamp still burning, a thin frost settled on the paper cover. He stood a while under the lamp, reached out and brushed the frost off the paper cover, and when he had brushed it off, went back to sleep.

Before light the next day, Yisi went to the hall as usual. Jingjing went too, as always, kneeling half a pace behind him. When worship was over, the two of them went out, and at the door of the hall Yisi untied a ring of keys from his belt, found one among them, of brass, worn bright, took it off and handed it to Jingjing, and said: this is for that cupboard in the scripture room; the register of baptisms is in it; Elder Yonan used to keep it, then he did; from now on you keep it. Jingjing took it and held it tight in his hand, the key still warm from his Pa's body. He asked his Pa whether Elder Samaan would need this reported to the Patriarchate first. Yisi said it would have to be reported; he'd write it in when he wrote; and having said it he smiled and said: the elder will be sure to make an entry. Jingjing smiled too, and hung the key on his own belt, with his ring of keys to the charity storeroom, and when he walked they jingled.

---

A few days later Jingjing went to the West Market. He went in at the north gate, through the silk-sellers, the medicine-sellers, the sellers of gold and silver ware, south, until he came to that narrow lane. The *hu* eating-house at the mouth of the lane was still there, a great pot still set up at its door, mutton bones still boiling in it; but the keeper had changed: a young Sogdian, who said the shop had come down from his grandfather, and his grandfather had died some years back. Jingjing asked whether, twenty years ago, among those who washed bowls at this shop's stove, there had been a woman called A-Lian. The young keeper shook his head and said in those days he was still at the breast. An old granny peeling scallions in the kitchen heard, raised her head, peered at Jingjing a while, and said: there was, there was one, called A-Lian, got chilblains on her hands every winter, winter after winter; later she took up with a *hu* monk, her belly got big, she had a child, gave it away, and she left too, left twenty years ago and never came back. When the old granny had said it she bent to her scallions again, peeled two, raised her head again and asked Jingjing whether he was from the monastery; Jingjing said he was; the old granny said: then do you know that *hu* monk? That *hu* monk was a good man; in winter he brought her medicine.

Jingjing stood at the kitchen door a while, thanked the old granny, and started back. When he reached that corner of the wall at the very end of the lane, he stopped. At the foot of the wall rotten vegetable leaves and charcoal ash were heaped, the ground was wet, the sun did not get in. He stood there a while, his head empty, stood till the damp came up through the soles of his feet, and only then noticed he was still carrying the medicine he had got at Shopkeeper Wang's shop—eye medicine made up for Babai—and left.

That night he went back to the side courtyard, and Pei Shiyiniang was waiting for him under the lamp and brought him a bowl of hot soup. He drank the soup; Shiyiniang sat across from him sewing Lingbao's little clothes, sewed a while, and without raising her head said: your Pa has told me everything; that year when he told me you were taken in, I knew you'd ask sooner or later. She said she wouldn't say anything; she only wanted to tell you one thing: your Pa, that night, after you left, sat under the lamp till dawn. Jingjing, holding the bowl, drank with his head down, drank till he saw the bottom, and said: *Yi*, give me another bowl of soup.

A few days later, in the evening, Jingjing came out of the west wing and saw Yonan going to the monastery gate with the oil jug, and followed behind him, ten-odd paces back. Yonan came to the stone block, poured out the dregs of oil left in the bronze dish, wiped the dish with a cloth, put in fresh oil, changed the wick, struck the slow-match, and lit it. When it was lit he did not leave, but as always stood under the lamp, looking toward the ward gate. Jingjing stood behind a pillar of the gallery, watching his back, watching his shoulders sag, watching the mud on the hem of that black robe, watching one hand hang at his side, the fingers moving little by little, as though writing in the air. Jingjing took two steps forward, and stopped again. He wanted to call him Elder, and wanted to call him something else; his mouth opened, and neither came out. The ward drum sounded; Yonan turned round, saw him, and asked whether he wanted something. Jingjing said nothing, Elder; he'd come to see whether there was enough oil in the lamp. Yonan said there was; he'd added it; and having said it, walked past him toward the back courtyard. As he walked past, his sleeve brushed Jingjing's arm. Jingjing stood under the gallery till the footsteps could no longer be heard, went to the lamp, and lifted the newly changed wick a little, lifted it till it burned brighter.

In those days Lingbao was fretful at night, and Shiyiniang walked the room carrying him till her legs swelled. One night Jingjing heard, went over and took the child from her, sent Shiyiniang to sleep, and sat himself with the child under the lamp in the outer room. The child cried a while in his arms, cried himself tired, and fell asleep sucking his own fist. Jingjing looked down at him, at his eyebrows, at his nose: the brows faint, the bridge of the nose high, the eyes shut, but Jingjing knew that when those eyes opened they were brown, like Pa's, like Uncle's. He held this child till nearly dawn; Yisi got up to go to the hall, passed through the outer room, saw the two of them, stopped, looked a while, made no sound, and pushed the door softly open and went out.

After that, Jingjing did not go to find Elder Yonan, and Elder Yonan seemed to notice nothing. Yonan as always went every evening to light the lamp at the gate, as always washed his hands three times a day, as always sometimes sat at the door of the west wing with a sand tray on his knees, writing characters to himself, still the same one, *heart*, writing and wiping out and writing again. When he came, no one in the room sent him away, and no one greeted him either. When Jingjing raised his head, tired of writing, he would sometimes see him at the door, head bowed, writing, the sunlight on that smooth face of his, so that he looked like a young man sitting there, only the hand that held the bamboo stylus was old. Before, when Jingjing saw that hand, he thought nothing of it; now when he saw it, he moved his eyes away, onto the scrolls, and after a while moved them back.

One afternoon the sun slanted in to the west end of the table and fell on the back of Jingjing's hand. Under the window, Narsai finished trimming a new pen, wiped the knife on his robe, and when it was clean put it into his sleeve. He flicked the nib with his thumbnail, listened, stood up, went to the table, laid the pen by Babai's hand, then glanced at Jingjing and saw that the tip of the brush in his hand had worn bald; he said nothing, went back to sit under the window, and took up another sheet of paper.

That day when Yonan had finished writing a *heart*, he raised his head and asked Jingjing how to write the word *ruanruo*, weakness, in Tang. Jingjing was taken aback; he wanted to ask Yonan what he wanted the word for, and did not ask. On a scrap of cut-off paper he wrote the two characters *ruan* and *ruo* and passed it over. Yonan took it, looked at it a while, laid the scrap beside the sand tray, and copied them. *Ruan* had many strokes; he wrote it five or six times, all in pieces, the cart radical and the yawn radical parting company; *ruo* he wrote somewhat better. He read: "*Ruo*—like two people." Jingjing looked: the two halves of *ruo*, left and right, exactly alike, side by side, both bent at the waist. Yonan looked a while longer and said: "Two people... kneeling." The rest of it he did not finish; he folded the scrap of paper in half, folded it once more, and put it inside his robe.

Note: The *Xutingmishisuo Sutra* writes "cool wind" (*liangfeng*) and calls Jesus *Yishu* ("shifting rat"), and has the line "The cool wind came upon a virgin, named Moyan"; the stele and the *Hymn of the Three Majesties* write "pure wind" (*jingfeng*); "Compassionate Father, Luminous Son, Pure Wind King" and "In the highest of the heavens, deep reverence and praise; on the great earth, all again think on peace and concord" are in the *Hymn of the Three Majesties*. "He hung up the luminous sun to break open the dark realm" is from the stele. Li Su (743/744–817), a Persian whose family lived in Guangzhou, was summoned to the Directorate of Astronomy early in the Dali years, according to the *Epitaph of Li Su* (studied by Rong Xinjiang); his lodging first at the monastery on entering the capital is the novel's invention. The seven luminaries appear in the *Xiuyao jing*; the marking of the sun's day with *mi* in Sogdian almanacs is seen in the annotated calendars of Dunhuang. Myrrh does not appear in the *Xinxiu bencao*, and was first included in herbals after the Song (to be checked). The age of twenty-five for ordination as deacon follows the canons of the Church of the East (to be checked); a chorepiscopus could not ordain to orders above deacon. The Tang custom of the third-day bath appears in the *Tang huiyao* and Tang miscellanies. The register of baptisms is written in the Seleucid era (the "years of the Greeks"); the month of Teshri of 1058 corresponds roughly to September–October 746, and the month of Shebat of 1078 to January–February 767. The register of baptisms, the medicine register, the third-day bath, the naming of Lingbao, and the keys are the novel's invention.
