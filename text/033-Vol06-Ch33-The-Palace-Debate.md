# Volume Six　The Shuofang Army

## Chapter 33　The Palace Debate

> But the Way is not spread abroad without a sage; the sage is not great without the Way. When the Way and the sage fit together like the two halves of a tally, the whole world is enlightened.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

In the first month of the first year of Guangde, Shi Chaoyi, at the end of his road, hanged himself in a wood north of Fanyang.

His head was cut off, packed in a wooden box, and sent west all the way. Counting from the rising at Fanyang in the twelfth month of the fourteenth year of Tianbao, this upheaval had lasted more than seven years.

At first the people of Chang'an hardly dared believe it. Seven years of war had made people forget what it was like not to be at war. The flatbread shops in the West Market set up their ovens again, and fired them three days without anyone coming to smash them, before people slowly began to believe.

The man who finished the war was Pugu Huai'en.

He led the Shuofang troops, with the Uighur Khagan's men and horses, and fought his way northeast from Luoyang. City after city he attacked, and city after city surrendered. The rebel generals of Hebei came out of their gates one after another, knelt by the roadside, and held their seals up over their heads. By summer, Hebei was pacified. The court heaped offices on him, layer upon layer, until he could not remember them all himself.

His daughter had married the Uighur Khagan a few years before. When the Khagan saw him, he called him father-in-law.

---

That year Yisi was in Chang'an.

In the spring Jingjing had come back from the Patriarchate, bringing three elders, a roll of the Patriarch's instruction, and a great heap of business. The monastery had new offices, a new order of things, and every day before light Yisi stood in the courtyard listening to one man talk of rice, another of firewood, a third of yet another child left at the monastery gate last night.

Every ten days or half a month, he went to the Qinren Ward.

Guo Ziyi had a mansion in the Qinren Ward. The mansion was very large, but few people came to its gate. The year before, the late emperor had died and the new Son of Heaven had come to the throne, and there was a powerful eunuch in the palace who disliked Guo Ziyi and said a few words, and Guo Ziyi's command was taken away. Now he lived idle in Chang'an with a few fine-sounding titles and charge of affairs at the late emperor's tomb; and in the mornings he still drank millet gruel, with pickles, and ate a steamed bun.

When Yisi went, the two of them sat in the gallery and said little.

Sometimes Guo Ziyi asked about the monastery. Yisi said there was enough rice to last till autumn. Guo Ziyi said: good. Yisi said that of the three elders, one preached too long, and the children below him fell asleep. Guo Ziyi thought and said: then make him preach standing up.

Sometimes Guo Ziyi asked nothing, only gazed at the locust tree in the courtyard.

Yisi knew that wherever he gazed, he was thinking of somewhere. But behind the locust tree was a wall, behind the wall a ward, behind the ward the city, and behind the city somewhere very, very far away. Yisi did not know where he was thinking of.

---

In the seventh month, Luo Fengxian came back from the north.

Luo Fengxian was a palace envoy, about forty, pale-skinned, soft-voiced, with two very deep lines at the corners of his eyes when he smiled. That year the Uighurs were going back to the steppe north of the desert, and the court sent men to escort them all the way; he was one of the escort. On the way back he passed through Taiyuan, and then Fenzhou. The commander at Taiyuan had an old grudge against Pugu Huai'en, kept him there a few days, and gave him a good many things. Fenzhou was where Huai'en was camped, and Huai'en too kept him there a few days.

What happened next, there are several accounts of.

Some say Huai'en laid on wine and invited him to drink, and around midnight Huai'en's old mother came out from the back hall, pointed at Luo Fengxian and said: "You swore brotherhood with my son, and then turned round and got thick with the Taiyuan side. Whose man are you?"

Some say Luo Fengxian wanted to leave, Huai'en wanted to keep him one more day, and had someone hide his horse.

Luo Fengxian could not find his horse, and panicked. He thought: if they're hiding my horse, they want my life. In the middle of the night he climbed out over the back wall, borrowed a post horse, and rode all the way back to Chang'an.

As soon as he was in Chang'an he submitted a memorial: Pugu Huai'en is conspiring with the Uighurs; his rebellion is plain.

---

A few days after that memorial went up, Huai'en's memorial arrived too.

It was very long. Someone in Chang'an copied it out, and it went round the wards to be read. Yisi never saw the whole of it; he only heard an old believer of the monastery who could read read out a few passages.

In the memorial Huai'en said he had six crimes.

His first crime: when the Tongluo rebelled, he fought the Tongluo for the late emperor. His second crime: his son went over to the rebels in battle and then escaped back, and he beheaded his son with his own hand, so that his men would know that one step back meant death. His third crime: he married his two daughters far away to the Uighurs, to make this alliance for the realm. There were several more after, each longer than the last.

Every crime was a merit.

He said too that of his family, forty-six had died in the king's service.

And he said he dared not come to the capital. That spring a military governor had answered a summons to court, entered the capital, and never come out again; he had been ordered to take his own life. He said: your servant is not afraid of death; your servant is afraid of dying without knowing why.

When the old believer had read this far, he raised his head and asked Yisi: "Master Yi, this man—is he going to rebel?"

Yisi said: "I don't know."

The old believer said: "You fought alongside him."

Yisi said: "I fought alongside him, and I still don't know."

---

At the beginning of the eighth month, someone came from the palace and said His Majesty wished to see him.

Yisi put on the purple robe. Usually he kept it folded, wrapped in a piece of blue cloth, and seldom wore it. The robe was a little big, with room to spare at the shoulders; Jingjing had taken it in two seams for him with a needle, crookedly. As he sewed Jingjing said: Pa, if the people in the palace ask you anything, think it over before you speak.

Yisi said: "I speak slowly anyway."

Jingjing said: "Slow isn't the same as thinking it over."

Yisi looked down at him. The boy was seventeen, and half a head taller than he.

---

The one leading the way was a young eunuch who walked very fast, the hem of his robe flicking and flicking.

Yisi followed behind him, counting as he walked. From the palace gate, a hundred and twenty paces north, there was a gate. Through the gate east, eighty paces, another gate. Then north, a long gallery, the pillars along it lacquered red—one, two, three—and when he had counted to the twenty-seventh, the young eunuch stopped and told him to wait in the gallery.

It was his old habit. In a strange place, first learn the way. How you came in, how you would get out, where there were gates, where there were walls, where you could turn.

Several officials waiting for audience were standing in the gallery too, all in scarlet robes and green; seeing him all in purple, and seeing his *hu* face, they looked at each other and all moved aside a little.

Yisi stood, his two hands hanging at his sides. His belt was empty—no sword or mace could be carried into the palace—and his back was empty too; the shield had been left at the monastery. After standing a while he felt his hands had nowhere to go, so he tucked them into his sleeves.

Having tucked them in, he remembered: Lord Guo stood like this too.

After about the time it takes to eat a meal, someone came out from inside to call him.

---

The side hall was not large, and very dim inside. The windows were pasted with paper, and the light that came through was yellow.

When Yisi went in, there were already people in the hall. Guo Ziyi stood on the left in court dress, his hands in his sleeves, his eyes on the floor. Luo Fengxian stood on the right, nearer His Majesty's seat, a smile on his face.

His Majesty sat above, a little past thirty, his face somewhat long, two blue patches under his eyes.

Yisi knelt and knocked his head on the floor.

His Majesty looked at him a while and said: "I remember you."

Yisi did not raise his head.

"That day at Xiangji Temple," said His Majesty, "I was with the centre, and you came back from the east. There was a flap of skin hanging over your face; you tore it off yourself and threw it on the ground. One of the men beside me was sick on the spot."

That year His Majesty had still been the Prince of Guangping, Marshal of the Armies of the Empire. He had sat under the tallest banner of the centre, and Yisi had only seen his horse from far off.

"Yes," said Yisi.

"That day it was you who guided Pugu Huai'en?"

"Yes."

His Majesty nodded. "Then speak. Luo Fengxian says Huai'en is rebelling. Huai'en says he is not. Lord Director Guo said just now that Huai'en is not. What do you say?"

The hall was very quiet.

Yisi glanced to the left. Guo Ziyi was still looking at the floor, motionless, as if what passed in the hall had nothing to do with him.

Yisi glanced to the right. Luo Fengxian was looking at him too, the two lines at the corners of his eyes smiling deeper.

"Your servant is a *hu*," said Yisi. "He speaks Chinese slowly. May Your Majesty permit him to speak slowly."

"Speak."

---

"Your servant and General Pugu are not of one party," said Yisi.

He said it word by word, biting each one clear.

"When your servant first joined the Shuofang army, General Pugu laughed at him in the tent and called him a juggler. Later in the field he didn't talk with your servant much either. Your servant does not know what is in his heart."

"That day at Xiangji Temple, Lord Guo had your servant guide him round behind the canals to the east. He followed your servant. The fog was very thick; he couldn't see where your servant was, only hear his horse. He followed one horse into an ambush of several thousand men. Of those several thousand in the east, he let not one get away."

"Your servant does not know what is in his heart," said Yisi again. "Your servant knows only that if he had meant to rebel that day, he need not have followed your servant. He had only to turn his horse's head west, and the back of the centre would have been empty."

His Majesty said nothing.

Yisi could have stopped there.

He glanced to the right.

"Envoy Luo stayed three nights in General Pugu's camp," said Yisi, "and saw that he means to rebel. Your servant fought several years at General Pugu's side and cannot see it. Either Envoy Luo's eyes are good, or your servant's eyes are bad."

He paused.

"Your servant's eye has had an arrow in it."

In the corner of the hall a young eunuch could not hold it in and snickered, then hastily covered his mouth.

The smile on Luo Fengxian's face did not change.

Yisi went on: "Lord Guo sees it the same way. Lord Guo judges men better than your servant."

When this sentence was said, Guo Ziyi on the left moved. A very small movement: the fingers of the hands tucked in his sleeves stirred. Yisi did not see it.

---

Luo Fengxian took half a step forward and bowed towards the throne.

"Your Majesty," he said, his voice soft and very amiable, "your slave makes bold to ask the Great Virtue Yi one question."

His Majesty raised his hand.

Luo Fengxian turned to Yisi and asked with a smile: "The Great Virtue Yi's present office is Associate Deputy Military Governor of Shuofang?"

"It is."

"The Military Governor of Shuofang was formerly Lord Director Guo. Now the troops of Shuofang are in General Pugu's hands," said Luo Fengxian. "The Great Virtue Yi is Deputy of Shuofang, and speaks for the Governor of Shuofang. Lord Director Guo is the old commander of Shuofang, and speaks for the old general of Shuofang. The Great Virtue Yi says Lord Director Guo judges men well, and Lord Director Guo said just now that Huai'en is not rebelling."

He bowed again towards the throne and said: "Your slave is dull-witted, and only listens; but it sounds as though, in the Shuofang army, they are all one family."

When he had said it he stepped back to his place, his hands hanging, and spoke no more.

The hall was silent a long time.

His Majesty did not look at Yisi, nor at Luo Fengxian. He looked at Guo Ziyi.

Guo Ziyi was still looking at the floor.

His Majesty looked a long time before he said: "You may all withdraw."

---

Outside the palace gate, the sun was fierce.

Guo Ziyi walked ahead and Yisi behind, and neither said a word. Outside the gate Guo Ziyi's attendants brought up his horse; Guo Ziyi mounted, looked back at Yisi once, and said: "Come and eat this evening."

Then he went.

Yisi stood in the sun outside the palace gate a while. The purple robe was very hot to wear, and his back was all sweat.

That afternoon, there was talk in Chang'an.

Where the talk came from, no one knew. But by evening the old man who sold vegetables at the monastery gate was already telling people: Lord Director Guo had a *hu* monk speak for Pugu Huai'en before His Majesty. The *hu* monk spoke too hastily, and talked Lord Director Guo into it as well.

When the old man had told it, he lowered his voice and said: "You know who that *hu* monk is? He's from this monastery of ours."

The listeners all turned their heads and looked in through the monastery gate.

---

A few days later the court sent a chief minister to Fenzhou to reassure Huai'en and urge him to come to court.

Before that, someone at court had suggested that Huai'en was Lord Director Guo's old subordinate, and that if Lord Director Guo went it would be better than anyone else going. It had been suggested once, and afterwards no one suggested it again.

Yisi heard it from Official Pei. When Official Pei had told him, he looked at him and said nothing more.

---

That evening Yisi went to the Qinren Ward.

Guo Ziyi was eating in the back courtyard. A small low table, a bowl of millet gruel, a dish of pickles, two steamed buns. The lamp stood at the corner of the table, lighting half his face.

Yisi came in, and did not sit. He stood before the table, stood a while, and knelt down.

Guo Ziyi did not tell him to get up, nor look at him; he broke off half a bun, dipped it in a little of the pickle juice, and chewed slowly.

"I've done you harm," said Yisi.

Guo Ziyi finished chewing the mouthful of bun, drank a mouthful of gruel, and only then said: "Of what you said, was one sentence false?"

"No."

"I know none was," said Guo Ziyi.

He put down his bowl.

"At Xiangji Temple you took an arrow for me," he said. "Not one—three. I remember."

Yisi kept his head bowed.

"On the battlefield, taking an arrow for me is saving me," said Guo Ziyi. "At court, taking a word for me is sometimes doing me harm."

He said it very evenly, as if saying it would rain tomorrow.

"An arrow comes from the other side; you can see it. A word doesn't," said Guo Ziyi. "Which side a word comes from, which way it goes, whom it falls on—you can't see. I can't see all of it either. I've lived a year in this city of Chang'an, and to this day I see only half of it clearly."

Yisi knelt there and made no sound.

"What Luo Fengxian said today," said Guo Ziyi, "was said for the ears of the men behind His Majesty. Tomorrow those men will go and tell His Majesty: the Shuofang army is all one family."

"Lord Guo—"

"I don't matter," said Guo Ziyi. "I'm old, I have no troops; they can't do anything to me—at most make me sit idle another couple of years."

He paused, took up his chopsticks, picked up a strip of pickle, and put it down again.

"What matters is Huai'en."

Insects were chirring in the courtyard. The lamp wick spat, the flame shot up and sank again.

"Now he's sitting in Fenzhou," said Guo Ziyi, "with troops in his hand, an old mother at home, and the Uighur Khagan for a son-in-law. Men in Chang'an say he's rebelling; he writes that he isn't. He doesn't dare come, afraid that if he comes he won't go back. The court sends men to persuade him, and the more men persuade him, the more afraid he is. The more afraid he is, the more the men in Chang'an say he's rebelling."

Guo Ziyi raised his head and looked at Yisi. His eyes, under the lamp, were a little cloudy.

"Even if Huai'en has no thought of rebellion," he said, "he'll be driven to one."

Yisi looked at him.

"Get up," said Guo Ziyi. "The floor's cold."

Yisi stood up. His knees had gone a little numb from kneeling, and he swayed as he rose.

"Go back," said Guo Ziyi, "and put that purple robe of yours away. From now on, if no one sends for you, don't go into the palace. If someone sends for you, come to me first."

"Yes."

"The rice at the monastery—enough to last till autumn?"

"Enough."

"And after autumn?"

Yisi thought and said: "After autumn, we'll see."

Guo Ziyi nodded. He took up his bowl again.

The gruel had long gone cold, and a thin skin had formed on top. He pushed the skin aside with his chopsticks, bent his head, and mouthful by mouthful drank the gruel to the end.
