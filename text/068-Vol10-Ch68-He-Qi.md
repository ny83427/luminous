# Volume Ten　Morning Praises

## Chapter 68　He Qi

> Its soil yields fire-washed cloth, incense that calls back the soul, bright-moon pearls and night-shining jade.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

In the first month of the fifth year of Kaicheng, the Son of Heaven died. People in the city said the Son of Heaven had died of vexation: after the Sweet Dew year he could decide nothing in the palace; whomever the eunuchs wished to raise they raised; the Son of Heaven sat in his hall like a block of wood, sat five years, sat himself sick, was sick a winter, and in the first month was gone. The eunuchs raised the Son of Heaven's younger brother, the Prince of Ying; the Prince of Ying took the throne; the city hung white; feasting and music were forbidden in the wards; the wine shops of the West Market shut their doors for a month. The monastery, following the old rule entered in Samaan's accounts, recited scripture once in the hall for the late emperor; the accounts recorded that when a Son of Heaven died, the monastery recited scripture—the psalms, without reciting the Son of Heaven's name.

That year morning devotion numbered twenty-three. In the twelfth month two old believers had died: one the old man who kept a vinegar works at the east end of the ward, one an old woman of the last of those seven households come back from the south of the city; when she died she clutched in her hand a knotted cord she had brought back from the south of the city; those dozen-odd years in the south of the city she had told it every day, told it forty years, till the knots shone. Jingfu laid that knotted cord in her coffin, and it was buried with her.

---

He Qi came back from Liangzhou in the second month. This time he had been on the road the better part of a year—to Liangzhou, to Ganzhou, into the Uighurs' country—and bought a load of goods: wool, horsehides, a few bolts of Uighur brocade, and a small chest of jade passed along from Khotan; he said this trip had doubled his capital, but he did not mean to travel again; he had come back to sell up the shop.

He told his sister first. That night he came to Lingbao's house carrying a leg of mutton and two bottles of grape wine; He Siniang stewed the mutton, and the three of them sat in the room; Xiaoman was two, walking about on the floor, walking a few steps, sitting down, getting up again, walking to He Qi's feet and hugging his leg; he lifted her onto his knee and fed her a piece of mutton. He said: Sister, he was going back to Xizhou.

He Siniang asked what he was going back to Xizhou for; Xizhou wasn't their home; their family had lived three generations in Chang'an; Pa was born in Chang'an; it was Pa's Pa who came. He Qi said he knew; but the He ancestors came from Xizhou; there were still He clansmen in the city of Xizhou; these years going west he had stayed in Xizhou twice; the clan acknowledged him and told him to come back; Xizhou was governed by the Uighurs now; the Uighurs traded, and the Sogdians kept their accounts, interpreted for them, led their caravans; life was good. He said Chang'an was done.

He Siniang asked how Chang'an was done. He Qi put Xiaoman down, drank a mouthful of wine, and said a great deal. He said: Sister, you don't go out; you don't know; the Tang in Chang'an look at the *hu* with different eyes now. He said when he was small, Pa took him walking in the West Market, and a Tang man meeting a *hu* merchant would call him *hu* sir, haggle over a price, and when the haggling was done clap him on the shoulder; now the Tang in the West Market, when they meet a *hu*, say nothing with their mouths, but there's something in their eyes. He said these years the Uighurs trading in Chang'an, relying on the Uighur khagan being nephew-in-law to the Son of Heaven, swaggered through the West Market, owed Tang money and didn't pay, beat Tang men and paid no penalty; the Jingzhao prefecture couldn't control them; the Tang hated the Uighurs, and hated them till they couldn't tell Uighurs from Sogdians, nor Sogdians from other *hu*; anyone with a high nose and deep eyes, they hated. He said last autumn at the south end of the West Market a Tang cake-seller quarrelled with a Uighur merchant over money and had his arm broken by the Uighur; that same evening the Tang of the West Market surrounded the Uighurs' warehouse and smashed half the street, and smashed not only the Uighurs' but the Sogdians' shops as well; the He shop had a hole smashed in its door-boards; he wasn't there; the two young men in the shop braced it with the bar and kept anyone from breaking in.

He said that was still a small thing. He said this time in Liangzhou he'd heard the Uighurs' country was in turmoil: last winter there was great snow, more than half the cattle and sheep froze to death, the khagan and his chief minister fought, the minister killed the khagan and raised another; people of the tribes fled, some south, some west; with the Uighurs in turmoil the western roads were hard to travel; but the Uighurs in Chang'an would have it harder still; if the Uighur khagan could no longer control his own tribes, whether the Son of Heaven would still recognize this nephew-in-law was hard to say. He said: if the Uighurs fall, the Tang of Chang'an, who have hated the Uighurs for decades, won't hate only the Uighurs. He said too that the new Son of Heaven had been on the throne only a month and already it was rumoured in the city that as Prince of Ying he had loved Daoist arts and kept company with Daoists in his princely mansion; the Daoists were the sworn enemies of the Buddhist monks, and not necessarily friendly to the *hu* monasteries either; he said: Sister, that monastery of yours, that Great Worthy of yours—the days to come won't be easy.

When he had finished, he looked at his sister. He said he had made up his mind: he'd sell the shop to a Tang silk merchant of the West Market—that very Wang who once wanted to marry you; the Wangs offered a fair price; with the shop sold he'd take his household with a camel caravan leaving next month for Liangzhou, and from Liangzhou on west, past Ganzhou, past Shazhou, to Xizhou. He said: Sister, come with me.

He Siniang said she would not go. He Qi said he knew you'd say you wouldn't go; he hadn't come today to hear you say you wouldn't go. He said: you're forty-nine; the Great Worthy is seventy-three, and his eyes can't see; you keep him company; after he goes, you'll be alone in Chang'an, a *hu* widow keeping a *hu* monastery, the monastery's people fewer every year—whom will you lean on then? He said: come with me; in Xizhou there's the clan, there are houses; when you're old, the clan's nephews will care for you to your end; when you die you'll be buried in the He ancestral graves, and the clan will burn paper for you every year.

He Siniang said she didn't want anyone burning paper for her. She said she'd keep the Great Worthy company, and when the Great Worthy went she'd keep the monastery; if the monastery's people grew few, she'd stand there; one standing is one; she was forty-nine; she wasn't going west. She said what you speak of—the house in Xizhou, the clan's nephews, the He ancestral graves—she knew were all good; but they weren't hers.

He Qi said: then what is yours? He Siniang reached out and lifted Xiaoman, who was sitting on the floor, held her in her arms, wiped the grease from the corner of her mouth with her sleeve, and said: this one is hers. She said: this child the Shi Second found under the willow outside the Jinguang Gate; her mother's dead, her father who knows; where she's from, nobody knows; but she's here, in this room, in this ward; every morning she's carried to the monastery to stand beside Old Mother Kang and watch everyone standing; she belongs here. She said it was the same with herself: she'd married once; her husband died at sea, and not even his body came back; she'd kept faith in Chang'an a dozen-odd years; the uncles said she was keeping faith with a dead man; what she kept faith with was here—with this monastery, with these people who stand in the courtyard every day before light.

He Qi drank a mouthful of wine, put down his cup, and turned to look at Lingbao. Lingbao had sat by all along, his eyes unable to see, his head leaning forward, listening to the sister and brother talk; at this he put down the tea bowl in his hand. He Qi said: Great Worthy, and you? Come with us. He said there were Luminous monasteries in Xizhou; he had been to them; there was one north of the city and another outside it; they had priests and brothers who spoke Syriac better than Chang'an's, and more scriptures than Chang'an's; if the Great Worthy went, he wouldn't be the only one any more. He said: further west, past the Pamirs, is Balkh; the Great Worthy's Pa was a man of Balkh; the Great Worthy's grandfather is buried in Balkh; in all his life the Great Worthy has never gone to take one look.

Lingbao said he had never gone outside the Guannei circuit. He said he was born in Chang'an, grew up in Chang'an, was seventy-three, and the farthest he had been was Xianyang—that time to give the last rites to a dying old believer; he went for a day and came back. He said his eyes couldn't see; to walk he needed someone to hold him up; tell him to walk to Xizhou, to Balkh, and he'd die by the time he reached Liangzhou. He said he knew his Pa was a man of Balkh; he knew his grandfather was buried in Balkh too; Brother Jingjing went once at ten and came back and told him: grandfather's and grandmother's graves are behind the monastery at Balkh, side by side, with a mulberry tree beside them. He said he wanted to go, and could not. He said: He Qi, you go; when you get there, stand a while in the monastery in Xizhou for him; at the offering once in seven, stand at the back; when they break the bread, take a piece, and count it as if he'd gone too. He Qi looked at him, looked at his sister, and looked at Xiaoman in his sister's arms. He urged no more, raised his cup, and drank the rest of the wine.

---

The shop was sold in the third month. The day the Wangs came to take it over, He Qi handed over the account books one by one, and when he had handed them over stood a while at the shop door; the hole in the door-boards he'd had patched; the patch of wood was newer in colour than the rest, plain to see at a glance. He came out of the shop without looking back and came to Yining Ward, to the monastery, to find Jingfu.

Jingfu was picking over rice in the back courtyard that day; He Qi squatted and helped him pick, and as he picked said: Leader, the things he said to his sister that night—the leader has probably heard them too: Chang'an has no future for the *hu*. He said he dared say it to the leader because the leader too was a *hu*'s son; the leader's Pa was a man of An, the "Sesame Cake" of the Shence Army, who died in Huaixi; the leader was born in Chang'an, grew up in Chang'an, spoke Tang better than the Tang; but let the leader take a walk in the West Market: when the Tang look at the leader, they look at the leader's eyes and nose; the leader's Tang they don't hear. Jingfu said he knew. He said when he was small in the ward street people shouted *hu brat* at him; those who shouted it later drew gruel at the gruel shed and called him Great Worthy as they drew it. He said: He Qi, you say Chang'an has no future for the *hu*; he won't argue with you; the road you're taking he can see; what you say is all true; but he's the leader, and the leader has no road west; the leader's road is this monastery gate; where the gate is, there he is. He Qi said: and if the gate is gone? Jingfu, picking the rice, stopped his hands a moment, and said: then he'll stand where there's no gate, and stand at morning devotion just the same. He Qi put the handful of rice in his hand into the basket, stood up, brushed the dust from his hands, and said that before he left he had one favour to ask of the leader. He said this journey west would pass Shazhou and the Moheyan desert; on that stretch one or two caravans in ten come to grief; before he left, he wanted to ask the monastery to bless his caravan. Jingfu said blessing was a priest's business; he couldn't bless. He Qi said he knew; he'd come to ask the leader to bring out the Great Worthy.

Lingbao came held up by He Siniang. That morning He Qi's caravan was waiting on the open ground outside the Kaiyuan Gate: a dozen-odd camels in a line, the loads all tied, the camels kneeling, chewing hay, the camel men sitting by the loads eating cakes. He Qi's household: his wife, two sons and a daughter; the younger son was only seven, sitting on a little camel, looking all about him with curiosity. Not many came from the monastery: Jingfu, Joseph, Elijah, Qushen, Salang, the Shi brothers, and a few old believers of the ward. Lingbao stood at the head of the caravan, He Siniang holding him up; he could not see the line of camels, only a line of dark shapes; he raised his hand and recited in Syriac the passage for those going on a journey: "The Lord shall preserve thy going out and thy coming in from this time forth, and even for evermore"—which Pa Yisi had recited long ago at Lingzhou seeing the Shuofang troops off to war, and Brother Jingjing too in the Shence camp seeing those fourteen soldiers off to Huaixi. When he had recited it he recited it again in Tang, very slowly, and at "from this time forth, and even for evermore" his voice went hoarse.

When he had finished, He Qi came before him, knelt, knocked his head once to him, rose, and went to stand before his sister; he opened his mouth and said: Sister. He Siniang took a little cloth packet from her robe and pressed it into his hand, and said: inside is a wooden cross, the one her husband left long ago; before her husband boarded the ship he left this cross at home, saying the sea was damp and a cross carried there would easily rot; he'd wear it when he came back; he didn't come back; she'd kept the cross a dozen-odd years; you take it; you're going by land; the land isn't damp. He Qi gripped the cloth packet and tucked it into his robe, and said: Sister, remember: if one day you want to leave, go west, to Xizhou, and ask for the He family, for He Qi; every Sogdian in the city of Xizhou knows him. He Siniang said she would remember.

The camel bells rang. The camels rose one after another, hind legs first, then forelegs; the loads swayed a few times and steadied. He Qi mounted the first camel, turned back, looked once at the Kaiyuan Gate, looked once at the little crowd standing under the city gate, turned his head, and the caravan went west, the bells growing distant beat by beat. By the great road going west outside the Kaiyuan Gate stood an earthen milepost, and on the post was written *West to Anxi, nine thousand nine hundred li*; the Four Garrisons of Anxi had been lost for decades; the milepost still stood, its characters blown illegible by the wind; the caravan passed by the milepost, went on to the edge of the sky, and was gone. Past the Long mountains, Liangzhou and Shazhou were both in Tibetan hands; the caravan would have to pay duty at the Tibetan barriers; He Qi said he'd been through three times; the Tibetans took the money and let people pass.

He Siniang held Lingbao up and walked back; in the gateway Lingbao said: what's written on that milepost is nine thousand nine hundred *li*. He Siniang said she didn't know; she couldn't read that many characters. Lingbao said he couldn't see it either; when he was small Brother Jingjing took him out of the city and pointed to that milepost and read it to him; Brother said the year he was ten he went west from here, went to Balkh, went to the Patriarchate, and didn't go the full nine thousand nine hundred *li*, but it was far enough—so far that when he came back, Pa's beard had gone half white.

---

That autumn, news from the west reached Chang'an every so often. First it was said that a great general of the Uighurs, at feud with the khagan, had brought in the Kirghiz—the Kirghiz, a tribe far away in the north, tall, red-haired, green-eyed—a hundred thousand horsemen, who broke into the Uighurs' royal tent, killed the khagan, and burned the city; the Uighurs' royal tent—what that city was called, few in Chang'an could say; they knew only that it was north of the desert, on the steppe, where the Uighurs had lived nearly a hundred years. Later it was said the Uighurs had scattered, broken into several bands; one band had fled west, to Anxi and Beiting; one band fled south, and was said to be heading for Tiande and Zhenwu, to beg a mouthful of food beneath the walls of the Tang frontier towns.

Of the Uighurs' warehouses in the West Market several closed in those months; some said their Uighur masters had gone north overnight to seek their own tribes; some said the masters dared not leave and hid in their warehouses without going out, while young Tang men stood outside the doors all day, craning to peer in. These were all rumours; they reached Yining Ward; the people of the ward talked them over once and talked no more.

From He Qi there was no news at all. He had left in the third month; reckoning the days, if the going was smooth he would reach Xizhou in summer; but over in Xizhou, in the Uighurs' country, what sort of turmoil there was now, no one knew. He Siniang came to morning devotion every morning as before, standing on the women's side with Xiaoman in her arms; when morning devotion broke up she carried Xiaoman home; when merchants came from the west she went to the West Market to ask, and when she had asked came home and cooked as before. Lingbao did not ask either.

One day in the twelfth month it was snowing at morning devotion. Jingfu stood in the middle of the courtyard, and when the sky greyed looked back and counted: twenty-one. Under the jujube tree stood Lingbao, telling a knotted cord in his hand—the one Brother Jingjing left, of wool, which Elder Ahad had given Brother long ago under the mulberry tree at Balkh; Brother told it all his life and clutched it when he died; Jingfu took it from Brother's hand and gave it to him. He told it, one knot, one name: the monastery's dead—Pa, Ma, Brother, Elder Yonan, A-Lian, Elder Narsai, Elder Samaan, Siyuan, Hanan, Old Cao; when he had told the dead he told the living: Jingfu, Joseph, Elijah, Qushen, Xiaoman, Siniang, Guangzheng. When he came to the last knot, he said: He Qi.

---

Note: In the first month of the fifth year of Kaicheng (840) Wenzong died; the eunuch Qiu Shiliang and others raised the Prince of Ying, who became Wuzong. That same year Wuzong summoned the Daoist Zhao Guizhen and others into the palace; see the *Old Book of Tang*, annals of Wuzong. In the fourth year of Kaicheng the Uighurs suffered great snows, with heavy losses of sheep and horses, and internal strife; in the fifth year the Uighur general Kulug Bagha brought in a hundred thousand Kirghiz horsemen, who broke the Uighur city and killed the khagan; the tribes scattered, one band fleeing west to Anxi and Beiting, one going south beneath the frontier at Tiande and Zhenwu; see the Uighur chapters of both *Tang Histories*. The milepost outside the Kaiyuan Gate reading "West to Anxi, nine thousand nine hundred li" is found in the *New Book of the South* and other notebooks; that it was "not wished to show people ten thousand li" is a Tang saying. "The Lord shall preserve thy going out and thy coming in" is Psalm 121.
