# Volume Twelve　Jade Gate

## Chapter 85　Pure Wind

> He set up the new teaching of the Triune Pure Wind, which is beyond words, to shape the good in man by right faith.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

Crossing the Tianshan took twenty-three days. The Uighur caravan took a road through the mountains. First they followed a river upstream. The valley was narrow, with rock walls on both sides, and ice hung on the rock walls—still hung there in the eighth month—and the river water was white and roared in their ears. After seven days they came out of the valley onto high ground with no trees, only grass, yellow grass, and a great wind.

"This is the top of the mountain," said the Uighur. "Past here, it's downhill."

Going down, the grass was greener every day, and there were more trees every day—pines, blackish green, growing on the slopes in stands. At the foot of the slopes lay a great plain, and on the plain was a river, and by the river were tents, white, scattered one by one over the grass, and beside the tents were sheep, horses, cattle.

Ayi, on horseback, looked at the plain, reined in, and didn't go on. Jingfu led the donkey up beside her. On the donkey's back rode Liu'er.

"Sister, why have you stopped?" Liu'er asked.

"My ma said the steppe was green," said Ayi. "She didn't lie to me."

---

Ayi's mother's people were a small tribe of forty-odd tents, by the river in summer, moving to the valleys in the south in winter. The tribe's headman was a man of middle years with a broad face and narrow eyes. He heard from the caravan's headman how Ayi had come, had her stand before him, studied her face, and sent someone to fetch an old woman.

The old woman was Ayi's mother's aunt, past seventy, half blind, and came on someone's arm. She stood before Ayi and put out her hands and felt Ayi's face—her forehead, her brows, her chin—and when she had felt it all she said something in Uighur. Ayi wept.

"What did she say?" Jingfu asked her afterward.

"She said, those are her brows," said Ayi. "My ma's brows."

Jingfu and Liu'er—the tribe didn't know what to do with them. The headman asked the caravan's headman who this man was. The caravan's headman said he came from the Tang side, from some monastery or other on the Tang side, and had brought the two girls.

The headman turned to Jingfu and looked at his black robe, at his face. Ayi translated beside him. "You're a Tang man?"

"I was born in Chang'an. The Chang'an registers write me down as a man of An, to be sent back to his own place," said Jingfu. "I didn't go to An. I brought Ayi here."

"What teaching are you?"

"The teaching of Daqin. I believe in Aluohe, I believe in Mishihe."

The headman said a few words to the men beside him, and the men beside him all looked at Jingfu, and their faces were not pleasant. Ayi lowered her head and didn't translate.

"What did he say?"

Ayi bit her lip. "He said, the Tang killed our Manichaean teachers, killed our people in Chang'an. A man from the Tang side, and what he believes isn't even Mani—what has he come for."

That night Jingfu and Liu'er had no tent to live in. Ayi stayed in her great-aunt's tent; Jingfu led the donkey and took Liu'er to a lone willow at the edge of the camp, put up a shelter of felt under it, lit a fire, and the two of them squeezed inside.

"Has Sister stopped wanting us?" Liu'er asked.

"No. Your sister is with her ma's family," said Jingfu. "She'll come tomorrow."

Ayi came the next day. She came running, ran all the way to the willow, knelt at the mouth of the shelter, and said, crying, "I told the headman. I said the Director is a good man, the Director took me in at Chang'an when my ma died, it was the Director who took me in, the Director told the Headman of the ward the truth, it was the Director who brought me all the way here. If the headman won't let the Director stay, I'll live with the Director under the willow."

The headman came that evening. He stood under the willow and looked at the shelter, at the three people squatting at its mouth, and had someone carry over an old tent and throw it on the ground.

"Live here," Ayi translated. "When we move in winter, come along. The tribe's rules, you keep. Your teaching, you chant it yourself—don't chant it in the tribe."

---

That winter, Jingfu nearly died. In winter they moved to a valley in the south. The wind in the valley was a little less, but the snow was heavy; one night of storm piled the snow at the door to a man's waist, and the tent door was blocked and had to be dug out from inside. Jingfu didn't know how to pitch a tent, didn't know how to burn dung, couldn't churn butter or dry curd, and no one in the tribe taught him. Ayi taught him. Ayi was spending her first winter on the steppe too; she learned from her great-aunt and came back and taught him. Liu'er was eleven and learned faster than either of them. She went with the tribe's children to gather dung, brought it back, dried it, and stacked it beside the tent, stacked it very neatly.

In the twelfth month Jingfu fell ill. First cold, then fever. The fever made him confused; he lay in the tent talking nonsense, talking in the speech of Chang'an—about the morning, about the gruel tent, about the lamp, about his master, about Uncle Lingbao, about the Kaiyuan Gate. Liu'er watched over him and wiped his forehead with snow, and when the snow grew warm, changed it for another handful.

Ayi went to beg her great-aunt. "I have a remedy," said the great-aunt, "from the Manichaean temple. A kind of grass root, boiled, and the water drunk. The roots are under the snow. You have to dig."

Ayi dug in the snow a whole morning, found it, brought it back and boiled it, boiled it black and bitter, and poured it down Jingfu's throat.

In Jingfu's bundle there was still a piece of ginger brought from Xizhou, dried out, shrunk to a little lump, hard as stone. Liu'er found it, scraped a little powder off with a knife, and put it in the pot of root water to boil with it; what came out was bitter and hot both. Jingfu drank it for three days, and the fever broke.

The day he came to, he saw a little light leaking in through the smoke hole at the top of the tent, white—snow light. Liu'er was asleep, sprawled beside him, still holding the knife she had scraped the ginger with. He lay looking at that bit of light and thought of his master. His master had feared cold water all his life and drunk ginger broth all his life; he had never feared cold water and had hardly ever drunk ginger broth. Now he had drunk it.

The days he was ill, an old man from a neighbouring tribe wintering in the same valley came every day and stood a while at the tent door, didn't come in, stood a while and left.

"What that old man's name is, I don't know," Ayi told Jingfu once he was well. "People in the tribe call him Old Sa. In his tent there's a cross hanging."

---

At the start of the first month, Jingfu first kept the three days of the Nineveh fast. By the rule of the Church, three weeks before the great fast there are three days in remembrance of Jonah's going to the city of Nineveh to give the word, when the people of the city, from the king to the cattle, fasted and repented. He kept it alone: three days drinking only water, chewing a piece of dry bread at evening. Ayi asked him what he was keeping, and he said it was a city whose people heard one sentence and turned back.

"What happened to the city after?" Liu'er asked.

"The Lord had pity on them and didn't destroy it."

He took out of his bundle the old wooden board from Xizhou. On the front were the characters he had written, the last of them *part*. The back Elijah had carved: on the morning of their parting, Elijah had borrowed a little knife from the old deacon of the monastery north of the city and squatted by the well curb carving for a whole hour, and when he had finished gave it to him.

"Director, for the next few years, I've worked out the day of the Lord's rising by the Church's reckoning, and the months and days in the Tang calendar are all here," Elijah had said. "Once you're on the steppe there'll be no one to check the days with you—look at this."

The back of the board was line after line, and the first line was: *Third year of Dazhong, third month, eighteenth.* From the twenty-ninth of the first month to the eighteenth of the third was fifty days, and that was the great fast.

In the great fast of the Church one eats no meat, no eggs, touches no milk, eats only once a day, and only after the sun has passed west. In Chang'an, during the great fast, the monastery ate bread, beans, pickled greens, millet gruel, and not a drop of mutton fat went into the pot. On the steppe flour was hard to come by and there were no greens; in winter there was only meat and the curd dried in autumn. Jingfu thought about it all night, and the next day told Ayi he would keep it.

"Director, you can't keep it on the steppe," said Ayi. "If you keep it, what will you eat?"

"Go and ask your great-aunt if she has any broomcorn millet."

The great-aunt really had some—half a sack, traded in autumn from merchants come from Xizhou, kept to make gruel for her grandchildren in winter. Ayi carried back a little bag, poured it out, counted it handful by handful—a handful a day, enough to last into the third month.

From the twenty-ninth of the first month Jingfu ate once a day. At evening, when the sun dropped behind the hills, Liu'er boiled a little pot of millet gruel over the dung fire, sprinkled in a pinch of salt, and brought him a bowl. He drank it, licked the bowl's rim clean, and gave the bowl back to Liu'er. On the Lord's day, in the morning, he led Ayi and Liu'er through the morning, finished the scriptures, and only then ate his one meal. By day he gathered dung as usual, went to the river to carry ice as usual, and prayed more than usual: once before light, once at noon, once at sunset, once more at night before lying down.

Liu'er and Ayi didn't keep it, and he didn't ask them to.

In the middle of the second month, with a small part of the bag of millet still left, Jingfu was the first to give out. That morning at the morning he stood chanting the scriptures, and halfway through his sight went black and his knees went, and he knelt in the snow and for a long while couldn't stand. Ayi and Liu'er helped him into the tent. He lay there, his face white, his lips blue, both hands icy.

Ayi turned and went out. When she came back she was carrying a bowl of hot milk, steaming.

"It's camel's milk," she said. "My great-aunt's she-camel still has milk in winter. She had me milk her."

Jingfu looked at the bowl of milk and didn't put out his hand.

"By the rule of the Church, in the great fast, milk may not be touched."

"Director, if you don't drink it, there'll be no one left to keep it," said Ayi, kneeling before him. "You've only been well a few dozen days. If you don't drink, on the day of the rising you won't even be able to stand. On that day, who will lead Old Sa's family in the chanting?"

Liu'er was crying beside him, tugging at his sleeve. "Director, drink it."

Jingfu lay looking at the smoke hole at the top of the tent. After a while he pushed himself up to sit, took the bowl, and held it.

"I know this is breaking the rule," he said. "I'm the one breaking it. If anyone asks hereafter, say Jingfu couldn't keep it and drank milk."

He drank the bowl of camel's milk. From that day on, every evening, what Liu'er brought was a bowl of millet gruel and a bowl of camel's milk. He finished the gruel, and he finished the camel's milk too, and when he had finished he sat holding the empty bowls with his head bowed. Meat he never touched.

---

Old Sa came in the second month. The snow was half melted; the river in the valley had broken up, and the ice went floating down in pieces and struck the stones, cracking. Old Sa came leading his grandson, a boy of ten or so who hid behind him. Old Sa was past sixty, his face dark, his beard white. He stood at the door of Jingfu's tent and said a great string of things in Uighur, and Ayi translated them beside him one sentence at a time.

"My family believes in Daqin. It was my great-grandfather's generation that came to believe, through the Sogdian merchants. Later the tribe's headmen and the rich families all went over to Mani, and my family didn't," said Old Sa. "In my family's tent hangs a cross. Every seventh day the family kneels under the cross and chants the scriptures, in the Syriac the Sogdian merchants taught, not understanding a word, just chanting by the sound—chanting for four generations." Jingfu listened.

"In our tribe there are three more households like this. Four households, twenty-some people, in this valley in winter, by the river in summer. Every seventh day we gather in my tent and chant, and chant to a certain place, and stop," said Old Sa. "My pa said there used to be bread at that place. We have no bread, so we stop."

"I hear you're from the Daqin Monastery in Chang'an," he said. "I'd like to ask you to my tent, on the seventh day, to lead us in the chanting."

Jingfu didn't answer at once. "Grandfather, does your family keep the great feast?"

Ayi translated it across, and Old Sa was taken aback. He said a few words to his grandson and turned back. "My pa said there's a great feast, once a year, the greatest of all," he said. "Which day it was, my great-grandfather's generation knew; by my pa's time no one knew. Four generations, and we haven't kept it once."

Jingfu turned the board over and pressed his finger on the first line. "The eighteenth of the third month," he said. "The day the Lord rose. This year it's the eighteenth of the third month."

---

The seven days before the rising, Jingfu kept strictest of all.

The eleventh of the third month was the Lord's day of Hosanna. Early in the morning he went to the river and broke off a bundle of willow switches, the switches just budding, a little tender yellow. He brought them back and stuck them in at the tent door and gave Ayi and Liu'er one each, told them to hold them up and chant after him: "Hosanna, blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord." Liu'er, holding up her switch, asked who had come that day. Jingfu said the Lord rode a donkey into a city, and the people of the city laid branches on the road and cried Hosanna; and five days later the people of the same city cried, crucify him.

The fifteenth of the third month was the Thursday of the Passover. That evening he broke a piece of bread and shared it with Ayi and Liu'er—it was a meal, it had not been blessed. He told them that it was on the night of this day that the Lord ate bread with his people for the last time.

The sixteenth of the third month was the Friday of the Passion. That day he ate nothing at all, and of water drank only a mouthful. From morning to night he knelt in the tent facing the eastern door and told the knotted cord knot by knot, chanting the same line at every one: Lord, have mercy.

---

The night before the rising, the Church calls the Night of Light. On the evening of the seventeenth of the third month Jingfu went alone to the river. The ice in the river had all melted, but the water was still cold and ran down dark. From his bundle he took a pottery dish, bought in the market at Xizhou, coarse ware, half the size of a palm, about as big as the one at the gate of the monastery in Chang'an. He put a lump of mutton fat in the dish, given by Old Sa's family, white, congealed. He pulled a strand of hemp thread from the lining of his robe, twisted a wick, stuck it in the fat, and struck the flint—three times before it caught. The fat melted, the wick lit, and the flame, small and yellow, wavered in the wind by the river.

He set the dish on a flat stone at the river's edge. The stone wasn't high—about as high as the stone block at the gate of the monastery in Chang'an. Wind came off the river and the flame leaned to one side, leaned down and stood up again. He crouched and cupped his two hands around it, shielding it on the windward side.

When it was fully dark Old Sa came, leading his grandson, and behind him the three households, one family after another, carrying children, helping the old, twenty-some people, and sat down behind the stones by the river. Liu'er and Ayi came too, carrying a felt for him. Ayi's great-aunt didn't come; she had Ayi bring a piece of dried curd.

"Director, what do we do this night?" asked Old Sa.

"Keep watch," said Jingfu. "Keep watch over this dish of lamp until daylight."

"And when it's light?"

"When it's light, you'll know."

That night was very cold. The children fell asleep leaning on the grown-ups; the grown-ups sat wrapped in felt, some dozing, waking, looking up at the little fire—still there—and lowering their heads again. Jingfu crouched before the stone the whole time, his two hands cupped around the flame; when his hands went numb with cold he changed one, then changed back. Liu'er wouldn't sleep. She squeezed in beside him and copied him, cupping her two small hands around it too.

In the second half of the night a gust came up and the flame sank, sank to a point of blue. Jingfu leaned his body forward and blocked it, and slowly the point of blue turned yellow again.

On the mountaintop across the river, it grew pale first.

---

At daylight Jingfu stood up, his legs so numb he couldn't stand steady, and Ayi steadied him. The mutton fat in the dish on the stone had almost burned dry; the flame was still there. He turned round and faced the twenty-some people by the river.

"It's light," he said. "Today the Lord has risen."

Ayi translated it. Old Sa knelt, and his grandson knelt after him, and the three households one by one all knelt, knelt on the wet grass by the river.

---

That noon, in Old Sa's tent, Jingfu led them through their first great feast. The tent wasn't large, and twenty-some people were crammed in. On the pole in the middle of the tent hung a cross, of wood, gone black, hung by a leather cord, facing the eastern door. Twenty-some people knelt on the felt, and Old Sa led the chanting, in Syriac. Jingfu listened. He could hear which passage it was—it was a psalm, it was the Lord's Prayer—but the sounds had gone crooked, badly crooked; some words had turned into Uighur sounds, some had lost half of themselves.

They chanted to a certain place and stopped. The people in the tent knelt, chanting no more, and waited.

Jingfu knew where that was. It was the place of the offering of the bread. Kneeling among those people, he thought of the hall in Chang'an, of the glass bowl, of Uncle Lingbao's hands shaking, of his own left hand holding the foot of the bowl, and the bowl not moving.

He didn't go on at once. He began to speak right there at the stopping place, and Ayi translated beside him one sentence at a time. "Friends, for four generations your families have chanted to here and stopped, knelt, and waited. Today let me tell you: the people of Mishihe once stopped at a place like this too." Old Sa raised his head.

"He was nailed to a piece of wood, buried in a tomb cut from stone, and a great stone was rolled against the tomb's mouth. His people all ran away and hid, thinking it was finished," said Jingfu. "On the third day, before it was light, a few women went to the tomb. They had nothing left, and they still went. When they got there and looked, the stone was rolled away, and the tomb was empty."

The tent was so quiet they could hear the river outside.

"He died and went down into the dark realm. The dark realm has a gate, and the gate is shut fast; from of old till now, no one who went in has ever come out. He went in, and broke that gate open, and walked out himself," said Jingfu. "My master wrote a stele, and on the stele there's a line: *He hung up the luminous sun to break open the dark realm.* Luminous—that is, light."

When Ayi came to the word *light*, her voice stopped a moment before she went on.

"The monastery in Chang'an has been pulled down, my master's stele is buried in the earth. Uncle Lingbao has gone west, alive or dead I don't know. Your families have chanted four generations, stopped here, with no bread, and still knelt and waited," he said. "What's buried in the earth, the Lord can see. What's stopped, the Lord remembers."

From inside his robe he took a piece of bread. Liu'er had baked it that morning, from the last little bag of flour Old Sa's family had hidden away, traded last autumn from merchants come from Xizhou; it wasn't well baked, and one side was scorched. He held the bread in his hands, and Ayi translated beside him one sentence at a time.

"I've never had hands laid on me; I'm not even a deacon. I can't bless the bread," he said. "This bread hasn't been blessed. It's food, Liu'er baked it this morning, and one side is scorched."

He held the bread on his hand. "In Chang'an there's a monastery, and in the monastery there was a priest named Lingbao. Every seventh day, at the offering, he blessed the bread and chanted the words of consecration, chanted very slowly, his hands shaking, and I held the foot of the bowl. Uncle Lingbao has gone west now, to his father's homeland; whether he got there I don't know," said Jingfu. "This bread, I'll break it, and everyone eat. When you eat, remember that One. That One is in the hall at Chang'an, in the hall at Xizhou, at Kashgar, at Balkh, and in this tent too."

"If some day hereafter a man who has had hands laid on him comes, from the west, from the east, and blesses the bread, then everyone will receive it," he said. "And when you receive it you'll know that today's piece was waiting for that day."

He broke the bread, broke it into twenty-some pieces, and handed them round one by one. He handed one to Old Sa, to Old Sa's grandson, to the people kneeling in the tent. At the end there was one piece left, and he handed it to Liu'er.

"I baked it, I won't eat it," said Liu'er.

"You eat," said Jingfu. "You baked it—you remember too."

The people in the tent ate, one by one. Old Sa ate, and when he had eaten said something in Uighur.

"What did he say?"

Ayi's eyes were red. "He said, four generations, and for the first time, at the place where we stop, there's something."

Jingfu broke his fast. That evening what Liu'er brought him was a bowl of mutton broth, and at first he drank only half.

---

In the fourth month of that year the grass by the river turned green again. The tribe moved back from the southern valley to the river. On the day of the move Jingfu walked behind the last high-wheeled cart, came out of the valley, and saw the plain. When they had come the year before, the grass was still green, and not many days later the tips had turned yellow; now it was green again, green as far as the eye could see, greenest by the river, and the ice in the river all melted, the water running bright toward the east.

That evening, when the tent was up, Jingfu found a wooden stake, chest high, drove it into the ground at the tent door, set the pottery dish on top of the stake, added fresh mutton fat, and lit it.

---

On the seventeenth of the eighth month, the tips of the grass had already turned yellow. "This is the day the stele in Chang'an went into the ground," Jingfu told Old Sa.

That day Old Sa's tent was crammed with people again. Four households, twenty-some people, and Jingfu, Ayi and Liu'er besides. Ayi's great-aunt came too, on someone's arm.

"I don't believe," she said. "I've come to look."

That morning Liu'er and Ayi had baked a great stack of bread. The flour Ayi had got by trading a pair of silver earrings her great-aunt had given her to merchants come from Xizhou.

The bread was still bread that had not been blessed. Jingfu broke it, everyone ate, and when they had eaten Jingfu told them about Chang'an, and Ayi translated. He began from the monastery in the Yining Ward, told of the lamp at the monastery gate, of the stele his master wrote, of the edict of the fifth year of Huichang, of that night with a dozen spades, and a blind old man sitting under the jujube tree reciting the characters on the stele from beginning to end.

"The next day after noon, that Daoist came with the Shaofu from the county and asked, where's the stele?" said Jingfu. "I said, buried. The Shaofu asked where it was buried, and I said, in the monastery. When he asked again, I said no more."

Old Sa's grandson laughed and tugged at Ayi's sleeve and asked, "What happened to that Daoist after?" Ayi asked Jingfu.

"That Daoist went away," said Jingfu. "The county men wrote six characters in their register: *Buried. Disposed of as judged fit.*"

The people in the tent sat, some looking at him, some with their heads lowered. Ayi's great-aunt sat by the door, squinting her half-blind eyes.

"That stone—will it ever be dug out?"

"I don't know," said Jingfu. "The stone is in the earth, and the characters are whole. If some day someone digs it up and reads it and knows there was once such a monastery in Chang'an, that's enough."

Just then the tent's door curtain was lifted, and the headman stood in the doorway.

He had heard that in Old Sa's tent people of the neighbouring tribe and people of his own tribe had packed the tent full, and the man from the Tang side was in there talking about something. He stood in the doorway and said a great string of things. Ayi's face went white.

"He says, he said it before: your teaching, chant it yourself, don't chant it in the tribe," said Ayi. "What are you talking about here today."

Jingfu stood up. "I'm talking about Chang'an. In Chang'an there is a monastery, and in the monastery a stele, and today is the day the stele went into the ground, and I'm telling how it was buried. It's the truth I'm telling; if the headman wants to hear, I'll tell it again," he said. "The headman said, don't chant it in the tribe. This tent is Old Sa's family's, and Old Sa is of the neighbouring tribe. But I know that sitting in this tent today are people of the headman's tribe—Ayi is, her great-aunt is too. I won't deny it. If the headman says this counts as in the tribe, I accept it. I won't come again. If the headman wants to drive me out, I'll go."

Ayi translated it across one sentence at a time. The headman listened and said nothing.

Ayi's great-aunt, sitting by the door, spoke then. She spoke very slowly, and the headman listened with his head bowed.

"The Tang in Chang'an killed our Manichaean teachers. This man was sent out by the Tang in Chang'an; his monastery was pulled down too, his stone was buried too." Afterward Ayi repeated these words to Jingfu, without missing a single word. "Our people were killed in Chang'an; his people were driven out of Chang'an. What he's telling today is about his monastery. Listening, it sounds as if he were telling about ours."

The headman stood in the doorway, and after a while let the curtain fall.

"When you've finished, break up," he said outside the door. "Don't go on till dark." His footsteps went away.

"Then hereafter, every year on this day," said Old Sa, "we'll all eat bread and tell it once."

"Good," said Jingfu.

---

When it had broken up, Jingfu walked out of the tent. It was still light; the steppe had entered autumn, and dark did not yet come early. The grass had grown to the horses' bellies, yellow, yellow all the way to the edge of the sky, and at the edge of the sky were mountains, and on the mountaintops snow, white.

From inside his robe he took the knotted cord. Wool, its knots rubbed shining—Elder Ahad's, his master's, given him by Uncle Lingbao. He told it, one knot, one name.

Pa—the sesame cakes—died in Huaixi. Master. Uncle Lingbao. Granny Siniang. Brother Joseph, died at Shazhou. Brother Elijah, Brother Salang. Rijin, Mother Kang. Lü Shao. Qushen. Assistant Yang. Guangzheng. Liu'er. Ayi. Old Sa.

At the last knot he spoke no name, only stood there, holding the cord tight in his hand.

In the tent behind him someone was counting people. It was Liu'er. She was copying what Jingfu did every morning, standing at the tent door, turning back, counting the people in the tent who hadn't yet left, counting them one by one.

"Nineteen," she told Ayi.

The wind passed over the grass. The grass lay down in waves, lay down and stood up again, from near to far, as though a very great hand were stroking the grass, lightly.

Note: After the westward migration of the Uighurs in the mid-ninth century, the tribes north and south of the Tianshan were scattered; many believed in Mani, and some Christians of the Church of the East entered the Uighurs along with Sogdian merchants. Syriac and Uighur Christian remains have been found at Turfan, Khotan, and in the Irtysh valley. That where the Church of the East had no priest the faithful kept a "rite without bread," chanting to the place of the offering of the bread and stopping, is the novel's elaboration. Easter in the third year of Dazhong (849), reckoned by the Julian calendar used by the Church of the East, fell on 14 April, roughly the eighteenth of the third month in the Tang calendar; the great fast of fifty days began about the twenty-ninth of the first month. The Church of the East's rule for the great fast appears in the Synod of Mar Isaac of 410: "keep the full forty days, seven weeks"; the old practice was one meal a day, eaten toward evening, forbidding meat, eggs and milk; three weeks before the great fast came the three-day Nineveh fast (*Ba'utha*); the week before Easter held the Lord's day of Hosanna, the Thursday of the Passover, and the Friday of the Passion. On the steppe there was no fasting food to keep the fast with, so Jingfu kept it with millet gruel, and when his strength failed drank camel's milk, owning it as breaking the rule; when the Kerait tribe converted in 1007, the patriarch specially permitted them to drink milk in the great fast (see Bar Hebraeus's *Ecclesiastical Chronicle*)—see the Epilogue. In winter pastoral herds have very little fresh milk; only camels have a long lactation. The night before Easter the Church of the East calls the Night of Light; the seventh-century patriarch Ishoʿyahb III fixed baptisms on that night. "He hung up the luminous sun to break open the dark realm" is from the stele. Elijah's carving the Easter dates on the back of the board follows Chapter 38, Li Su's reckoning of the Easter date, and Chapter 80, "Different Roads."
