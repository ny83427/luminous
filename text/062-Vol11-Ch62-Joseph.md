# Volume Eleven　Jade Gate

## Chapter 62　Joseph

> One Person came into the world, to save without limit; the sun rose, the darkness perished, and all bore witness to the deep truth.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

Past the Xiao Pass, north-west to Huizhou, and west again, was the Yellow River.

When they reached the bank of the Yellow River, it was already the eleventh month. The river had frozen, but not solid; near the banks it was white and hard, and toward the middle the ice went blue, blue verging on black, and beneath it you could see the water moving.

The ford was held by a band of Tibetan soldiers, and there were a few ferrymen, local men, who spoke Tang speech and wore Tibetan robes. The ferrymen said no boat could cross these days, the ice-shards would smash it; nor could anyone walk the ice, it was still young; wait ten days or a fortnight, till it froze solid, and then it could be walked.

So they waited by the river.

They waited nine days, and the ferrymen said: now it can be walked.

Jingfu stood on the bank, looking at that stretch of ice.

He thought of his master's two rolls of diary. He had read the diary more than once; after his master died he had taken them from his master's room and kept them by his own pillow, and when he could not sleep he would leaf through them. A ten-year-old's writing, crooked and wobbly, a circle drawn for every character he did not know how to write.

The first few days said: Left Lingzhou. Wind. Crossed the Yellow River. Walked on the ice.

Uncle Xu says you can't stop on ice, if you stop you sink. I didn't stop.

Those two rolls of diary he had left with Lü Shao that day outside the Kaiyuan Gate. The words on them he knew by heart.

"You can't stop on ice," said Jingfu.

Kang Zhen looked at him once.

"If you stop you sink," said Jingfu.

He was the first to step onto the ice.

Salang, with the old priest on his back, followed behind him. Behind him came Joseph, Elijah, Kang Zhen, the people of the Zoroastrian shrine, the few *hu* households. One after another, a few paces apart, no one speaking. The ice creaked underfoot, and once it creaked especially loud, as if something had cracked, and a child walking in the middle gave a wail, and his mother clapped a hand over his mouth and dragged him on.

No one stopped.

On the far bank Jingfu looked back once. On the face of the ice a string of footprints ran from this bank to that, curving, like a thread.

---

Liangzhou was a great city.

Greater than Jingzhou, greater than Fengxiang, its walls thick and high, its gates several deep. Jingfu had heard his master say that Liangzhou used to be the first city of Hexi; there were more *hu* in it than Tang, and when a Sogdian caravan came it came with hundreds of camels, there was everything for sale in the streets, and the *pipa* was played better than in Chang'an.

That was all before the Tibetans came.

The Tibetans had held Liangzhou for more than eighty years. The city was the same city, the streets the same few streets, but the people walking in the streets had changed their look. The men wore braids and left-lapped robes and smeared red clay on their faces. The women wore Tibetan robes too, and great strings of beads. Of the voices you heard, six in ten were Tibetan, three Sogdian, one Tang.

Even the Tang had changed. Jingfu had to hear it several times before he understood it.

They lodged in the Sogdian ward of the city.

In the ward there was a Zoroastrian shrine, still standing. The fire in the shrine still burned. Salang carried the old priest of the Zoroastrian shrine to the shrine's door; he climbed down from Salang's back, stood at the door, and looked at the fire within, looked a long time.

Then he went in and knelt by the fire.

He untied the cloth over his jar, turned the jar's mouth toward the fire, and poured a little in. It was ash, greyish white; it fell at the edge of the fire. The flame wavered once, and stood up straight again.

The people of the shrine gathered round and asked him where he had come from. He said Chang'an, the Buzheng Ward. An old man of the shrine, hearing it, sat down and took his hand and asked him many things—asked whether the fire in Chang'an still burned.

The old priest said: the house was pulled down.

No one in the shrine spoke after that.

That evening, the nine of the Zoroastrian shrine all stayed in the shrine. They said they would go no further. Liangzhou had their people, had their fire; going west, who knew where they would end.

The old priest gave his empty jar to Salang.

Salang held the jar and did not know what to do with it. The old priest said: you carried it the whole way, it's yours to carry water in.

---

That winter, they spent in Liangzhou.

In the first month, Jingfu saw something.

It was New Year's Day. In the morning he went out to the well to draw water, and saw that the gate of a Tang household in the ward was open, and in the courtyard stood the whole family, old and young.

They were not wearing Tibetan robes.

They were wearing Tang clothes. The men in soft caps and round-collared robes, the robes lapped to the right; the women with their hair up in coils, in jackets and long skirts. The clothes were all old, their colours faded, moth-eaten in places, patched. You could tell they had been pressed at the bottom of a chest for many years, and taken out once a year.

They knelt in a row before the ancestral tablets in the hall, and knocked their heads to the ground.

When they had done, the whole family wept.

They did not weep loudly, but the whole family was weeping. The old wept, the young wept too, and the smallest child, not knowing why, seeing the grown-ups weep, wept along.

When they had wept, they stood up, took the clothes off piece by piece, folded them, and put them back in the chest. And changed into Tibetan robes again.

Jingfu stood outside the gate and watched it all.

The old man of the house came out to shut the gate and saw him.

The old man looked at his clothes. Jingfu wore a Tang cloth robe, the one he had come out of Chang'an in, worn pale by the road. The old man stared at his robe, stared a long time, and then at his face.

"Where are you from?" the old man asked.

"Chang'an," said Jingfu.

The old man was taken aback. He pushed the gate open and let Jingfu in.

The whole family in the courtyard looked at him. The old man had someone bring a stool and sat him down, and had someone bring a bowl of hot soup. The soup was mutton soup, with pepper in it, and burned the mouth.

The old man sat across from him and asked about Chang'an. Asked whether the city walls still stood, whether the West Market still opened, whether the water of the Qujiang still ran clear, what colour the roofs of the Daming Palace were. Jingfu answered each in turn; some he knew, some he did not. What he did not know, he said he did not know.

At last, the old man stopped.

He laid his two hands on his knees, looked at Jingfu, and asked one thing.

"The Son of Heaven," he said, "does he still remember Hexi?"

The courtyard was very quiet.

Jingfu thought a while.

"I don't know," he said.

The old man looked at him.

"The Son of Heaven sent us out," said Jingfu.

The old man was a long time silent, and then he laughed, laughed a few times, laughed till he coughed. Coughing, he wiped his eyes, wiped them once, and again.

"Then," he said, "you and we are the same now."

Jingfu finished the bowl of soup.

---

In the second month, the thaw came, and they took the road again.

The people of the Zoroastrian shrine stayed in Liangzhou. Of the *hu* households one stayed too; the man had found work in the city feeding camels for caravans. The rest went on with a Sogdian caravan going west.

Past Liangzhou, past Ganzhou, past Suzhou, the road grew more desolate the further it went.

On either side of the Hexi corridor: on one side the Qilian Mountains, snow on their tops; on the other the gobi, and on the gobi nothing, only stones and wind. Jingfu had read these lines. His master had walked this road at ten, and these were the lines he wrote. Jingfu walked it once himself, and thought his master had written it right, and that there was nothing to add.

Outside Ganzhou many tents were pitched. They were the Uighurs'.

The men of the caravan said that after the royal camp in the north was scattered, one branch of the Uighurs had come south-west, come to the country round Ganzhou, asked the Tibetans for pasture, asked the Tang for grain; no one gave, so they took for themselves. The tents were pitched outside the city, patch after patch, smoke rising from the tents, lean horses tethered outside, the horses gnawing grass roots from the ground.

A few Uighur children ran to the roadside to watch the caravan go by, their hands held out.

The men of the caravan paid them no heed. Elijah took half a flatbread from his bundle, broke it in three, and gave it to three of the children.

The children took the bread and ran. A dozen paces off, one of them turned his head and looked at Elijah.

Elijah made the sign of the cross to him.

The child was taken aback, and then he too put out his hand and drew something on his chest. He drew it wrong; the crossbar and the upright were both askew.

Elijah looked at him, and stopped.

The caravan went on, and Salang turned and called him. Only then did Elijah follow. He walked a long way, still looking back.

---

In the fourth month, they reached Shazhou.

Shazhou was Dunhuang. The city stood in an oasis, and beyond the oasis were sand hills, yellow, ridge upon ridge, combed by the wind into lines like ripples on water. South of the city was a mountain whose cliff was cut all over with caves, one against another, like a honeycomb. In the caves Buddhas were enshrined, pictures painted, and the incense smoke was thick.

Shazhou had been under the Tibetans for eighty years too. But Shazhou was not like Liangzhou. Shazhou had many temples, many monks. The Tibetan *tsenpo* believed in the Buddha; in Shazhou no one pulled down the temples, no one sealed them, no one ordered the monks back to lay life.

Jingfu stood at the city gate, looking at temple after temple in the city, at the pagodas on the temple roofs, at the monks coming and going in the streets, looked a long time.

Kang Zhen beside him looked too.

"Chang'an is pulling temples down," said Kang Zhen.

"Mm."

"Here they're building them," said Kang Zhen.

Jingfu said nothing.

The Tibetan official in charge of the city—the Tang people called him the *jie'er*—wished to see passing caravans and question travellers. When he came to Jingfu, he asked what he was, where he came from, where he was going.

Jingfu said: of the Daqin monastery, come from Chang'an.

The *jie'er* asked: going where?

Jingfu said: my place of origin.

The *jie'er* asked: where is your place of origin?

Jingfu said: Xizhou.

The *jie'er* looked at his face, then at the travel pass the county had given him, on which were written the words "sent back to place of origin," and after them the two characters for Xizhou. The *jie'er* gave the pass back to him and waved him on.

Out of the door, Salang asked: "Master, are you a Xizhou man?"

"In the register I am," said Jingfu.

---

On the third day in Shazhou, Joseph fell ill.

He had not been well since Liangzhou: a cough, sweats at night, eating very little. He did not say so, but Elijah knew, and Jingfu knew too. In Shazhou he lay down, and could not get up.

They were lodging at a temple on the east side of the city.

The temple was not large. Its abbot was a Tang monk, past sixty, thin, his eyebrows very long, long enough to hang below the corners of his eyes. He was a Shazhou man, had left home for the monastic life in this temple, and been its abbot for thirty years. The caravan men knew him well; every time they came to Shazhou they stayed in the empty courtyard behind his temple.

The abbot came once to see Joseph. He stood at the door, looking at Joseph, at the cloth-wrapped book in Joseph's arms, at Elijah kneeling by the pallet saying prayers. He could not understand what was being said.

He asked Jingfu: "What teaching are you?"

Jingfu said: "The Luminous Teaching."

The abbot thought and said: "I've heard of it. There's a stele in Chang'an."

"Buried," said Jingfu.

The abbot asked no more. He went back and had a little novice bring a pot of gruel, with jujubes in it.

---

Joseph lay in that temple seven days.

On the seventh night, he called Elijah to him.

He could no longer say much. He took out the book from his coat—one layer of cloth, two layers, three—peeled them off one by one, and uncovered the book. Its cover was of sheepskin, gone black, its corners worn round. He stroked the cover with his hand, then opened the first page and looked at it a while.

Then he closed the book and gave it to Elijah.

He said one sentence in Syriac.

Elijah nodded.

Jingfu was listening beside them. What Joseph had said was: "Give it back to the Patriarchate."

Elijah took the book and held it.

Joseph looked at Jingfu once more. He opened his mouth and said one sentence in Tang speech. His Tang speech was poor; he said it very slowly, squeezing it out word by word, as if digging it up from somewhere very deep.

"He called it right," said Joseph.

It took Jingfu a moment to understand that he meant the clerk.

When Joseph had said it, he shut his eyes.

He went near dawn. Elijah held his hand the whole time.

---

Joseph was buried in the sandy ground east of the city.

The abbot had people help dig the pit. When the pit was dug, Elijah said the prayers, and Jingfu said them too. Salang laid Joseph down. They filled in the earth; there was no stone to set up, so Salang went behind the temple and found two sticks, one long, one short, bound them with cord into a cross, and planted it on the grave.

The abbot stood beside them and watched. He put his palms together and said the Buddha's name once.

Back at the temple, Jingfu went to find the abbot.

In his hands he had two scrolls.

Those two scrolls Lü Shao had pushed into his bundle before that day outside the Kaiyuan Gate. Lü Shao had not said. Only at Wugong, opening his bundle to get out a bowl, had Jingfu seen them. Wrapped in blue cloth, on each a paper label pasted, in Lü Shao's hand, every stroke level and upright, with a flavour of stone to it.

One scroll was the hymn. *The highest heavens all deeply revere and praise; the great earth longs again for peace and harmony.*

One scroll was a list of scriptures: the scriptures his master had translated, their names one after another, copied out together. At the end were a few more lines, saying who had translated these scriptures, and where.

Jingfu laid the two scrolls before the abbot.

"These," he said. "Could they be kept here, with you?"

The abbot looked at the two scrolls and did not at once take them.

"You won't carry them yourselves?"

"The road is long," said Jingfu. "If we carry them, who knows where they'll be lost. Kept here, they're safe."

The abbot picked up the scroll of the hymn, untied the blue cloth, unrolled it, and read a few lines. He read them aloud, very slowly.

"*The highest heavens all deeply revere and praise; the great earth longs again for peace and harmony...*"

He read a few lines, stopped, and looked up at Jingfu.

"It's like ours," he said.

Jingfu said nothing.

The abbot read a few more lines, rolled the scroll up, wrapped it in its blue cloth, and tied it. He stood up and led Jingfu toward the back of the temple.

Behind the temple was a room, not large, without a window. The abbot pushed open the door; inside it was dark, with a smell of paper and dust. Along the walls were rack after rack, and on the racks scriptures were heaped—some rolled, some stacked, some wrapped in cloth, some loose—heaped from the floor to the roof.

"These," said the abbot, "some are our temple's, some other temples sent, some caravans left in keeping, some families sent when someone died. In Tibetan script, in Tang characters, in *hu* letters, all sorts. Torn ones, old ones, ones no longer used—they all go in here."

He went to a corner at the very back, moved a stack of scriptures aside, made a space, put in the two blue-wrapped scrolls, and moved the stack back on top of them.

"Kept here," said the abbot, "no one burns them."

Jingfu stood at the door, looking at that corner.

It was already getting dark, and the room could no longer be seen clearly. Only rack after rack, stack after stack of scriptures, dark, heaped all the way to the roof.

---

That evening the abbot came and sat in the back courtyard a while.

He told Jingfu some things about Shazhou. That the Tibetans had ruled Shazhou eighty years; at first they killed people, later they stopped killing, and wanted grain, wanted cloth, wanted men for soldiers. That the Tang people in the city grew fewer year by year, and those who could speak Tang grew fewer year by year. That the little novices in his temple could read Tang characters, but spoke Tang poorly.

At last, he lowered his voice.

"The Zhang family in the city," he said, "are practising mounted archery at night."

Jingfu looked at him.

"I never said this," said the abbot.

"You never said it," said Jingfu.

The abbot nodded, stood, and went back to the front courtyard.

Elijah sat on the steps holding the sheepskin book, and had not spoken all this while. Salang sat beside him, on his knees the jar the old priest had given him, full of water.

After a long while, Elijah said one sentence in Syriac.

Salang could not understand. He looked at Jingfu.

Jingfu said: "He says Joseph was in Chang'an thirty-four years, and never learned Tang speech whole."

Salang thought and said: "He said that one sentence right."

Elijah did not understand Tang speech. Yet he seemed to have understood. He nodded, and held the book a little tighter.

Salang stood up, took the jar of water over, and held it out to Elijah. Elijah took it, drank a mouthful, and handed it back. Salang drank a mouthful too.

Then he tied the mouth of the jar over with its cloth.
