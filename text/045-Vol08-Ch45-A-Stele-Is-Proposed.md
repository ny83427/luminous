# Volume Eight　The Daqin Monastery

## Chapter 45　A Stele Is Proposed

> We wish to carve a great stele, to make his noble deeds known.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

In the fifth month of the fourteenth year of Dali, Emperor Daizong died.

The crown prince came to the throne—he who was later Dezong. The new Son of Heaven was young, thirty-seven, and in the first months of his reign issued edict after edict. He dismissed the Pear Garden musicians, released palace women, forbade the provinces to present auspicious omens, and even had the white hawks and white sparrows sent as tribute from the provinces let go back into the hills. The people of Chang'an said: there's to be a great change.

Guo Ziyi was changed too this time.

The Son of Heaven honoured him as Venerable Father, added the title of Grand Commandant, and made him concurrently President of the Secretariat; his titles were a size larger than before, but the troops in his hands were all handed over. He was no longer Deputy Marshal, no longer commissioner of Shuofang and Hezhong. That year Lord Guo was eighty-three, and from then on he did not leave the city again, but ate his breakfast every day in his mansion in the Qinren Ward: a bowl of millet gruel, a dish of pickles, a steamed bun.

Yisi's title of *Associate Deputy Military Governor of Shuofang* thereupon became merely a name.

The Shuofang troops were no longer under Lord Guo, and naturally not under this deputy of his. He drew his stipend as before, held his titles as before, but no one from the camps came to call him to watch the roads any more. He was fifty-four that year, his hair half white, still cut very short, close to the scalp. The old wound in his right shoulder ached in wet weather, and when it ached he could not lift his arm above his head. He said nothing of it; only in the mornings when he put on his robe, he put the left sleeve on first, and then slowly worked his right arm in.

---

In the seventh month, an edict was posted at the ward gate of the West Market.

The ward headman stood on a wooden block and read it at the top of his voice. A ring of people stood round below, more *hu* than Tang. Yisi had gone to the West Market that day to buy paper and happened along, and stood behind the crowd listening.

The edict was not long. The headman read it twice, the second time more slowly, as if afraid someone would not understand:

"The Uighurs and the various *hu* in the capital shall each wear their own dress, and shall not imitate the Chinese."

When he had read it, the headman jumped down from the block, smoothed the edict flat, pressed it against the wall once more, and went.

The crowd did not disperse. Someone asked what it meant. Someone said: it means *hu* wear *hu* clothes and aren't allowed to wear Tang clothes. Someone else asked: then this robe of mine, is it *hu* or Tang? No one could answer.

Beside Yisi stood an old Sogdian spice seller whom Yisi knew; every year before Easter the monastery bought its frankincense from his shop. The old man tugged Yisi's sleeve and asked under his breath: "Master Yi, *the various* hu in the edict—does that mean us?"

Yisi said: "It does."

The old man said "Oh," looked down at the round-collared Tang robe he wore, and then at Yisi's grey robe.

"And yours?"

Yisi looked down too. The grey robe had been sewn by a woman of the monastery; the cut was Tang, the cloth was Tang, the fastenings were Tang. He had worn such robes more than thirty years and had never once thought which country it belonged to.

"I don't know," he said.

The old man sighed, and went.

Yisi stood before the edict a while longer. He read Tang characters slowly, and read down it character by character, and at the two characters *the Chinese* he stopped. He thought of a man. That man had said to him more than forty years before, in Balkh: when you get to the East, they won't ask you what you believe; first they'll ask you where you come from.

That man was his father.

---

Half a month later, a man came from Chang'an county.

It was a young assistant magistrate, a little past twenty, newly appointed to the post, with a clerk and a runner. He said that with a new Son of Heaven on the throne, every ward and every monastery was to have its register drawn up afresh: how many monks, what property, when founded—all to be stated plainly.

The elder Azarel came out to receive him. The elder was past sixty that year too, very tall, a little bent, and spoke Tang even more slowly than Yisi. He showed the assistant magistrate into the front hall and had tea poured; the assistant magistrate did not drink, opened a register, took up his brush and began asking.

"What is this monastery called?"

"The Daqin monastery."

The assistant magistrate wrote it. He asked again: "When was it founded?"

"The twelfth year of Zhenguan."

The assistant magistrate's brush stopped. He raised his head and looked at Azarel, and then at the pillars of the hall. The pillars were new timber, put in only last year, the lacquer still bright.

"Zhenguan?" he said. "That's a hundred and forty years ago."

"Yes."

"Is there proof?"

Azarel turned and said something to the man beside him. After a while the elder Babai came out from the back carrying a wooden box. Babai had kept the scriptures more than twenty years, and the ink under his fingernails had never been washed clean. He set the box on the table, opened it, and took things out roll by roll.

What came out were parchment rolls, birch-bark rolls, stacks of yellow hemp paper. The writing on them curled and twisted, written right to left, and the assistant magistrate knew not one letter of it.

"What's this?"

"Documents of the Patriarchate," said Babai. "The Patriarchs' instructions, the letters of past bishops, the monastery's accounts."

"Any in Tang characters?"

Babai searched a long time at the bottom of the box and found a sheet of paper. It was very old, yellowed and brittle, its edge eaten round by insects like a flatbread gnawed by rats. On it were Tang characters, a copy of an edict, copied very hastily, the latter half no longer legible.

The assistant magistrate took it, held it up at the window a long while, and made out only the first few characters. He gave it back to Babai and said: "This is a copy. Where's the original?"

Babai looked at Azarel. Azarel looked at Yisi. Yisi stood by the door, and had said nothing all along.

"The original was lost in the troubles," said Yisi. "The fifteenth year of Tianbao, the year Chang'an fell."

The assistant magistrate nodded. He did not make difficulties; he took up his brush, wrote a few characters in the register, blew them dry, closed the register, rose and went. At the door of the hall he looked back once at the cross on the roof of the hall, as if to ask something, and did not ask.

When he had gone, Babai quietly took note of that page of the register. He had asked the clerk, and the clerk, an honest man, read out to him what was written there.

What the register said was:

"Persian monastery, in the Yining Ward, monks: some; date: unknown."

---

That night, Yisi sat alone at the monastery gate.

A small lamp hung at the monastery gate, a bronze saucer no bigger than half a palm, hanging from an iron hook by the door. In the winter of the second year of Zhide, after Chang'an was recovered, he had turned this lamp up in a side room, green with verdigris. For the first few years it had stood on the step; later a hook was driven in, and it was hung up. Every day lit at dark, blown out at daybreak, twenty-two years, never once missed.

The ward was already quiet. Far off was the watch drum, *dong*, *dong*, *dong*. Now and then a dog ran through the lane, ran into the lamplight, paused, looked at him, and ran off.

He thought of those words. *Date: unknown.*

He thought of the year he first came to Chang'an, when the old priest of the monastery took him to every corner of the monastery and told him: this pillar was put up in such a year, that stone basin was brought from the west by such a bishop, that jujube tree in the back courtyard was planted by such a man. The locust tree at the gate was older than the monastery, the old priest said; when Alopen came, this was empty ground, grown over with weeds, with only this locust tree and a well under it. The old priest was past seventy then; he said he had heard it from his teacher, and his teacher had heard it from a disciple of Alopen's.

The old priest died the next year.

Then came the troubles of Tianbao. Chang'an fell for more than a year. The people of the monastery died, fled, scattered. When he fought his way back from Fengxiang, the monastery gate was open, the door leaves taken off and burned for firewood, the scriptures in the hall scattered across the floor and half rotted by rain. Of the things the old priest had pointed out to him, the pillars had burned, the jujube had been half cut down; only the locust at the gate and the well remained.

Now, of the people in the monastery, he alone was left who knew where those pillars had once stood.

He thought: in a few more years, I'll be gone too.

---

The next day after the morning office, Yisi asked the three elders, Azarel, Marya and Samaan, to the small room in the back courtyard. Babai and Sabrisho came too. Jingjing sat by the door with brush and paper.

Yisi always spoke briefly. He told them about the assistant magistrate the day before, and read out those few characters in the register. Then he said:

"I want to raise a stele."

No one in the room made a sound.

"Raise it in the monastery," said Yisi. "Carve on it when Alopen came, who met him, which emperor issued the edict, what we've done these hundred years and more. Carve it in Tang characters."

The elder Marya spoke first. He was the Elder of Teaching, preached more than twenty times a month at the monastery, and his voice was always hoarse.

"Good," he said. "It should have been done long ago. In sixteen years in Chang'an I've seen I don't know how many steles. The Buddhist temples have them, the Daoist shrines have them; even that fire temple in the Chonghua Ward has one at its gate. The Tang trust stone. Tell a Tang man a hundred times and he won't believe it; let him see it once in stone and he will. We have no stele, and in Tang eyes it's as if we never came."

The elder Samaan did not speak at once. He was Warden of the Rule, in charge of church law, very thin, thin-lipped, and before he spoke he always first tucked his two hands into his sleeves. When his hands were tucked, he said:

"Let me ask one thing. This stele—who is it raised for to see?"

"For the Tang to see."

"And when the Tang see it, what do they see?"

"They see us."

"There you are," said Samaan. "To raise a stele is to want men to see us. It is recorded in the scripture: the men in the land of Shinar built a tower whose top might reach unto heaven, to make them a name. The Lord came down and confounded their language. Our names are written in heaven; they need not be written in stone."

Marya said: "What's written on the stele is the Lord's name, not ours."

"Will Yisi's name be written on the stele?"

Marya said nothing more.

The room was silent again. The elder Sabrisho had kept his head down all along, his beard spread on his chest like a piece of felt. Now he raised his head and said: "Won't do."

Everyone looked at him.

"Won't do," said Sabrisho. "Not that the stele won't do. That discussing it like this won't do. To raise a stele, the stele must name the Patriarch, name the Patriarchate, name where this branch of ours comes from. It's a matter for the whole church. It's not for Chang'an to say. We must ask the Patriarchate."

Azarel nodded.

Babai asked something unrelated: "How many characters will be written?"

Yisi thought and said: "Until they recognise us."

---

They deliberated all that morning and came to no conclusion.

Samaan had one more point: money. To raise a stele needed stone, needed masons, needed someone to write the characters, needed someone to carve them. Every month the monastery gave out gruel, gave out medicine, coffined the unclaimed dead, and all of it cost money. Last winter had been cold; dozens had frozen to death in the south of the city, and the monastery had coffined twenty-seven of them, and to this day half the money for the coffins was owed.

"The price of one stone," said Samaan, "would pay for a winter's gruel."

Yisi said: "I'll pay for it."

Again they all looked at him.

"Those titles of mine," said Yisi. "Grand Master of the Golden Seal and Purple Ribbon, Acting Director of the Palace Administration, Associate Deputy Military Governor of Shuofang. I've drawn the stipends all these years and had no use for them. If they buy a stone, they'll have been of some use at last."

Samaan looked at him a while, and said no more about money.

Azarel spoke last. He said Sabrisho was right; this must be asked of the Patriarchate. Yisi would write a letter setting out the state of things in Chang'an, the purpose of the stele, what would be written on it, all of it plainly. The letter would go to Baghdad for the Patriarch's decision. If the Patriarch allowed it, they would raise it; if not, not.

"Meanwhile," said Azarel, "no one is to touch a stone."

Yisi said: "Very well."

When they broke up, they went out one by one. Jingjing was last; he set the stools straight and wiped up the tea spilled on the floor. When he had wiped it, he straightened up and saw Yisi still sitting there.

"Pa."

Yisi looked up at him.

"Who will write the inscription?"

"You will."

Jingjing was taken aback. "What do I write?"

Yisi stood up, first propping his left hand on the table, then slowly straightening his back. It had rained again last night on his right shoulder, and it had ached all night.

"Write what you've seen," he said.

---

Yisi wrote that letter over seven days.

He wrote in Syriac. His Syriac was what his father had taught him as a boy; in thirty years and more in Chang'an, apart from reading scripture, he had seldom put pen to it, and his letters were big and clumsy, only a few to a line. He wrote a line, stopped and thought a while, wrote another. When he wrote something wrong, he scraped the parchment thin with a small knife and wrote it again. Jingjing sat beside him grinding ink, and ground ink for seven days.

The letter told of Chang'an. Told how many of the monastery had died since Tianbao, how many had scattered, how many had come back. Told of the purple robe. Told of the gruel given out fifty to a group. Told of the new Son of Heaven's edict, *each shall wear their own dress, and shall not imitate the Chinese*. Told of that assistant magistrate and those few words.

At the end it told of the stele.

When the letter was finished, the three elders Azarel, Marya and Samaan read it, and each signed his name below. Before signing, Samaan added a line of small writing at the end of the letter. Yisi did not ask what he had added, and he did not say.

At the beginning of the eighth month a Sogdian caravan was going west. Yisi wrapped the letter in three layers of oilcloth, sealed it with wax, and gave it to a trusted member of the Luminous congregation in the caravan. The man put the letter inside his clothes against his body, patted it, and said: "Master Yi, rest easy."

Yisi said: "On the road, don't drink cold water."

The man was taken aback, not understanding what this meant. Jingjing, beside them, turned his face away.

---

The letter was gone a year.

In that year Chang'an changed its era name; the fourteenth year of Dali ended, and it was the first year of Jianzhong. The new Son of Heaven brought in the double tax and abolished the old grain, labour and cloth levies, and the whole city was reckoning how much it owed this year. Lord Guo kept his eighty-fourth birthday in his mansion in the Qinren Ward, and it was said he ate half a bowl of noodle soup at the feast. The locust tree at the monastery flowered, and shed its flowers. Babai took the old records out of the box roll by roll to air them in the sun, and when they were aired put them back roll by roll.

No one at the monastery spoke of the stele again.

But everyone at the monastery knew that every night Jingjing lit a lamp in the scripture room and wrote something until very late. What it was he did not say, and no one asked.

In the ninth month of the first year of Jianzhong, a caravan coming back from the west entered the Jinguang Gate.

The letter was brought back by that same man of the congregation. He was much thinner, his face burned a dark red; he came in at the monastery gate, first knelt on the ground and crossed himself, and only then took an oilcloth packet from inside his clothes.

In the oilcloth packet was an answer. Yellow-brown parchment, folded into a long strip, tied with fine cord, and the knot sealed with a lump of black wax. On the wax was pressed a seal—a cross, with a ring of Syriac letters round it.

Yisi knew this seal. Twenty-four years before, in the side courtyard west of the city of Lingzhou, he had opened a letter sealed with such a seal. That time, the name on the seal had been Jacob.

This time, the name on the seal was Hnanisho.

Azarel opened the letter before everyone. It was not long either, in the hand of the Patriarchate's secretary, with at the end a name and one word in the Patriarch's own hand. That word, everyone in the room knew.

*Granted.*

The elder Marya wept on the spot. When he wept his voice was hoarser still, like a door that had not been oiled. Samaan did not weep; he took the letter and read it through from beginning to end, and looked a long time at that word at the end, then gave it back to Azarel and said: "Then let's raise it."

That evening the monastery had an extra dish: mutton stewed with turnip.

---

Here one more thing must be said.

That letter had left Baghdad in the spring of the first year of Jianzhong. Two months after the letter went out of the city gate and east, the Patriarch Hnanisho died. By the time he died, the Patriarchate was already in an uproar over who should succeed. From Baghdad to Chang'an caravans took the better part of a year, and such news travelled more slowly than letters.

So on this night in the ninth month of the first year of Jianzhong, in the Daqin monastery in the Yining Ward of Chang'an, twenty-odd people gathered round a pot of mutton and turnip and prayed for the Patriarch Hnanisho, asking the Lord to grant him long life, to grant him peace, to grant that he govern the church well in Baghdad.

They prayed very earnestly.

When Yisi had eaten, he went back to his room and laid the answer on the table. He looked a while at the seal on the wax, looked a while at that word *granted*, then took a bowl from the cupboard and set it on the letter.
