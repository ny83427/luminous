# Volume Eight　The Daqin Monastery

## Chapter 45　A Stele Is Proposed

> We wish to carve a great stele, to make his noble deeds known.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

In the first month of the first year of Jianzhong, the court put the Two-Tax Law into effect. The old grain, labour and cloth levies were abolished; households were no longer divided into native and guest, but registered where they now lived; persons were no longer divided by age into able-bodied and minor, but graded by wealth and poverty; money was collected twice a year, in summer and autumn. All over the city of Chang'an people were reckoning how much they owed this year, and every ward and every monastery had to draw up its register anew: how many persons, what property, when founded—all to be written plainly.

The one Chang'an county sent was a young registrar, a little over twenty, newly filling the post, with a clerk. Elder Azarel was past seventy and badly stooped; he came out to see him once and then had Jingjing deal with him. Jingjing showed the registrar into the front hall; the registrar would not drink tea, but spread open his book and began to ask: the monastery's name, the number of persons, the fields and property; Jingjing answered each. When it came to when it was founded, Jingjing said the twelfth year of Zhenguan. The registrar's brush stopped; he raised his head and looked at the pillars of the hall—the pillars had been set up in the first year of Shangyuan, and their lacquer had faded—and said the twelfth year of Zhenguan, that was more than a hundred and forty years ago; was there proof?

Babai came out carrying that wooden box and took out roll after roll: parchment, birch bark, hemp paper, the writing right to left, curling and curving; the registrar could read not one of them. Babai rummaged a long while at the bottom of the box and turned up one in Tang characters, a copy of that edict of the twelfth year of Zhenguan, yellow and brittle, eaten round the edges by worms, the latter half no longer legible. The registrar asked where the original was; Jingjing said the original had been lost in the fifteenth year of Tianbao, the year Chang'an fell. He took the registrar round behind the hall to see that old stone too; the stone leaned against the foot of the wall, and the characters for "twelfth year of Zhenguan" Jingjing had once picked clean bit by bit with his fingernail, and they could still be read. The registrar squatted and read out a few characters—twelfth year of Zhenguan, the Persian monk Alopen—stood up, brushed the dirt off his knees, and said this was carved by the monastery itself, not an official document, and the copy had no seal; the book could not be written on the strength of these. He went back to the front hall, wrote a few lines in the book, blew them dry, shut it, and left; at the door of the hall he looked back once at the cross on the roof of the hall.

What was written in the book the clerk read out to Babai. Babai told Jingjing afterwards that what it said was: Persian monastery, in the Yining Ward; year of founding unknown.

In the fourth year of Tianbao the Son of Heaven had issued an edict changing the Persian monasteries of the two capitals to Daqin monasteries; it had been changed thirty-five years; and in the county's book it was still the Persian monastery.

The next day Jingjing went to the Bureau of Sacrifices. Director Liu had retired to his home district some years before; the Bureau had a new director, surnamed Wei, who did not see people much; the one who saw Jingjing was an old archivist who had copied records at the Bureau thirty years, his back as bent as Elder Azarel's. Jingjing told him he wanted to look up that edict of the fourth year of Tianbao; the old archivist led him into the record room at the back; in the record room were rows of wooden shelves, and on the shelves rolls were heaped, and on the rolls dust; half had been carried back from elsewhere after the upheaval, and half recopied after the upheaval. The old archivist searched most of the morning and found it: a recopied roll of edicts, ninth month of the fourth year of Tianbao: the Persian monasteries of the two capitals shall be changed to Daqin monasteries; those established in the prefectures and commanderies of the realm likewise. Jingjing asked too for the one of the twelfth year of Zhenguan. The old archivist shook his head and said the Zhenguan records were incomplete even before the upheaval, and burned once more in it; if you want something from Zhenguan, you can only look for the stone that was carved at the time; the record room hasn't got it. Jingjing asked where the carved stone would be. The old archivist said: that you'll have to ask yourselves; if your monastery has one, it's in your monastery.

The Bureau of Sacrifices sent a dispatch to Chang'an county, and the county's book was corrected, corrected by that registrar's own hand; when it was corrected the registrar showed Jingjing the book, and on it was written: Daqin monastery, in the Yining Ward, renamed in the fourth year of Tianbao; year of founding unknown. Jingjing looked at those last four words and thanked the registrar. The registrar saw him out, stood a moment at the gate of the county office, and said he wasn't making difficulties for you; what's written in the book needs proof; a stone privately carved—anyone could carve one; if you had a stele the government permitted to be set up, that would be different.

---

That evening, Jingjing went to the gate to light the lamp.

Since Yonan had gone, the lamp was his to light: every evening adding oil, changing the wick, striking the slow-match, and when it was lit standing a moment under the lamp. He remembered his Pa saying that in the year his Pa first came to Chang'an, the monastery's old priest had taken him all round the monastery and told him which year this pillar was set up, which bishop had that stone basin carried from the west, that this locust tree at the gate was older than the monastery: when Alopen came, here was a stretch of wild grass, only this locust tree and a well beneath it. The old priest had heard it from his teacher, and his teacher had heard it from a disciple of Alopen's. The old priest died the next year. Later Chang'an fell, the pillars burned, the scrolls were soaked to ruin, and now of those in the monastery who knew where those pillars had first stood, only his Pa was left; his Pa was fifty-five.

He remembered too that letter of the spring before last, the paragraph the Patriarch had added at its end: if the people of Chang'an wished to carve a stone to record events, it was permitted; if they wished to write the Patriarchate's name on the stone, to write the name of the reigning patriarch, that too was permitted. When that paragraph had been read, Elder Samaan entered a line in his account book, and no one ever raised it again.

The ward drum finished, and he was still standing under the lamp.

---

The next day after the morning office, Jingjing asked several elders into that small room in the back courtyard. Azarel, Marya and Samaan sat at the head; Babai, Sabrisho and Ishaq on either side; Yisi sat by the door. Jingjing told of the registrar, of the words in the book, and then said he wanted to set up a stele, set it up in the monastery, and carve on the stone when Alopen came, who received him, which emperor issued the edict, and what the Church had done here these hundred-odd years—carve it in Tang characters.

Marya was the first to say good. His voice was hoarse, speaking like a door without oil; he said he had preached seventeen years in Chang'an and seen how many steles—the Buddhist temples have them, the Daoist abbeys have them; the Tang believe stones; tell them a hundred times and they don't believe it; let them see it once on a stone and they believe it; without a stele, in Tang eyes we might as well never have come.

Samaan folded his two hands into his sleeves before he spoke. He asked whom the stele was set up for. Jingjing said: for the Tang to see. Samaan asked: and when the Tang see it, what do they see? Jingjing said: they see us. Samaan said: there you are; the scripture records that the people of the land of Shinar built a tower whose top reached unto heaven, to make them a name, and the Lord came down and confounded their language; our names are written in heaven; they need not be written on stone. Marya said what was written on the stele was the Lord's name. Samaan asked whether the stele would write Yisi's name. Marya could not answer, and looked at Jingjing.

Jingjing said it would. He said the people of Shinar built the tower because they feared to be scattered over the earth, and wished to make themselves a name; the people of Chang'an have no need to fear being scattered; the people of Chang'an have been scattered already: that time in the fifteenth year of Tianbao, scattered till the whole monastery had three men left, and of the three, two died and one ran. He said what he wanted to carve was those who came back after they were scattered: Alopen came, they scattered, others came, they scattered, others came; when the stele writes of his Pa, it writes what his Pa did, not his Pa's name; his Pa's name the Tang can't say; what they remember is the *hu* monk who gave out rice by the Qujiang Pool. He said the county registrar said a privately carved stone doesn't count; it needs a stele the government permits to be set up; he wanted to set up a stele the government permitted.

Samaan looked at him a while and said: you speak fine words, but the characters carved on stone live longer than the words you speak; of all you've said, the stone can't hold one sentence. Jingjing said: then carve what it can hold. Haddad beside them said that when he was young, by the sea in the south, he had seen the merchant ships of the Tazi with words carved on all their prows: the name of the ship's owner and the name of God; when a ship sank and its timber washed ashore, people who picked it up and looked could still tell whose ship it was. Gabriel said: and if the stele falls? Jingjing said: if it falls, it's buried in the earth, and waits for those who come after to dig it up; this year, sweeping, he swept out that stone of the twelfth year of Zhenguan; those who come after can sweep out his too.

Sabrisho, stroking his beard, said: the stele will write the Patriarch's name, write the Patriarchate's name, write where this branch came from; that is a matter for the whole Church; Chang'an's word does not settle it; the Patriarchate must be asked.

The room was quiet a while. Samaan took his hands out of his sleeves, opened his leather-bound account book, turned to the page of the spring of the thirteenth year of Dali, and read a line he had written himself: The Patriarch permits Chang'an to carve a stone to record events; the reigning patriarch's name may be written on the stone. When he had read it he shut the book and said the Patriarchate had already permitted it; he had it recorded; he did not approve, but what had been permitted he could not say had not been permitted.

Ishaq beside them made a sound, like a laugh, or like a wheeze. Babai asked something beside the point: how many characters would be written? Yisi, by the door, said: write till they recognize us.

Samaan had one more point: money. Stone, stonemasons, the one to write, the one to carve—all needed money; every month the monastery gave out gruel and medicine; Narsai, burying those dead no one gathered, bought reed mats and wooden stakes, which needed money too; last winter was cold, and south of the city dozens froze to death; Narsai gathered twenty-seven. He said the money for one stone would be a winter's gruel.

Yisi said he would pay. He said those few titles of his—Grand Master of Splendid Happiness with Gold and Purple, acting Director of the Palace Administration, Assistant Vice-Military Governor of Shuofang—Lord Guo had handed over his troops last year, and the vice-governorship was just a name, but the stipend was still drawn; all these years it had been of no use; to buy a stone with it would at least be some use. Samaan raised the money no more.

Azarel said last: set it up. He said too that on the stele the names of the monastery's people should be carved—not only Chang'an's, but Luoyang's, Lingzhou's, Zhouzhi's, every one that counted—and told Jingjing to write to have each place send its names.

That afternoon Jingjing wrote three letters: one to Luo Fifteen in the Xiushan Ward of Luoyang, one to the Kang nephew at the Lingzhou monastery, one to those seven households in the gully at Zhouzhi. The letters said Chang'an was to set up a stele, with names carved on its sides; those in each place who wished their names carved should send them, Syriac names and Tang names both. The reply from Luoyang came at the end of the second month: Luo Fifteen had written a whole sheet, twelve households, seventy-odd souls, and added a line at the end asking that his grandfather Old Jacob's name be carved too. The reply from Lingzhou came in the third month; the Kang nephew's Syriac letters were harder to read than his Tang characters; he sent eleven households, with Old Master Jiao's name written first; Old Master Jiao had died the winter before last. Of those seven households at Zhouzhi no one could write; word was sent by an old man coming to the city to sell firewood, brought to the monastery gate; the old man could not remember it all, only that all seven households wanted to be carved; the names he had recited all the way, recited them to Chang'an, and dropped two.

On the evening the letters were written, Jingjing waited at the side gate for Narsai. Narsai came back pushing his cart; the cart was empty; that day he had gathered no one. Jingjing told him of the stele, said the names of the monastery's people would be carved on its sides. Narsai set down the cart and asked whether the dead would be carved. Jingjing said they would; the monastery's people, living and dead, whoever was sent in would be carved. Narsai asked again: and that row at the edge outside the Kaiyuan Gate? That row weren't baptized, and the names weren't complete either; some had only the character for *old man*. Jingjing said that row could not be carved; that row were not the monastery's people. Narsai nodded and said he knew; he only asked. He pushed his cart toward the inside, went a few steps, turned his head, and said: those stakes in that row, every spring he had to carve them over again; the winter rain and snow soaked the characters shallow; the characters on stone probably wouldn't need carving over. Jingjing stood at the side gate and watched him push the cart in; the wheel bumped once on the threshold.

That day at the hour of *wu* the monastery prayed for the Patriarch as usual, begging the Lord to grant Patriarch Hnanisho long life, to grant him peace, to grant that he govern the Church well in Baghdad. Marya led the prayer, longer than usual, and when he came to the name Hnanisho said it with especial weight.

When they dispersed, Jingjing lingered last, setting the stools straight, and saw his Pa still sitting by the door, first bracing his left hand on the doorframe, then slowly straightening his back; his right shoulder hurt on overcast days. Jingjing asked him: when the stele writes of Pa, how did Pa want him to write it? Yisi said: write little. Jingjing did not agree to that, and asked again: and the rest? Writing alone, writing a hundred and forty-odd years of events, where should he begin? Yisi thought, and said: what Master Cen told you that year in the felt tent—do you remember? Jingjing said he remembered: don't write only what ought to be; write what you see. Yisi said: then write what you see; what you can't see, ask Babai; it's all in that box of Babai's.

---

That spring A-Lian's cough never stopped.

In the first month she still went to open the shop in the West Market, leaving as soon as the ward gate opened and coming back before the gong, and each time she came back her steps were slower. Early in the second month she got up one morning and squatted before the little oven to stick on the cakes, and once she had squatted she could not get up; she coughed a long while, and coughed up a mouthful of blood, spat it into the oven ashes, and covered it with ashes. Shiyiniang went to the north end of the ward that day to bring her the paste, saw that patch in the ashes, said nothing at all, helped her to bed, and came back and told Yisi. Yisi went to see her, felt her pulse, came back and made up medicine, and when it was made up sat before the medicine chest, the little steelyard gripped in his hand, and did not get up. Ishaq went to see her too, and came back and said it was the lungs, hurt in the winter, and spring would not nurse them back.

The shop was shut; at its door hung that small accounts board, and at its very bottom Yonan's line, traced over with the stylus, which would not rub off. The young helper still went once a day, took down the shutters and put them up again, saying he was afraid people would think the shop wasn't open; in fact the shop was not open.

Jingjing went every night to sit a while, sat till the ward drum finished, and then slept on a reed mat Shiyiniang laid down; before light he went back to the monastery to say the morning office; by day he went to the gruel kitchen, to the medicine chest, to the county; at night in the scripture room he went through that box of Babai's till the fourth watch, and then went to the north end of the ward again. Shiyiniang said: going on like this, you won't last many days. He said he would last; on the road back from Ctesiphon he had lasted through longer. One night he went late, and A-Lian was still awake, and asked what he'd been doing so late. He said he was in the scripture room writing the stele. She asked how far he'd got. He said he'd got to Alopen, was writing how Alopen rode his camel to outside the walls of Chang'an and saw the road full of banners, and thought they were welcoming someone else, and had his disciples lead the camels to the side of the road. A-Lian heard it and laughed and said: then he was like your Elder Yonan; when people gave him a cake, he'd first ask, is it for me? She laughed till she coughed, and when she had coughed said: go on writing; when it's written, read it to me.

Shiyiniang moved to the north end of the ward to stay, and Lingbao brought them food from the monastery every evening. Jingjing went every night, sat by the bed, and read scripture to her, read in Tang, the things he had translated these years; when he came to the hymn, "In the highest of the heavens, deep reverence and praise; on the great earth, all again think on peace and concord," she said it after him, could not say it all, and halfway through began to cough. She asked him about the stele, and he told her: the stele would be carved with Tang characters and with Syriac letters too; the Syriac would be carved at the foot and on both sides of the stele, carving the names of the monastery's people. She asked whether his name would be carved. Jingjing asked: which "his"? She looked at him and did not answer. Jingjing said it would; Elder Yonan's name—his Pa would carve it in. She nodded and said: once it's carved, those who see it later will know he was in Chang'an.

One night at the beginning of the third month she coughed half the night, and in the small hours stopped coughing and lay there, breathing very shallowly, her eyes half open. Shiyiniang was heating water on the stove; Jingjing sat by the bed. She put a hand out from under the quilt and felt along the edge of the bed, found his hand, and held it. Her hand was hot, burning. She held it a while and said: your hand's cold; you've been drinking cold water again.

Jingjing said: "No, *A-niang*. No, Ma."

When he had said it he stood stunned himself. Those two characters he had kept pressed under the inkstone three months, had struck through on paper so hard the ink soaked to the back, had said in his heart who knew how many times, and never once spoken aloud. Now they came out of themselves, said quite ordinarily, as if he called her so every day, had called her so for thirty-odd years.

A-Lian did not open her eyes. Her hand tightened once on his, the corner of her mouth moved, and she said: "Then that's good."

That was her last word. Near dawn Shiyiniang came in with the hot water, and Jingjing was still holding that hand; it was no longer burning, and was slowly going cold. Shiyiniang put down the basin and stood at the door and did not come over; after a while she went to the kitchen and banked the fire in the stove.

When it was light Shiyiniang went back to the monastery with the news; she went into the side courtyard; Yisi was putting on his robe, left sleeve first, then slowly working his right arm in. Shiyiniang told him, told him she was gone, and that before she went Jingjing had called her Ma. Yisi's right arm stopped at the mouth of the sleeve, stopped a moment, and went in; he tied the strings and said: as he should. When he had said it, he went to the back courtyard to split wood, split a few strokes, put down the axe, went back into the house, changed into a clean robe, and went off to the north end of the ward.

---

A-Lian was buried outside the Kaiyuan Gate, beside Yonan, a foot of ground between them, as she had arranged with Shiyiniang before she died. Jingjing carved the wooden cross himself, and below the cross carved one Tang character: *lian*, lotus. He did not carve it well; the cart in its middle was carved too big. Babai stood by watching and did not correct it for him.

Narsai came that day. He was pushing that small cart of his, a roll of reed matting and an iron spade on it, coming back from gathering a body south of the city; passing the burial ground, he stopped. He stood at the edge of the burial ground beside his row of stakes and did not come near, and when everyone had gone, went over, squatted on that foot of ground between the two graves, patted the earth with his hand, patted it firm, rose, and went off pushing his cart.

That night Shiyiniang took a cloth bundle from her chest and gave it to Jingjing. In the bundle was that wooden board, the one A-Lian had hung many years in the little room behind the West Market shop, with four letters written on it in charcoal, Alaph, Lamadh, Yudh, Nun, and a *heart* drawn below. Shiyiniang said: she gave me this the night before her wedding; she said if you ever asked about these years, give it to you; if you didn't ask, keep it with me. She said: you never did ask; I'm giving it to you anyway.

Jingjing took the board and looked at the four letters: the tail of the Nun flicked upward; the charcoal had faded, but that upward stroke could still be seen clearly. He took the board back to the west wing and leaned it in the corner, beside the Xizhou board, which was turned round, its writing to the wall.

Seven days later he went to the West Market and closed up the shop. That small accounts board at the door he took down and read from top to bottom, line by line: Zhang San, owes four cakes; Old Wang, owes two soups; some with a Nun drawn after them—those paid—some without—those not. He stood at the shop door, and the people of the West Market who had run up accounts came one by one: some brought money, some a packet of something; the porter who had blocked the carriage with a rolling pin brought a string of cash, saying it was for the three bowls of soup he owed. Jingjing said she had said those three bowls of soup were forgiven. The porter said: her saying they're forgiven is her affair; his paying is his; and put the money on the threshold and left. Jingjing gathered the money together, took it back, and gave it to Samaan; Samaan asked under what heading to enter it; Jingjing said under the gruel; and Samaan entered it under the gruel. That small board he did not wipe; Yonan's line at the very bottom he did not touch either; he took it back to the west wing and leaned it with the other two.

The shop's lease was given up. That little clay oven Shiyiniang carried back to the side courtyard and set up at the kitchen door, and in winter baked cakes for Lingbao on it; Lingbao said the cakes it baked weren't as good as Granny A-Lian's.

---

Note: The Two-Tax Law of the first month of the first year of Jianzhong—"households no longer native and guest, but registered where they now live; persons no longer able-bodied and minor, but graded by wealth and poverty"—follows the *Jiu Tang shu*, "Biography of Yang Yan," and the *Tang huiyao*. The edict of the ninth month of the fourth year of Tianbao renaming the Persian monasteries Daqin monasteries. The edict of the twelfth year of Zhenguan originally said "the Persian monk Alopen." The stele has "the great benefactor, Grand Master of Splendid Happiness with Gold and Purple, Assistant Vice-Military Governor of Shuofang, acting Director of the Palace Administration, granted the purple robe, the monk Yisi." The Chang'an county registrar's drawing up of the register, the old stone, and Hnanisho's permission to carve a stone are the novel's invention.
