# Volume Twelve　Jade Gate

## Chapter 80　Parting Ways

> One Person came into the world, to save without limit; the sun rose, the darkness perished, and all bore witness to the deep truth.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

They reached the town of Xizhou in the seventh month of that year. From Yizhou to Xizhou was another half month and more, but the road was easier than the Moheyan desert—water, grass, villages; the villagers grew grapes and cotton; the houses were of mud brick, flat-roofed, and on the roofs raisins lay drying in patches, purple and yellow, shining in the sun. He Qi said Xizhou was hot—so hot in summer you could bury an egg in the sand and roast it—and in winter cold, cold enough to ice the mouths of the wells; in summer the townspeople lived underground—dug a room under the earth—it was cool. Xiaoman heard and asked: and in winter? He Qi said in winter they lived underground too; it was warm. Xiaoman said: then they live underground all year round. He Qi said: near enough.

The town of Xizhou stood on a plain, its walls of rammed earth, high and thick, its gateway very deep; inside the gate the streets were crowded—Uighurs, Sogdians, Tang, Tokharians, all sorts—and the tongues spoken were even more mixed than at Liangzhou. He Qi said Xizhou was governed by the Uighurs now; after the royal tent fell, part of the band that fled west came here and joined the Uighurs already here; the town's officials were Uighur, the accountants Sogdian, the farmers Tang; everyone lived his own life, and nobody much minded anybody.

---

They went to the north church the next day. The church was in a lane north of the town, a very narrow lane, mud-brick walls on either side with grass growing on top. The church gate was not large; on it was carved a cross, its four ends rounded; the gate's lacquer had peeled; rust grew on the knocker; it was as it had been ninety-odd years before. Kang Zhen led the way, pushed the gate, and it opened with a creak. The courtyard was clean, no weeds; the main hall's door stood open; inside a lamp was burning, and on the east wall hung a wooden cross.

There were four people in the church: three old men, one past seventy and two past sixty, all Sogdians, deacons of the church; and an old woman, wife of one of the old men, who kept the church's stove. Hearing from Kang Zhen that people had come from the Daqin Monastery at Chang'an, they came out of the hall and stood under the colonnade looking at this crowd come in at the gate—at the black robe Jingfu wore, at the blind old man lying in the cart, at Elijah and Salang, at the two little girls. The one past seventy came over to Jingfu and asked in Syriac: from Chang'an? Jingfu answered in Syriac: yes, from the Daqin Monastery in Yining Ward at Chang'an; the monastery was abolished in the fifth year of Huichang; we were sent back, travelled three years, and got this far.

The old man asked again: in the Chang'an monastery—is there a priest? Jingfu said there is, and turned to point to the man in the cart, and said: that is the Great Worthy Lingbao, son of the Great Worthy Yisi, Chang'an's last priest. When the old man heard, he took two steps toward the cart, stopped again, turned round, and looked at Jingfu, his lips trembling, and said: the north church of Xizhou has had no priest for thirty years. He said the last priest died in the Yuanhe years; after he died no one came; the three of them, at every offering once in seven, recited scripture in the hall, and when they came to the offering bread they stopped, empty-handed, and recited so for thirty years.

---

That offering once in seven was held in the hall of the north church of Xizhou. The hall was not large, three bays; the wooden cross on the east wall Kang Zhen said was the very one Jingjing had stood on tiptoe underneath to steady long ago while the elders cleaned it; its wood had gone black and had a crack along it that someone had bound with copper. Twenty-odd came to the hall: the church's three old men, the old woman, Kang Zhen's family, the few Sogdian households of Xizhou who still believed; He Qi came too and stood at the back. Lingbao was helped into the hall, helped to the foot of the east wall, before the table; he could not stand; Jingfu brought a folding chair and he sat; the glass bowl was set on the table before him, and in it the bread He Siniang had baked that morning on the church's stove.

He recited the consecration. He recited very slowly, word by word—the passage he had recited for nearly forty years; the year Elder David laid hands on him he was forty-three, and now he was eighty-one. His hands shook badly; he reached for the bread, and took two tries to take it; Jingfu beside him held the bottom of the bowl with his left hand, and the bowl did not move. He broke the bread and handed it out piece by piece, handed it to the church's three old men; the three knelt and put out their hands to receive it; the one past seventy took the bread and held it and did not eat it, held it and wept, wept aloud; he said he had not received bread in thirty years; he had thought that in this life, till he died, he would never receive it.

When Lingbao had handed it out, there were still a few pieces left in the bowl; he asked Jingfu who had not received; Jingfu said He Qi, at the back. Lingbao said: call him. He Qi came up from the back and knelt; Lingbao put the bread in his palm, felt his hand, and said: He Qi, that time outside the Kaiyuan Gate he gave you a blessing; today he gives you bread.

---

In those days Ayi was asking about her people in the town of Xizhou. She was fourteen that year, spoke Uighur well and Tang well too; in the streets of Xizhou, when she heard Uighurs talking she drew near to listen; after a few days of listening she found an old Uighur woman selling curds by the West Market; the old woman heard her say her Ma was of the Manichaean monastery at Chang'an and died in the third year of Huichang, and her Ma's Ma came from beside the royal tent north of the desert; which tribe, she named a tribe; the old woman heard it and said she knew that tribe; after the royal tent fell, that tribe did not go west but went north, over the Tianshan, onto the northern steppe, following another chief, herding sheep, herding horses—in winter in the valleys south of the mountains, in summer on the pastures north of them; from Xizhou, twenty-odd days north.

Ayi came back and told Jingfu. She said she wanted to go. She said she had never seen her Ma's Ma; her Ma told her the steppe was green, the grass in summer grew to a horse's belly, the snow in winter buried a man to the waist; her Ma said she could never go back in this life, and told her to remember: she came from the steppe. She said she wanted to go and see. That night Jingfu sat alone in the hall.

---

In those days the church's people debated what came next, debated several evenings. Elijah meant to go west. He said he would go to Merv and stand before Joseph's Pa's and Ma's graves for him; Joseph went half the way; the other half, he'd go for him. He said once at Merv he'd go further west, to the Patriarchate, to see the Patriarch and tell him plainly the affairs of Chang'an: the Chang'an monastery is gone, Chang'an's people have been sent out, the stele is buried, Chang'an's last priest is called Lingbao—he'd tell the Patriarch all of it. Salang said he'd go with Elijah; that jar of ash on his chest must be handed over at a place with fire; going west, past the Pamirs, past Balkh, are the Persians' lands, and Persia has fire; when he had handed over the ash he'd go with Elijah to the Patriarchate; his grandfather's father came from the Patriarchate; he'd go and see.

He Qi said he'd stay in Xizhou; his home was here, his caravan was here; he was past fifty, and from now on he'd go back and forth on the road between Xizhou and Shazhou. He said: Sister, you stay too; Xizhou has the He clan, has houses; the Great Worthy will be at the church; the church has people to look after him.

He Siniang looked at Lingbao. Lingbao said he would go west. He said he would go to Balkh. He said his Pa was a man of Balkh; his grandfather Milis was buried behind the church at Balkh and his grandmother beside his grandfather; Brother went once at ten, came back and told him there was a mulberry tree by the graves. He said: all his life the Tang called him *hu* and the *hu* called him Tang; the Office of Receptions' roll says he's of Balkh in Tokharistan, to be sent back to his own place, and his own place is Balkh; he's never been to Balkh, but the roll says Balkh, so he'll go to Balkh.

The church's people all disapproved. The old deacon past seventy said: Great Worthy, from Xizhou to Balkh you go by Karashahr, by Kucha, by Kashgar, and over the Pamirs; the Pamirs are snow all year round; even crossing in the seventh or eighth month people freeze to death; the Great Worthy is eighty-one, his eyes can't see, he can't walk; this road is half a year at the least; the Great Worthy won't make it. He said: let the Great Worthy stay in Xizhou; the north church of Xizhou has had no priest for thirty years; if the Great Worthy stays, at the offering once in seven Xizhou's people will have bread; if the Great Worthy dies in Xizhou, the church's people will see him to his end and bury him behind the church. Elijah said: Great Worthy, he and Salang are going west; they're young and can walk; the Great Worthy's wish they'll carry to Balkh for him, and stand before his grandparents' graves for him. He Qi said: brother-in-law, if you die halfway, what will Sister do alone on the Pamirs? Jingfu sat opposite Lingbao, both hands on his knees.

Lingbao heard them out. He smiled; he said: all you say, he knows; he won't make it, he knows; he's eighty-one, his eyes can't see, he needs someone to hold him up to walk; the snow on the Pamirs he's never seen, but Pa told him, Uncle told him, Brother told him too; Brother said the snow on the Pamirs is blue. He said: to reach Balkh, to look once at the place his Pa was born, to look once at his grandparents' graves—of course that would be good; not to reach it, to die on the road—die at Karashahr, die at Kucha, die on the Pamirs—that's good too; dying on the road, he's still going home; it's the same road wherever he is. He said: all his life he never went outside the Guannei circuit; he came out, walked three years, reached Xizhou, and he'd like to walk a little further.

When he had said it, everyone in the hall looked at him. The old deacon past seventy bowed his head, rubbing both hands on his knees. Elijah bowed his head too. He Qi looked at his sister; He Siniang reached out and took Lingbao's hand and said she'd go with him.

Jingfu, who had sat opposite Lingbao all along, stood up then. He said: then we won't argue any more. He said: let us all pray for Uncle Lingbao. That night in the hall of the north church of Xizhou everyone knelt—the church's three old men, the old woman, Kang Zhen, He Qi, Elijah, Salang, Ayi, Xiaoman, Jingfu—kneeling round Lingbao; Lingbao sat on the folding chair, and He Siniang stood behind him holding his shoulders. Jingfu led the recitation: Psalm 121, *the Lord shall preserve thy going out and thy coming in from this time forth, and even for evermore*—which Lingbao had recited outside the Kaiyuan Gate for He Qi's caravan; Jingfu said a line and everyone followed with a line; when it was done Jingfu said a few words in Tang, asking the Lord to keep Uncle Lingbao safe to reach Balkh, and if he did not reach it, asking the Lord to take him home. When he had said it, Lingbao said *Amen*, and everyone said *Amen*.

When the prayer was done, He Qi found his sister at the well curb behind the church. He Siniang was washing the robe Lingbao had changed out of; the moon shone on the well curb; the water was cold. He Qi squatted and helped her wring the robe, wrung one, and said: Sister, you're fifty-seven. He Siniang said she knew. He Qi said: that time outside the Kaiyuan Gate, he went and you stayed; he missed you, missed you eight years, and when at last they met at Shazhou, you're going again; this time you're going to the Pamirs; the snow on the Pamirs he's crossed; he knows what kind of place it is; if you go, he'll never see you again in this life. He Siniang put down the robe in her hands and said: He Qi, when you left back then, it was you who wanted to go; she couldn't stop you; today she's going, and it's she who wants to go; you can't stop her either. She said her husband died at sea and not even his body came back; she kept faith seven or eight years, and the uncles said she was keeping faith with a dead man; afterwards she married the Great Worthy; the Great Worthy is eighty-one and his eyes can't see; she's keeping faith with a living man; the living man wants to go west, and she goes with him; wherever they get to, that's where it'll be. He Qi squatted there wringing that robe, wringing it till not a drop was left in it, and still wringing; He Siniang reached out and pressed his hands and said: stop wringing; you'll wring the robe to tatters. He Qi said: Sister, tomorrow he'll pick you the two best camels.

---

Jingfu told Lingbao the next day. He said he would go north. He said Ayi meant to go north to find her Ma's people; she was fourteen and couldn't go alone; he'd take her. He said: he's the leader; the Office of Receptions' roll says he's a man of An, to be sent back to his own place, and his own place is An, north of Balkh; he won't go to An; there's no one in An he knows; his Pa was in the Shence Army and died in Huaixi; his Pa's grandfather came from An, how many generations ago he doesn't know. He said on the northern steppe there are Uighurs, there are other tribes; perhaps there are people who believe in Daqin, perhaps not; he'll go and see.

The old deacon past seventy was listening beside them, and said then: among the Uighurs going north there truly are believers in Daqin; when he was young he saw some at Beiting—a few households, crosses hanging in their tents, who'd come to believe through Sogdian merchants; at the offering once in seven they recited in their tents, in Syriac, not understanding a word, just reciting by the sounds, recited so for decades; he asked them what they were reciting, and they said it was the words of Aluohe.

Elijah, beside them, hearing Jingfu say north, spoke first. He said: Leader, come west with us. He said: when you reach the Patriarchate and see the Patriarch, the Patriarch will lay hands on you—make you deacon, then priest—and then you can bless the bread; then come back from the Patriarchate—back to Chang'an, back to Xizhou, back to the steppe—and when you come back, you'll have bread in your hands. He said: that's how Guangzheng went long ago; Guangzheng went and didn't come back; you go, and come back.

Jingfu said he had thought of it. He said from Xizhou to the Patriarchate, if the caravan's journey goes well, it's the better part of a year; there and back, over a year; but a journey going well he hadn't seen once these years; from Chang'an to Xizhou, a few months' road, they took three years—three months waiting at Liangzhou, over a year waiting at Shazhou—war, roads cut, waiting for caravans. He said: and at the Patriarchate the laying on of hands isn't done just like that; you must see the Patriarch, must wait; for deacon you wait, and for priest you wait again; Guangzheng has been gone twenty-five years—his letter arrived; he didn't. He said he's fifty-three; going there, three years, five years without coming back is ordinary; those few households in the north—whether they can wait for him he doesn't know; and in the years they wait they'd never once receive bread. He said he'd rather go empty-handed; go, and there are people standing, people telling the knotted cord, people reciting scripture; and when they come to the offering bread they stop, and everyone knows where they stop, and knows that there ought to be bread there; and later, when someone comes from the Patriarchate with hands laid on him, they won't have to learn from the start.

He Qi said: if the leader wants to go north, he won't stop him; but Xiaoman—a child of ten—the steppe is cold in winter, the tents let in the wind; let her stay in Xizhou; his house has room; his wife is dead, and the house is short of someone. Xiaoman, beside them, heard, ran over, caught hold of Ayi's sleeve, and said she'd go with Sister Ayi. He Siniang looked at her and said: your Grandpa is going west; go with your Grandpa; Xiaoman shook her head and said: Grandpa has Granny Siniang over there; Sister Ayi has nobody over there.

Lingbao said: what will you do there? You've never had hands laid on you; you can't bless the bread. Jingfu said he knew. He said he can't bless the bread; he can stand morning devotion, can recite scripture, can tell them the things Master told, can tell them that once in Chang'an there was a monastery, and the monastery had a stele, and what was written on the stele. He said: Master at the last bade him: those few scriptures in the scripture room not yet translated—if you can't translate them, leave them for now; left, they won't spoil; he thinks the people of the steppe are those few scriptures not yet translated; he'll go, and leave them for now; left, they won't spoil; later, when someone comes who can lay on hands and bless the bread, then they'll be translated.

Lingbao sat there and heard him out, reached out, and felt Jingfu's face. He felt from Jingfu's forehead to his chin, felt the beard on his face; the beard was already half white. He said: when you came to the monastery at five your face was round; he held you, and you cried; your Pa had died in Huaixi; Lord Mi handed you to Brother; that day Brother gave you a bowl of cold water to drink. Jingfu said he drank it himself; Master stood by watching and didn't stop him. Lingbao smiled and said: right, you drank it yourself; your stomach was good, better than Brother's; Brother drank ginger broth all his life, and you gulped down cold water and nothing happened.

He took something from his robe and put it in Jingfu's hand. It was that knotted cord, of wool, its knots told till they shone, which Elder Ahad had given Brother under the mulberry tree at Balkh; Brother told it all his life and clutched it when he died; Jingfu took it from his hand and gave it to Lingbao. Lingbao said he was going to Balkh; this knotted cord came from Balkh, and by rights should be taken back to Balkh; but he thought: a knotted cord is for telling, not for enshrining; taken back to Balkh and enshrined in a church, no one would tell it; you take it; you're going north; when you stand before light, tell it, one knot, one name; when you come to him, tell a knot for him too. Jingfu gripped the knotted cord in his hand, gripped it. He said: that glass bowl—Uncle Lingbao, you take it; all along the road you'll be blessing bread; the bowl should be with you. Lingbao said: good.

---

They parted ways on the first of the eighth month. That day before light, in the courtyard of the north church of Xizhou, they stood the last morning devotion with all of them together. Jingfu stood in the middle of the courtyard, and when the sky greyed he looked back and counted. Lingbao, He Siniang, Elijah, Salang, Ayi, Xiaoman; the church's three old men, the old woman, Kang Zhen; He Qi. When he had counted, it was thirteen. He read a passage of scripture and wrote a character on that old board; what he wrote was *fen*, to part.

Kang Zhen said that morning he would stay. He said of the three old men in the north church even the youngest was past sixty; after them there'd be no one to keep the church; he'd stay; he knew a few Syriac letters; he'd keep it, and at the offering once in seven recite empty-handed till the day a priest came, and then receive bread.

At the church gate the two parties separated. The party going north—Jingfu, Ayi, Xiaoman—went with a Uighur caravan bound north over the Tianshan to buy horses on the northern steppe; the caravan's headman was the son of the old woman selling curds whom Ayi had found. Jingfu carried a bundle on his back; in the bundle were Jingjing's old rolls and that old board from Xizhou; the knotted cord was tucked in his robe; he led a donkey, and on the donkey's back rode Xiaoman. Ayi rode a little horse a Uighur had lent her, sitting very steady; she said her Ma had told her Uighur children ride at three.

The party going west—Lingbao, He Siniang, Elijah, Salang—went with a Sogdian caravan bound for Kucha to which He Qi had entrusted them. Lingbao lay in the cart, the glass bowl wrapped in his arms, He Siniang beside him. Salang led the first camel, the clay jar bound to his chest. Elijah rode a donkey behind the cart.

He Qi stood at the church gate, Kang Zhen beside him, the three old men inside the gate. The northbound party set off first. Jingfu went a few steps and turned back to look at the westbound party, at that cart, at the man lying in the cart; he could not see, but he had put one hand out from under the felts and was holding it up, holding it up in the air. Jingfu stood on the road looking at that hand, and he too raised his hand, and held it up. Ayi on her horse looked back and called to him: Leader, we're going.

---

Note: For the north church of Xizhou see Chapter 12 (the church repaired by the seven elders, with Jingjing, on their way east in the second year of Zhide). In the mid-ninth century the westward-migrating Uighur tribes scattered north and south of the Tianshan; accounts differ on who held Xizhou, and the novel follows the view that it was under Uighur control. The Syriac and Sogdian Christian texts excavated at Turfan are evidence that the Luminous Teaching survived at Xizhou in the late Tang and early Song. For Psalm 121 see Chapter 68.
