# Volume Seven　A-Lian

## Chapter 43　Narsai

> Once in seven days they make the offering, to wash the heart and return to simplicity.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

That morning Narsai came in at the monastery gate from the well and went straight on into the side courtyard where Yisi lived. The door of the side courtyard was open; there was no one in the outer room; on the low table lay Yisi's account book open, the ink in the inkstone not yet dry, the last line in the accounts recording two *shi* of millet bought the day before. Narsai knelt down across the low table from it, his two hands on his knees; the blood on his hands was already half dry, and the water on the front of his robe was still dripping.

Yisi came back only near the hour of *si*; he had seen the ward headman at the well and sent the ward headman to report to the county. He saw Narsai kneeling there, and stopped. Narsai raised his head and said: "I killed Yonan."

Yisi took the little knife from his sleeve and laid it on the account book, the blade pressing down that line of two *shi* of millet; in the joint between blade and bone handle it was still dark red. He sat down on this side of the low table and said: the lame old man says you went to catch him; the blind old woman heard the sound of someone slipping; Jingjing saw it too; you reached out to catch him. Narsai said he went to catch him with the knife in his right hand, and he had forgotten; the knife had been in his hand thirty-one years, like a finger; he never felt there was a knife in his hand; the knife was his knife, the hand was his hand, the man was the one he caught, and he caught him and the man died; if that didn't count as his killing, whose killing did it count as? He said Yonan at the end said it was you who caught me, said it twice, and never said one word of forgiveness; to him it sounded as if Yonan were bearing witness for him, bearing witness so quickly, as if he'd long had it ready. When he got this far his two hands clenched on his knees, clenched till the knuckles were white; in all these days he had not said as much as in this little while.

Yisi stood up and said: sit here; people will come from the county; when they come, tell it as it was. He went out and pulled the door of the outer room shut behind him, and did not lock it.

---

It was afternoon before the county's people came. The Yining Ward was under Chang'an county; the county sheriff sent a clerk and two runners. The clerk was about thirty, thin, with a pair of moustaches, and coughed before he spoke. At the well he rubbed the blue stone with the toe of his shoe and scraped off a patch of sodden locust flowers, and said a matter of a man's life had to be examined by the county; where was the man? Yonan was laid in that empty room at the north end of the west wing, where Gabriel and Ishaq were washing his body. The clerk squatted down, held a finger against the edge of the wound to measure, said: a finger long, the whole blade went in; and asked where the knife was. Yisi brought it out and said this humble monk pulled the knife out at the well and wiped it on his sleeve; he should not have wiped it. The clerk looked at him once, weighed the knife in his palm, said it was a pen-knife, and had a runner write down: knife a finger long, bone handle, used for trimming pens, the weapon wiped, as the monastery's head himself states.

He called the lame old man and the blind old woman, and last questioned Jingjing. The old woman said she'd been blind twenty years; the sound of someone slipping and the sound of a fight she could tell apart. Jingjing was still in the robe of that morning; he told what he had seen from the beginning, and when he came to "there was no time to shout," paused, and said too Yonan's last words: it was you who caught me.

When he had done questioning, the clerk saluted Yisi and set out the matter in two layers. As to persons: both were foreigners outside the pale, offending among their own kind, and by your own custom the county would not judge it. As to household: the dead man had set up a household in this county after his marriage; his wife was registered in this county; by the code, accidental killing was dealt with by redemption: a hundred and twenty *jin* of copper, levied from the one who wielded the knife and paid to the household of the killed; this was what the Tang code gave to his family, and the county had to ask. He said the word *accident* had a gloss in the code: what eye and ear could not reach, what thought could not foresee; that pen-trimming elder forgetting he had a knife in his hand was exactly what thought could not foresee.

The next afternoon the clerk went to the shop in the West Market to ask A-Lian. Jingjing was kneading dough at the board. The clerk read out that article of the code and asked whether she would accept it. The rolling pin in A-Lian's hand did not stop, and she asked how many cakes a hundred and twenty *jin* of copper would buy. The clerk reckoned and said a good many years' worth. A-Lian said she would not accept it, and pointed with the rolling pin at that small accounts board at the door; the very bottom line was in Yonan's hand, thin letters slanting right; she said he owed her one cake, not copper, and that one, from now on she'd settle with him herself. The clerk could not read that line; he looked at Jingjing, and Jingjing said she would not accept; so the clerk made a note on his paper, bowed, and left.

---

Yonan was buried on the evening of the day he died. Narsai knelt all the while in Yisi's outer room; the bowl of gruel Ishaq brought him was still at the door at night, not one mouthful touched. When Yonan was carried out of the monastery gate they passed the door of the side courtyard; Shiyiniang, through a hole in the window paper, saw Narsai knock his head on the ground toward the door, his forehead pressed to the floor, and when the bearers had gone far off he still had not raised it.

That night Yisi came back into the room, lit the lamp, and told him: buried outside the Kaiyuan Gate, beside that small willow; A-Lian had a *heart* carved. Narsai nodded once. Yisi sat a while, shut the account book, blew out the lamp, and lay down on the old felt by the wall. Shiyiniang in the inner room did not sleep all night; there was not a sound from the outer room; Yisi did not snore. When it was light, Narsai had moved to the middle of the room and was kneeling facing the wooden cross on the east wall.

---

The council was held on the third day, in the back courtyard.

The pomegranate tree was still flowering, and the ground beneath was covered in red. By the order, Chang'an would for the present have no bishop; what a bishop should decide would be decided by the Superintendent together with the Warden of the Rule and the elders in council; cutting off was to be reported to the Patriarchate, and until the Patriarchate's reply came, no one was to be cut off. Azarel, Marya and Samaan sat in the front row; the five left of the seven from Xizhou sat in the second row; Yisi as Superintendent sat beneath the pomegranate tree; Jingjing as Deacon of Charity sat at a small table beside him keeping the record. Narsai stood in the middle, still in that grey robe, the dark patches on its front not washed out.

Samaan first folded his two hands in his sleeves, and only then spoke. He said that one ordained who had blood on his hands, whether by design or by mistake, could no longer stand before the altar; that was an old rule handed down from Ctesiphon; whether to cut him off must be reported to the Patriarchate, and until the Patriarchate's reply came Chang'an could only suspend him from his office. He said he had gone through the canons two nights; the canons had killing by design, killing by mistake, wounding in a fight; there was not one article saying what should be done when catching a man brought about a death. Marya said people in the ward were already saying: the old monk of the *hu* temple took a wife, and on the third day another old monk stabbed him to death. Sabrisho stroked his beard and said tongues grow on other people; you can't stop them; that year when Baaluo went out of the Chunming Gate in a cangue, he had argued for cutting Baaluo off, and Baaluo was cut off, and the talk was not one word the less; that woman was afraid of talk for thirty years, and now her man had died by the well, and she wasn't afraid any more; yesterday the county went to ask whether she wanted the redemption copper, and she said no; if she wasn't afraid, what are we afraid of? Haddad said that on the wedding day Yonan had told him Narsai would come; when he comes, I'll talk to him. Gabriel said that twenty years ago at Xizhou he had asked Jingjing how to say *absolve* in Tang; Jingjing taught him, and he had never used it. He did not go on.

Yisi asked Narsai very little. That morning Narsai had trimmed three pens and laid them on Jingjing's table in the west wing, and then trimmed that bamboo stylus, which was for A-Lian to keep her accounts with; he had trimmed it halfway when he heard Lingbao shout in the courtyard that Elder Yonan was giving out cakes at the gate; he meant to take it first to Yonan to see whether it suited, and went out, not knowing the knife was still in his hand. Jingjing's hand paused on the paper; in the brush rack on his table stood three newly trimmed reed pens; he had seen them that morning and thought it the usual thing.

Yisi asked again: "The night before, he went to your door. Why didn't you open it?"

Narsai's lips moved. He said that night he hadn't yet thought what to say to Yonan. What he was thinking of was that year on the road to Balkh, when Yonan held out flatbread to him and he did not take it; thinking of Xizhou, when Yonan alone recited that character *lian* till midnight, and he listened next door; thinking of the west wing, when Yonan asked Jingjing how to write the word *weakness*, and when it was written said *ruo* was like two people, and said again, two people kneeling, said half of it, and folded the paper and put it inside his robe—that day he sat under the window trimming pens and heard it. He said that by near dawn he had thought it out: he still would not forgive Yonan; a vow was taken to the Lord; he could not forgive it; but that half sentence, he meant to finish it for Yonan: two people kneeling—he would kneel with him, kneel all the way to the Lord, and whether the Lord forgave or not, let the Lord say. At the well he had said the Patriarch had granted it, said he submitted to the Patriarch, and before he had said that half sentence, Yonan reached out for the stylus.

Jingjing's pen came to "two people kneeling," and the ink spread into a small blot on the paper; he did not change the sheet, but went on writing.

Someone was speaking at the courtyard gate: A-Lian. Sargis was stopping her, saying there was a council here; Yisi said let her in. She wore an old blue cloth jacket, flour still on her cuffs, and stood just inside the courtyard gate; she spoke in Tang, and Jingjing translated for the elders sentence by sentence. She said she didn't understand your rules; she had only come to say one thing: he slipped; all his life he walked like that, back straight as a rod, not looking at his feet; she'd told him how many times. When she had said it, she took from her sleeve that bamboo stylus, went to the middle, put it in Narsai's hand, and said: that end of yours isn't finished; finish it, then give it to her; she wanted it to keep accounts with. Narsai looked at her and said one thing in Tang, said even more awkwardly than Yonan's Tang, jabbing the words out one by one: "I... finish." A-Lian nodded, bowed to Yisi, and went out.

Yisi waited till the courtyard gate was shut before he spoke. He said: by the order, Chang'an cannot cut you off; until the Patriarchate's reply comes, you are still of the monastery; from today you do not mount the pulpit, do not read the scripture, do not baptize, do not break the bread; before the altar you do not go to stand; the Body will wait for the Patriarchate's reply.

Narsai, gripping that bamboo stylus, asked why they would not make him pay with his life. He said by the Tang code he who kills pays with his life; by your law, he who sheds blood, his blood too shall be shed; suspension was not enough; to eat and sleep in the monastery every day, looking at that well—he could not bear it. Yisi did not answer him.

It was Jingjing who answered him. Jingjing laid down his pen, stood up, and said he had charge of the charity, and on the matter of charity he could say one thing. He said that every winter in the city of Chang'an there were people frozen to death at the foot of the ward walls, and in the back lanes of the West Market people starved to death, and no one gathered them; the county had them dragged outside the city to the common burial ground, rolled in a reed mat, without so much as a mark; the monastery's gruel and medicine were for the living; the dead the monastery had never seen to. He said Elder Yonan at the end said: it was you who caught me; he wanted to ask the Elder from now on to go and catch these: the ones no one gathers, let the Elder gather, and bury; gather one, record one name; where there's no name, record what day, what place, what age, what clothes; record it on a wooden stake, in Tang characters, so the Tang can read it.

Narsai said his Tang characters were poor. Jingjing said: write till they're good. Samaan asked: those gathered won't have been baptized; where will they be buried? The canons forbid it in the monastery's burial ground. Jingjing said: buried at the edge of the burial ground, a row of their own, no cross set up, a stake driven in. Samaan was going to say something more, and Azarel pressed his sleeve once.

Yisi had stood under the pomegranate tree listening the whole time. He remembered twenty-one years ago on the bank of the Yellow River, Guo Ziyi looking at him and saying: I don't want you to kill men; I want you to see the road. He took out the little knife; it was wiped clean now, the dark red picked out of the joint; he gave it to Jingjing, and Jingjing carried the knife to the middle and laid it on the long bench before Narsai, beside that bamboo stylus. Yisi said: so be it.

Narsai bent down, put the knife into one sleeve and the stylus into the other, went back to his own room, and after a while came out again carrying the stole he had received at his ordination, folded in four, and held it out in both hands to Yisi, and left again, passing Jingjing's small table without looking at Jingjing. What Jingjing wrote on the paper was: "Stole, folded in four, handed to the Superintendent."

When Narsai had gone, Samaan opened his leather-bound account book, wrote a line, and said there was one more thing. He said what had come out of today's council was only the one word *wait*: wait for the Patriarchate, a year and more there and back; for a matter like this there ought to be a bishop, to cut off and to absolve and to lay on hands, and one word from him would settle it. He said Chang'an had been without a bishop nearly thirty years; deacons could not be ordained; priests had no one to ordain them; Yisi was fifty-one, Marya past sixty, he himself past sixty too; in a few more years those in Chang'an who could break the bread could be counted on one hand; Jingjing was thirty and still a deacon "for the time being." He said the canons had someone standing over them; here in Chang'an, the place above was empty. Azarel asked: then what would you do? Samaan said: write; ask the Patriarchate to give Chang'an a bishop; let the deacon write it; his Syriac letters the Patriarchate's scribes all say are good.

Jingjing, at his small table, raised his head. Yisi, under the pomegranate tree, did not look at him.

---

That afternoon Narsai pushed a small cart out of the Yining Ward, a roll of reed matting and an iron spade on it. In the evening he came back, and in the reed mat was wrapped an old beggar who had died at the foot of the wall of the Yankang Ward two days before. He buried the man at the edge of the burial ground outside the Kaiyuan Gate, separated from Yonan's grave by that small willow, in a row of its own, no cross set up; he drove in a wooden stake and wrote on it in charcoal a few Tang characters, written like two crossed spears:

"Eleventh year of Dali, seventh month, second day. Below the wall of the Yankang Ward. Old man, about sixty."

When he had written it he carved it over again on the stake with that knife; charcoal would be washed off by the rain; carving would not.

From then on, every day before light he pushed his cart out, and came back at evening. The Tang characters he could not write he copied from models onto a piece of wood and came back to ask Lingbao. Lingbao was nine, and knew more Tang characters than Syriac letters. Once Narsai had taught him one Syriac letter every other day; now it was turned round; the two of them squatted at the foot of the wall by the side gate, Lingbao wrote a character once in his palm, and he traced it once on the wood, and when he traced it wrong Lingbao said: this stroke has to trail down, trail to the bottom. Once Lingbao asked him: before, when I got a letter wrong, you made me write it twenty times; now that you've traced it wrong, will you write it twenty times too? Narsai said he would, and wrote it twenty times on the ground; it was the character *weng*, old man; the one he had buried that day was another old man.

At the end of the seventh month he finished the bamboo stylus, shaving the square end flat, and had Lingbao take it to the woman who baked cakes in the West Market. A-Lian took it, ran her thumb over the flat end, went to that board at the door, and found the line Yonan had written, owes one cake, in charcoal, already rubbed faint by people's sleeves. She dipped the point of the stylus in a little ink and traced over those few letters one by one, and when the ink dried, that line could never be rubbed off again. She had Lingbao thank that elder for her: the stylus was good to use; from now on she'd keep her accounts with it.

---

At the beginning of the eighth month a company of Sogdian merchants was to leave by the Kaiyuan Gate going north, and Yisi wrote to the Patriarchate.

His Syriac had been taught him as a child by his brother Shapur; in thirty-odd years in Chang'an, apart from saying the offices, he had seldom set pen to it; his letters were big and clumsy, slanting right, and when he wrote one wrong he scraped the parchment thin with a knife and wrote it again. How Yonan slipped, how Narsai went to catch him, how the county judged, how the elders deliberated, he wrote all as it was. Yonan's last words: the Syriac one he wrote as it was; the Tang one he spelled out by sound letter by letter, nine sounds, broken in two, two and then seven. What A-Lian had said—he owes me one cake—he spelled out by sound too. The gathering of the dead he wrote down as proposed by the Deacon of Charity, Adam; Narsai had gathered twenty-three dead this month, and there were Tang characters on every stake. Last he wrote one line: "What the Patriarch granted was not wrong. The wrong is in Chang'an. Chang'an is in my charge. How it is to be dealt with, I will abide by the Patriarch."

Jingjing in the west wing wrote the other letter, in the name of the elders, asking the Patriarchate to give Chang'an a bishop. He drew a reed pen from the brush rack, one of those three that Narsai had trimmed and laid on his table that morning. What the canons and the order said, Samaan had said already; he wrote as Samaan had said, wrote very smoothly, wrote half a sheet. He began a new paragraph, and at its head wrote a few words, Elder Yonan was my—and there stopped, and scraped those words off with the knife; the parchment was scraped thin, and held to the lamp, the light came through. He wrote a few lines anew: This year in Chang'an a man died; he slipped; the one who caught him had a knife in his hand. The one who caught him now gathers the dead in the city of Chang'an; for each one he gathers, he records a name, in Tang characters. The people of Chang'an ask: in such a matter, who decides? We cannot answer. The canons there are; above the canons there is no one. At the end of the letter he wrote the elders' names, and last his own: written by Adam, Deacon of Charity. Narsai's name he did not write.

When both letters were written it was nearly dawn. In the corner of the room leaned an old wooden board, its edges worn round, its face covered with scrape-marks line after line: it was the board he had used twenty years ago at Xizhou to teach the seven elders their characters—water, fire, gate, road, hunger, cold, sickness, peace; it was facing this board that Yonan had traced characters in the sand tray. Jingjing lifted the board onto the table, wiped it clean with a wet cloth, waited for it to dry, took up his own brush, dipped it in ink, and wrote a character, wrote it very large, each stroke set apart, as if writing it for someone who could not read to trace:

*Fu*. Father.

He called Yisi Pa, had called him so thirty years, and would go on calling him so. This character, he had never called anyone. When Yonan was alive he called him Elder; when Yonan was dying, what he called him was still Elder. He looked at the character; the ink slowly dried, dried to a very deep black. Then he turned the board over, the character facing the wall, and leaned it back in the corner.

---

Note: *Tang lü shuyi*, "Mingli": "Foreigners outside the pale who offend among their own kind shall each be judged by their own custom; where those of different kinds offend against each other, they shall be judged by the code." "Dousong": "Whoever by accident kills or wounds a person shall in each case, according to the circumstances, be dealt with by redemption," with the gloss "meaning what eye and ear could not reach, what thought could not foresee"; the commentary says that for accidental killing the redemption is a hundred and twenty *jin* of copper, paid to the household of the one killed or wounded. The Yining Ward belonged to Chang'an county. The treatment of accidental killing in the canons of the Church of the East is the novel's invention (to be checked).
