# Appendix 6　The Name of Heresy: Nestorius, the Church of the East, and Luminous Christology

Everyone in this book, from Yisi to Jingfu, carries one name on his back: heretic. In Chang'an the people of the Tang called them foreign monks; in the churches of the West they were "Nestorians." They have carried that name for more than fifteen hundred years, and only in the last hundred or so has it slowly been looked at again.

In that last century and more, scholars have come to see this history rather differently. What Nestorius himself held may have been some distance from the positions for which he was condemned; the Church of the East was not founded by him; and in 1994 the Catholic Church and the Assyrian Church of the East even signed a common Christological declaration. But that is only one side of the story. The concerns of the Council of Ephesus were not conjured out of thin air; many serious theologians today still think Nestorius's Christology was genuinely flawed; and the Christology of the Church of the East has its own lineage and cannot simply be equated with Nestorius's own views.

So this appendix takes the question in three layers: what Nestorius himself actually held; what the Church of the East, later called "Nestorian," officially believed; and what kind of Christology Alopen and his companions actually brought to Chang'an in the seventh century. The three layers are connected, and also differ a good deal. There is much dispute over the theological details; the reasons on every side are set out here as far as possible, without passing judgement for any of them.

## 1. The starting point: a quarrel over a title

In 428 the monk Nestorius, from Antioch in Syria, was appointed archbishop of Constantinople. Soon after his arrival he was drawn into a controversy over how Mary should be addressed.

The faithful of Constantinople were in the habit of calling Mary *Theotokos*, "God-bearer," that is, "Mother of God." A priest Nestorius had brought with him said in a sermon that no one should call Mary Theotokos: Mary was human, and God could not be born of a human being. Nestorius backed his priest. He feared the title would make people think the Godhead had a beginning and came from a woman, and he proposed instead *Christotokos*, "Christ-bearer," stressing that what she bore was Christ made flesh, in whom the divine and human natures were each complete and unconfused.

His intention was to protect the precision of the faith. He stood in the tradition of the school of Antioch: two generations, from Diodore of Tarsus to Theodore of Mopsuestia, had taught this way, and no one had called them heretics. The historian Henry Chadwick points out that Nestorius said nothing new in his sermons.

But some of what he said sounded very harsh to his opponents. At Ephesus it was reported that he had said privately, "I cannot call a baby two or three months old God." He later explained that he meant one could not say God was two or three months old, not that he refused to acknowledge the child as God. His sermons also contain the words: "I separate the natures, but I unite the worship." To those who supported him, this was carefully distinguishing the divine from the human; to those who opposed him, it was proof that he split Christ in two—one the eternal Son of God, one the man born of Mary, the two merely worshipped together.

Cyril, archbishop of Alexandria, rose against him. Letters went back and forth, in ever sharper language, with the long rivalry between the two great sees behind them. In 431 the emperor summoned the Council of Ephesus. The council, led by Cyril's party, opened before the Antiochene bishops who supported Nestorius had arrived, declared Nestorius a heretic and deposed him. Nestorius was exiled, moved from place to place, and died in the Egyptian desert around 450.

Twenty years later the Council of Chalcedon framed the definition of "one person in two natures": Christ has only one person, but two natures, divine and human, "without confusion, without change, without division, without separation." Many scholars have since pointed out that this formulation is not far from what Nestorius had meant to say. In exile late in life, Nestorius read the letter of Pope Leo of Rome to Constantinople—the *Tome*, on which Chalcedon would later rely—and in his apology declared his agreement with it.

## 2. The reasons of those who condemned him

To look at this history fairly, one has first to understand what the Council of Ephesus and Cyril were really worried about.

Cyril's concern came down, in the end, to one thing: whether the one who became flesh is one and the same. The Christian faith says that the eternal Son of God became man, suffered for men, died, and rose again. If the one born of Mary and dead on the cross was only "a man assumed by God," and the Son of God himself was neither born nor suffered, then salvation comes to nothing: what hung on the cross was only a man very closely joined to God, and God did not himself enter into human birth and death. So Cyril insisted that one may and must say "the Son of God was born of the Virgin," "the Son of God suffered in the flesh"—that the properties of the divine and human natures are all attributed to one and the same subject. In theology this is called the "communication of idioms." Calling Mary *Theotokos* was the touchstone of this principle: the one she bore was the Son of God himself, so she is the Mother of God.

Seen this way, Nestorius never admitted to teaching two sons; what Cyril really seized on was something else: the account he gave of how God and man were united seemed to Cyril not enough to guarantee that Christ was one subject. Nestorius spoke of a "conjunction" (Greek *synapheia*) of the two natures, of two "faces" (*prosopon*) making one "face of union." These words could be understood as one Christ, or as two subjects cooperating; the line was never clear enough.

This criticism still carries great weight today. The German Jesuit Aloys Grillmeier, who studied the history of Christology for decades, held that Nestorius cannot simply be accused of deliberately teaching two sons, but that his theory of union has serious difficulties. The British scholar of Cyril, John A. McGuckin, puts it more bluntly: though Nestorius denied there were two Sons, he never established a truly unified subject in Christ, and the dispute at Ephesus cannot be explained entirely as misunderstanding. The American theologian Roger E. Olson, in *The Story of Christian Theology*, broadly takes this position: he grants that confused terminology and politics were both at work, grants that Cyril's and Nestorius's thought may have been closer than it looked, but his final judgement is still that Nestorius failed to explain how two natures, each with its own properties, truly make up one Christ.

It should also be known that Chalcedon, though its formulation took in the concerns of the Antiochene side, explicitly reaffirmed the condemnation of Nestorius. In 553 the Second Council of Constantinople, in the affair of the "Three Chapters," condemned Theodore of Mopsuestia and his writings as well—and Theodore was precisely the one the Church of the East revered most, as "the Interpreter." So in the Orthodox and Catholic traditions the question was never Nestorius alone. The Eastern Orthodox Church today has not changed its view of Nestorius; the Oriental Orthodox churches, such as the Syriac Orthodox and Coptic Orthodox, regard him as one of the chief heretics.

## 3. What Nestorius himself thought

The modern reassessment of Nestorius began with the rediscovery of a book.

In 1889 a Syriac manuscript was secretly copied out of the patriarch's library at Kochanes in what is now southeastern Turkey, the seat of the patriarch of the Church of the East. It was the apology Nestorius wrote in exile late in his life, issued under the name of "Heraclides of Damascus" to escape destruction, and later known as the *Book of Heraclides*. The original manuscript was destroyed in the First World War; the copies survived. In 1910 the Syriac text and a French translation were published; in 1925 an English translation appeared under the title *The Bazaar of Heracleides*—"bazaar" being a misunderstanding of a Syriac word meaning "treatise."

For the first time people read Nestorius's own words, not his opponents' report of them. The English scholar J. F. Bethune-Baker published *Nestorius and His Teaching* in 1908, and the German Friedrich Loofs *Nestorius and His Place in the History of Christian Doctrine* in 1914; both defended him in some degree. The Byzantinist Milton V. Anastos went furthest, with an article in 1962 whose title was simply "Nestorius Was Orthodox."

But scholars are not agreed on how to use this book.

First, it is Nestorius's retrospect and self-defence more than ten years after the event. Whether a late account can represent his position in 428–431 is itself a question. Some hold that he never taught two subjects and that his early words were taken out of context; some that in exile he really did revise and mature his account; and some that his central thought did not fundamentally change, and that late in life he still had not solved the problem of how two natures make one subject. Olson puts forward two possibilities—that he later really did change some of his views, or that he restated his position to clear his name—without deciding between them.

Second, the German scholar Luise Abramowski argued that the opening part of the book may be by a later hand, the work of a "pseudo-Nestorius," whose Christology differs from Nestorius's own. This adds another layer of uncertainty about which words in the book truly represent him.

Third, even if Nestorius meant something orthodox, whether his way of saying it was adequate is another question. "Failed to explain how the two natures are united in one subject" and "explicitly believed in two Christs" are two different charges. The first is still held by many scholars today; few now think the second can stand.

The American scholar Richard Kyle published an article in 1989 in the *Journal of the Evangelical Theological Society* titled "Nestorius: The Partial Rehabilitation of a Heretic." "Partial rehabilitation" probably sums up the mainstream view of scholars today as well as any phrase can.

## 4. The other lineage of the Church of the East

A common misunderstanding needs clearing up here: the Luminous Religion of this book—the Church of the East—was not founded by Nestorius, and its Christology did not, for the most part, come from him.

Long before Nestorius was born there were Christians in the Persian Empire. They read the scriptures in Syriac and had their centre at Ctesiphon. In 410 they held a synod at Seleucia, accepted the Nicene Creed, and had their own catholicos; in 424 they declared that they would no longer be subject to the Western church. When the Council of Ephesus opened, the Church of the East was already an independent church. The ecumenical councils of the Roman Empire were summoned by the emperor, and the church in Persia did not take part; the decisions of Ephesus had no direct force over it.

The real source of the theology of the Church of the East was Theodore of Mopsuestia. In the 430s a great many of Theodore's works were translated into Syriac at the "School of the Persians" in Edessa. When the school at Edessa was closed in 489, its masters and students moved east into Persia, to Nisibis, and throughout the sixth century the School of Nisibis took Theodore's exegesis and Christology as its standard. In 486 the Church of the East held a synod at Seleucia-Ctesiphon that for the first time formally stated its Christology: it confessed two natures, divine and human, unconfused, and anathematised anyone who said the Godhead suffered or changed. Sebastian P. Brock, the British scholar of Syriac Christianity, points out that the language of this statement belongs to the Theodoran line and shows no influence of Nestorius's own writings. The synod of 605 explicitly required the clergy to accept the commentaries and writings of "the blessed Interpreter, Theodore."

Nestorius's place in this church was chiefly that of a revered father. The Church of the East named him with Diodore of Tarsus and Theodore of Mopsuestia as "the three Greek Doctors" and commemorated him in its liturgy; of its three anaphoras, one bears his name. But Brock says that Nestorius's actual influence on the theology of the Church of the East was very small; his works were little known in the East, the one exception being the *Book of Heraclides*, which was not translated into Syriac until the sixth century. That is, in the hundred years before the Christology of the Church of the East took its settled form, what they read was mainly Theodore, not Nestorius.

## 5. From Babai to Alopen: the Christology that came to the Tang

So in 635, when Alopen reached Chang'an, what did his church officially believe? The answer begins with a dispute inside the Church of the East at the end of the sixth century.

In the second half of the sixth century, Henana of Adiabene, head of the School of Nisibis, departed from Theodore in exegesis and theology; he held that Christ had only "one *qnoma*," close to the Chalcedonian position. The School of Nisibis split over it, and the church argued for decades. Henana's party had the support of the court physician Gabriel at the Persian court; it was said that "the medicine of the palace was all on Henana's side."

The one who rose against Henana was the monk Babai (Babai the Great, c. 551–628). He wrote the *Book of the Union* (*Liber de Unione*), a systematic account of his church's Christology that became the standard teaching of the Church of the East: Christ has "two *kyane*, two *qnome*, one *parsopa*." In 612, in a public debate with the West Syrian Miaphysites, the bishops of the Church of the East for the first time formally used the phrase "two *qnome*." From then on this formula was the orthodoxy of the Church of the East.

These three Syriac words need explaining. *Kyana* corresponds roughly to the Greek *physis*, "nature." *Parsopa* comes from the Greek *prosopon* and corresponds to "person." The troublesome one is *qnoma*. By the dictionary, *qnoma* is the standard rendering of the Greek *hypostasis*, and the Chalcedonian definition says Christ has "one hypostasis," which in Syriac becomes "one *qnoma*." So to Greek and West Syrian ears, when the Church of the East said "two *qnome*," it was saying Christ had two persons—Nestorian heresy pure and simple. Brock's research, however, shows that in the usage of East Syrian writers, *qnoma* means something like "the concrete reality of a nature, its defining characteristics." Babai usually says "the two natures and their *qnome*," stressing that the divine and human natures each really exist, joined from the moment of conception in the one person. To them it was the Chalcedonian formula that was faulty: with only one *qnoma*, one of the two natures would have to be unreal. The two sides used the same word for two different things, and each thought the other wrong.

The dispute inside the Church of the East did not end in 612. In the mid-seventh century a bishop and spiritual writer named Martyrius-Sahdona again taught "one *qnoma*," and was deposed for it; in 676 the patriarch Gewargis I held a synod over it and wrote a letter in refutation. Bishara Ebeid, who teaches at the University of Venice and has studied the creeds of the synods of the Church of the East before 612, argues that the "two *qnome*" line won out gradually over generations of dispute, and was not fixed from the start.

Taking all this together, we can answer the question readers of this book may care about most: how much did the Christology the Luminous Religion held when it came to the Tang differ from Nestorius's own views?

There were differences, in three main places. First, the source differed: the Christology of the Church of the East came chiefly from Theodore, Nestorius being only one member of the same tradition, and his book only translated in the East in the sixth century. Second, the terminology differed: Nestorius spoke of two "faces" making one "face of union"; Babai and the synod of 612 spoke of "two *kyane*, two *qnome*, one *parsopa*," stating "one person" more definitely than Nestorius had—and it was within exactly this framework that the Church of the East firmly denied "two sons." Third, the degree of precision differed: after more than a century of debate, especially with Henana and with the Miaphysites, by the time Alopen set out the Church of the East had a quite rigorous formulation. Alopen reached Chang'an twenty-three years after the synod of 612 and seven years after Babai's death. What he brought must have been this newly settled Christology.

But there was also continuity. The Church of the East always insisted that the divine and human natures were each complete and unconfused; it was always unwilling to say things like "God suffered" or "God died," and the synod of 486 even attached an anathema to them; in the matter of Mary's title it always preferred "Mother of Christ"—Babai accepted *Theotokos* in certain contexts, but stressed that the two natures existed together. These were exactly the points on which Cyril and Nestorius had quarrelled. So to say that the Church of the East "differed greatly" from Nestorius is not accurate. A better way to put it may be this: they stood in the same river of the Antiochene tradition; Nestorius was a name upstream, made conspicuous by controversy; the Church of the East was a channel drawn off from Theodore, which had run through Persia for two hundred years and been banked and dredged into good order. Whether the river itself is orthodox is precisely the heart of a dispute that has lasted fifteen hundred years, and today the Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox and Assyrian churches still do not give quite the same answer.

## 6. Christ in the Chinese Luminous texts

The Chinese-language Luminous texts do not discuss terms like *qnoma*. They faced Tang readers and had more basic things to say. Still, some clues can be read between their lines.

The stele speaks of the incarnation in this sentence:

> 我三一分身景尊彌施訶，戢隱真威，同人出代。

That is: one Person of the Trinity, the Luminous Lord Mishihe, folded away His true majesty and came into the world in the likeness of men. The sentence has one subject only: it is Mishihe himself, who belongs within the Three-in-One, who "came into the world in the likeness of men." This is a very typical statement of the incarnation; it shows no sign of dividing Christ in two.

The *Hymn in Praise of the Three Majesties* (*Sanwei mengdu zan*) calls Christ "Mishihe, the universally honoured great holy Son," and says "King of everlasting life, the merciful and joyful Lamb, who bore great suffering everywhere and did not shrink from toil": the one who suffers is "the merciful and joyful Lamb," the Messiah as Lamb of God; the hymn does not say that Alaha (God) suffered. This is consistent with the Church of the East's habit of avoiding phrases like "God suffered," though Chinese hymns are terse by nature and one should not draw too much from them.

The stele has another feature that is often mentioned: it tells of the birth and of the ascension at "high noon," but says almost nothing of the suffering on the cross or the resurrection. Some have taken this as evidence that "Nestorians" avoided the Passion. That claim does not stand. The *Jesus-Messiah Sutra* (*Xuting mishisuo jing*) describes in detail how the Messiah was bound to the wood and died, "the four quarters went dark, the earth shook and the mountains crumbled, and every tomb in the world was opened." The stele's omission of the Passion is more likely a deliberate avoidance of executions and punishments in a text meant for Tang officials and the emperor—a matter of missionary strategy, with little to do with Christological party.

On the whole, the Christ of the Chinese Luminous texts is a Mishihe who was born as a man, taught, died and ascended—plainly described, in a great many words borrowed from Buddhism and Daoism, with no trace of "two sons." The scholar of the Luminous Religion Glen L. Thompson, in a recent monograph, discusses its Christology specifically and comes to broadly the same conclusion: the basic faith and structure of the Church of the East were preserved in the Luminous Religion.

## 7. A thousand-year label

From the fifth century to modern times, in the minds of Western Christians the Church of the East was always "the Nestorian heresy."

In the thirteenth century the Franciscan William of Rubruck went on a mission to the Mongols and met many clergy of the Church of the East at the court in Karakorum. His travel account is often critical of them—ignorant, he says, fond of drink, unseemly in conduct. Marco Polo too mentions "Nestorian Christians" many times, scattered along the trade roads from Persia to China. When Westerners met them, the first reaction was surprise—so there are Christians in the East too—and the second was wariness: a pity they are heretics.

After the stele was unearthed in the seventeenth century, the Jesuits for a time were unwilling to admit that the people on it were "Nestorians," preferring to explain them as early Catholics (see the Prologue). When the Syriac had been read and the facts were plain, many turned instead to stressing that there was not one heretical word in the inscription. Whatever the explanation, almost no one questioned the premise of "heresy."

The Church of the East itself always rejected the name "Nestorian" and called itself "the Church of the East." There were writers of the Church of the East in history who used the name of themselves, but only after others had called them so for centuries. In 1976 the church formally took the name "Assyrian Church of the East." In 1996 Brock published an article whose title was simply "The 'Nestorian' Church: A Lamentable Misnomer"; in 2024, in another summarising article, he repeated that the name means entirely different things to different people and should be avoided.

## 8. Reconciliation between the churches, and its limits

The scholarly reassessment eventually reached the churches themselves.

In 1984 the patriarch of the Assyrian Church of the East, Mar Dinkha IV, made the first official visit to the Vatican. On 11 November 1994 Pope John Paul II and Mar Dinkha IV signed the *Common Christological Declaration* in Rome. In language both sides could accept, the declaration confesses that Christ is true God and true man, the divine and human natures united in one person without confusion, without change, without division, without separation. On the title of Mary that had caused a thousand years of dispute, the declaration says that the Assyrian Church of the East calls her "the Mother of Christ our God and Saviour," and the Catholic Church calls her "the Mother of God" and also "the Mother of Christ," and both recognise these as legitimate expressions of the same faith. It also says that the divisions of the past arose in large part from misunderstandings.

In 2001 the Catholic Church, in a pastoral guideline, recognised as valid the ancient eucharistic prayer of the Church of the East, the *Anaphora of Addai and Mari*. This prayer lacks the "words of institution" usually regarded as essential, and Rome still recognised it as valid—a very rare thing in the history of the liturgy.

But the declaration's significance has limits, which should be made clear. First, it concerns the faith the two churches share today; it is not a verdict on fifth-century historical figures. It does not mention Nestorius by name, does not rehabilitate him personally, and does not revoke the decisions of Ephesus or of the Second Council of Constantinople. The Catholic Church has not changed its condemnation of Nestorius himself, while the Assyrian Church of the East still commemorates Nestorius and the two Theodores in its liturgy. Second, only the Catholic Church has signed such a declaration with the Assyrian Church of the East. The Eastern Orthodox and Oriental Orthodox churches have reached no similar agreement with it. Brock writes in his 2024 article that the Assyrian Church of the East is still excluded from the Middle East Council of Churches—"a wound that urgently needs healing." Third, the Church of the East still does not use phrases like "God suffered," and in the tradition that follows Cyril this is not a matter of mere wording.

So what the 1994 declaration shows is that the Church of the East, long called "Nestorian," can today confess together with the Catholic Church the unity of Christ's person. That is a fact of real weight. But it does not mean that the old dispute was all a misunderstanding, nor that history has reversed the verdict on Nestorius.

## 9. In the Chinese-speaking world

The Chinese term *jingjiao* comes from the stele itself. The stele says the true and constant Way is wondrous and hard to name, its works shine so plainly that we are forced to call it the Luminous Teaching. This is the name the believers of the Church of the East in the Tang gave themselves: the teaching of light.

Since modern times Chinese scholars studying the Luminous Religion have mostly followed Western usage and called it "Nestorian," often with the word "heretical" in front. Textbooks, encyclopedia entries and sermons from the pulpit today, when they mention the Luminous Religion, still mostly say one sentence: a heretical form of Christianity that entered China in the Tang, the Nestorian sect. The scholarly discussions of the last hundred years and the common declaration of 1994 are largely unknown to ordinary Chinese Christians.

This novel does not set out to rehabilitate anyone, nor to pass sentence on anyone. Its people have their weaknesses and their errors, like people of any age and any church. Only, in reading their story, the reader might remember three things: that the controversy which pinned the label "Nestorian" on them began with a title, was tangled with rivalry between sees, and still contains serious theological disagreement; that their own church had a longer lineage, independent of Nestorius; and that what they brought to Chang'an was a Mishihe who "folded away His true majesty and came into the world in the likeness of men."
