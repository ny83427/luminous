# Volume Six　The Shuofang Army

## Chapter 35　Pugu Huaien

> The sick were healed and raised up.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

In the third month of the second year of Guangde, the first thunder of the year sounded over Lingzhou. There was no rain that night, only wind, blowing over from the Helan Mountains, blowing till the paper in the windows of the governor's headquarters flapped; the thunder rolled a few times far off, then suddenly was overhead, a *crack* like someone splitting the sky.

Huaien sat in the hall and did not sleep. In more than a month at Lingzhou he had not had one good night's sleep; when he lay down, his sword lay by his pillow, and every little while he woke, and on waking called for someone and asked whether there was any stir at the gate. Spread before him was a document from Chang'an, its wrapper stamped with a vermilion seal: the court, mindful of his past services, would not pursue what had gone before; it conferred on him in absentia the titles of Grand Preceptor, concurrently Director of the Secretariat, and Prince of Daning, and his posts as Military Governor of Shuofang and the rest were suspended; if only he would come to court, all could be settled. He read it through once, and again. A servant of fifteen or sixteen came in carrying a basin of hot water to wash his feet; the boy had been taken into the household only after they came to Lingzhou, and was not yet very handy, and just as he set the basin down there was another thunderclap outside; his hand shook, the rim of the basin knocked the floor, and hot water splashed out onto Huaien's boots. Huaien looked down at his boots, stood up, took the horsewhip down from the wall, and brought it down once, and then again. The boy knelt on the floor, both arms over his head, without a sound; several guards stood below the hall, and not one came forward. Then the old officer Baye the Third came in from outside, took one look, went up and caught Huaien's arm from behind, and said: Governor, enough. Huaien turned his head and looked at him, those long narrow eyes red. The two of them stood locked like that; Huaien's arm slowly went slack, and the whip fell to the floor.

Outside, all at once, everything blazed bright. The lightning had struck the flagpole outside the headquarters gate, a pole of old pine set up twenty years before, from which flew the great banner of the Military Governor of Shuofang; it split from the top in one stroke, and the banner went up with a *whoosh*; with no rain, in the blink of an eye it was a ball of fire, wheeling in mid-air. Everyone in the headquarters ran out; some shouted to put out the fire, some shouted fire from heaven, some knelt on the ground knocking their heads. Huaien went out too, and stood at the headquarters gate, head tilted back, watching the banner burn, the firelight making his cheekbones higher and his eye sockets deeper. Someone behind him said softly that it was an ill omen; Huaien gave a laugh and said: they say I'm rebelling—that's what you call an ill omen. He turned and went back into the hall, took up the document from Chang'an, lit it at the lamp, and threw it into the brazier. The boy who had been whipped was gone the next day; some said he had run away, some said he was dead; no one went looking for him.

---

The Pugu were a branch of the Tiele, who had submitted to the Tang back in the Zhenguan years; Huaien's ancestors had been enfeoffed as Governor-General of Jinwei, and generation after generation had held the north for the Tang. Huaien himself had been in the Shuofang army since the Tianbao years; when An Lushan rebelled he fought his way out of Shuofang with Guo Ziyi, fought the Tongluo, fought in Hebei, fought for the two capitals, fought until Shi Chaoyi hanged himself, seven or eight more years all told. His son Pugu Fen had surrendered to the rebels on the field and then escaped back, and he had beheaded his son with his own hand before the troops; his two daughters had married far away to the Uighurs, making this alliance for the Tang; forty-six of his brothers, sons and nephews had died on the field. These things he had written out himself, one by one, in that petition, written them as six crimes. That year, coming back from Hebei, he had passed Taiyuan, and Xin Yunjing had shut the city gates tight, and the men on the wall had shouted at his banners: any of the Pugu who wants to come into the city, leave your troops outside first. He stood below the wall half a day, then turned his horse's head and left. In the first month of the second year of Guangde, when the Shuofang troops heard Lord Guo had reached Hezhong, they ran south night after night, and his son Pugu Yang was killed at Yuci by his own men. On the day he left, his old mother came after him out of the camp gate with a knife in her hand, her hair loose, cursing as she ran: where has the country wronged you, that you do a thing like this? I'll kill you for the country, and take out your heart to show the three armies. He rode away and did not look back. He crossed the river north with three hundred-odd horse, came to Lingzhou, and in the gateway killed Hun Shizhi.

That year at Lingzhou he was like a man fallen down a well; the mouth of the well grew smaller and smaller, and he was at the bottom, and whoever reached a hand down, he bit. Zhang Shao did not live out that year either. Huaien despised him as a man without loyalty, had both his legs broken, and threw him into the town of Mi'e, where no one tended him, and so he died. The people of Lingzhou, when they spoke of him, did not much care to say more.

---

In the tenth month of that year Huaien led a hundred thousand-odd Tibetan and Uighur horse bearing down from the northwest, all the way to Fengtian. Yisi now held the title of Assistant Vice-Military Governor of Shuofang, still at Lord Guo's service before his tent, and had hurried there from Hezhong with Guo Ziyi to hold Fengtian. The generals asked to give battle; Guo Ziyi would not allow it, saying: they've come far, their advantage is in a quick fight; if we hold the city and don't go out, they can't stay many days. The Tibetans circled outside the city for a few days, plundered some cattle and sheep, burned some villages, and sure enough, left. All those days Yisi was on the wall, watching the campfires outside spread patch by patch, like stars grown out of the ground; he thought one cluster of them was Huaien's, but he did not know which.

In the autumn of the first year of Yongtai, news came again from the north that Huaien was coming again, with more men this time, Tibetans, Uighurs, Tanguts and Tuyuhun all among them. Guo Ziyi was readying troops at Hezhong, awaiting the court's edict.

Late in the eighth month a man came to the camp gate at Hezhong, wearing a Uighur leather robe, but speaking the speech of Shuofang; brought before the headquarters tent, he knelt and said nothing else but that he wanted to see the Great Virtue Yi. Yisi came out, and only when the man was sure it was he did he say: his name was Pugu Hong, he was of Military Governor Pugu's command, had followed the Governor twenty years; in camp they couldn't break the habit and still called him Governor. The Governor had been sick seven or eight days, with the flux, passing nothing but pus and blood, throwing up whatever he ate; the camp doctors had been changed three times: the first one's medicine the Governor drank and threw up, said there was poison in it, and had him given forty strokes and driven out of camp; the second ran away in fright; the third didn't get away, and was beaten to death. A few of the old brothers had talked it over and thought of the Great Virtue Yi. He said he had seen with his own eyes in the Shuofang camp how the Great Virtue Yi treated the brothers' wounds; a literate clerk in camp had said, too, that the Great Virtue Yi's family had practised medicine for three generations, and the Tang had an old saying, *if a physician's line is not three generations deep, take not his medicine*, and the Great Virtue Yi's was three generations deep, and he had medicines from the west besides. He said too that they'd heard how, that time in the palace the year before last, the Great Virtue Yi had spoken for the Governor before the Sage One. As he spoke he knocked his head on the ground, and said: "The Governor doesn't know I've come. If he knew, he'd kill me."

Yisi went into the tent to report. Guo Ziyi, sitting behind his desk, heard it out, asked the man which road he had come by, how many parties of horse he had seen on the way, how many men Huaien still had about him. When he had asked, he said: "Go, then. If he's willing to turn back, persuade him to turn back. If he won't be persuaded, see what kind of men he has around him." And having said it, he bent his head to the map on the desk.

Yisi went back to his own tent, opened his medicine bag, and looked at what was in it. A small clay jar, its mouth sealed with wax, a cross stamped in the wax: the jar of theriac from the hidden compartment of the medicine chest in the Yining Ward, which the Patriarchate had long ago sent to Chang'an by a caravan. He had opened it for the first time the winter Chang'an was recovered; in the years since, when someone at the monastery had a bad flux, he had used it a few more times, and of those he used it on, some had lived and some had not. The year before last, going to Hezhong, he had taken it out of the hidden compartment and carried it with him; there was only half left in the jar. Besides that there was a packet of coptis root and a packet of smoked plums, made up at Shopkeeper Wang's shop in the West Market. When he was small in Balkh, he had carried the medicine box behind his grandfather; his grandfather was by then so old his hands shook, yet when he felt a pulse they did not shake at all; later, when his father Milis became a priest, by day he said the offices, and at night still people came knocking to ask him to see the sick, and Yisi carried that old medicine box after him. One year two households in the city had children with the flux at the same time; his father gave the house at the east end a heating medicine and the house at the west end a cooling one, and when Yisi asked why, his father told him to go and feel the two children's hands for himself. In Chang'an he had carried the medicine box for some years for the old teacher at the monastery who could heal eyes. He put the things away, tied the bag tight, and went out and took the road with Pugu Hong.

---

Yisi travelled seven days. Two men, two horses, by byways, hiding in gullies by day and travelling by night; on the way they met Tibetan scouting parties a few times, and avoided them all. When they rested at night, Pugu Hong told him in bits and pieces about the Governor's year. He said the Governor hardly slept at night; when he lay down he woke every little while, and on waking asked whether anyone had come from Chang'an; they said no, and he lay down, and after a while woke again and asked again. People had come from Chang'an a few times, all to urge him to come to court, and every time one came, he beat someone; yet when no one came, he still asked. He had his servants taste his food first, had someone drink his water first; his sword never left him; his tent was pitched in a different place every night, and when it was pitched he still was not easy, and in the middle of the night had it struck and pitched again. The camp had fewer men day by day; some days ago a guard who'd followed the Governor twenty years had left in the night, leaving only a sword on his bedding; the Governor sent men after him, said bring him back and he'd be cut down; the men went, and they didn't come back either. When Pugu Hong had got this far, he put the half flatbread left in his hand back inside his coat and said: "Great Virtue Yi, it's not that we don't want to leave. We can't. He's like this now, but in the old days on the field, he took sword-blows for us."

At dusk on the seventh day, they reached Mingsha. By then Guo Ziyi had received his edict and led his troops toward Jingyang.

Mingsha was a small county south of Lingzhou, on the Yellow River; outside the town was a stretch of sand hills, and when the wind blew, the sand sang, a humming, like many people chanting scripture far away. Huaien's main army had already gone south; he was too sick to go on, and his retainers were to carry him back to Lingwu, but when they got here they could carry him no further, and pitched their tents by the river, with only a few hundred men left about him.

The guards at the tent door took Yisi's mace and knife before they let him in. A smell rushed out at him; he had smelled it at Changshan, and in the eastern half of the main hall in the Yining Ward. Huaien lay on a felt, so thin there was only a frame of bones left, the flesh of his face all gone, the skin pulled tight over the bone, his cheeks sunk into two pits, his cheekbones like two stones pushing up the skin, his lips cracked, black scabs in the cracks. By his hand lay an unsheathed short knife, a foot or so long, its hilt wrapped in blue cloth. His eyes were open, still long and narrow, but sunk too deep, so that looking at people he seemed to be looking out from a cave very far away. He saw Yisi, studied him a while, knew him, had them help him sit up, propped straight against a roll of felt, and only then said hoarsely: "The trick-performer." When he had said it, the corner of his mouth moved. He saw Pugu Hong behind Yisi too, and said: "You." Pugu Hong knelt down.

Yisi squatted by the edge of the felt, looked first at his colour, had him open his mouth and looked at his tongue: dry, with a yellow coating; then took his pulse: fine as a thread, beating fast and ragged. He had them bring what the Governor had passed for him to see, had them fetch a bowl of urine and looked at its colour against the lamp, asked how many times a day he passed, whether his belly hurt when he did, whether he had fever at night; asked in great detail. At first Huaien did not answer; later Pugu Hong answered for him. When Yisi had done asking, he said: this was the red flux, dragged on too long; the body's fluids had all run out; there was heat within; first stop the blood, settle the belly, then feed in water and rice mouthful by mouthful; save the life first, and the rest after. Back in the Yining Ward, when he prescribed for the flux, he had put in dried ginger, and Shopkeeper Wang had once asked him, "You still put in dried ginger?"—that time the patient was cold without; this time the tongue was dry and the coating yellow, and dried ginger could not go in at all. He had them only boil a bowl of coptis and smoked plum, the plum for that bit of binding it gave. He had a pot of very thin rice water boiled too, with a pinch of salt thrown in; then he opened the small clay jar, peeled back a little of the wax, and with a finely shaved bamboo splint lifted out a little of the blackish-brown paste—three or four times what he had given the child in the Yining Ward, and still no more than half a bean—then looked at how wasted out of all shape Huaien was, scraped a little off, and dissolved it in half a bowl of warm rice water.

Huaien watched him the whole while, and when he opened the clay jar, gave a cold laugh and asked what that was. Yisi said it was called theriac, a medicine from the west; the Tang *Materia Medica* recorded it too, saying it came from the Western Rong; the books said it was boiled from gall, but in fact it wasn't; the most important ingredient in it was what the Tang called *minang*, poppy; drink it and the belly would stop raging, and you'd feel drowsy and sleep deep—there was no need to be afraid. Huaien said: "I, Pugu Huaien, have fought twenty years and cut off more heads than I can count, and in the end it comes to a *hu* monk lifting a bit of black paste on a bamboo splint to feed me. Did Guo Ziyi send you? Why didn't he come himself?" Yisi said: "Lord Guo allowed this humble monk to come. Lord Guo said, if the Governor is willing to turn back, persuade the Governor to turn back." Huaien, hearing it, turned his face away and said: take it away; and said again: "Go back and tell Guo Ziyi, if he wants my life, let him come and take it himself; there's no need to trick me with medicine."

Pugu Hong, kneeling on the ground, moved forward two steps on his knees and said: Governor, we few begged for this medicine; it's no affair of the Great Virtue Yi's; if the Governor must kill, kill me. He said that when the Governor charged into the ditch at the Xiangji Temple, the Great Virtue Yi was right behind the Governor's horse; and the year before last in the palace, when Luo Fengxian said the Governor was rebelling, it was the Great Virtue Yi who said before the Sage One that the Governor wasn't. What he had heard had already changed shape by the time it reached his mouth. As he spoke he began to knock his head on the ground, knocking till his forehead was covered in sand. The old officer Baye the Third knelt too, only knelt.

Huaien turned his face back, looked at Pugu Hong, looked at Yisi, and asked Yisi what in fact he had said before the Sage One—had he said he wasn't rebelling? Yisi said: "This humble monk didn't say the Governor wasn't rebelling. This humble monk said he couldn't see that the Governor meant to. This humble monk doesn't know what the Governor thinks in his heart; he knows only that that day at the Xiangji Temple the Governor charged into the ditch to the east and didn't turn his horse's head west. Whatever this humble monk sees, that's what he says." Huaien stared at him; that layer in his eyes stirred, and closed again; and he said: "You drink first."

Yisi dissolved another bowl, the same dose as Huaien's, took it up and drank it all down, put down the bowl, and waited. Huaien waited too, everyone in the tent waited; after as long as it takes to eat a meal, Yisi sat there, his colour as usual, only his eyelids somewhat heavy. Only then did Huaien have them help him up; Yisi held the back of his head and brought the bowl to his lips, and he drank half a bowl, then drank the bowl of coptis and plum, so bitter his whole face screwed up, and after all did not throw it up.

---

At first it went well. After a good quarter hour the griping pain in his belly, coming in wave after wave, slowly eased; he grew warm, his eyelids heavy; he lay back against the felt, his breathing even. At the tent door, Pugu Hong wiped his eyes. Yisi kept watch beside him, every little while feeling his pulse.

By midnight things had changed. Huaien slept very deep, so deep that at last it was as if someone were pressing him down into a deep well; he could not wake, and yet could not sleep easy; in his dreams people came one by one: first his son Pugu Fen, that knife-cut on his neck still running blood, standing at the tent door looking at him; then Hun Shizhi, leaning against the wall of the gateway, his beard stirring; then many faces, from Chang'an, from Taiyuan, ones he knew and ones he did not, none speaking, only looking at him. He wanted to raise his hand to his sword and could not raise it; his limbs were heavy as if they were not his own; he wanted to shout and could not make a sound. Only one thought was left in his heart: there was something in the medicine; he had been tricked.

When at last he struggled awake, only a small lamp was lit in the tent; Yisi sat in the corner, the old officer at the tent door. Huaien snatched up the knife by his pillow and sat up, sat up too fast, everything went black, then steadied; he shouted hoarsely for men, said the *hu* monk had poisoned him, tie him up. The men outside ran in and stood there not knowing whether to move. To Huaien everyone looked to be in league with the *hu* monk; the knife-point went from one man to the next, came to the old officer's face, and he said: you too, you went over to Guo Ziyi long ago, all of you, one by one, you're all hoping I'll die. The old officer knelt before him, did not dodge, did not speak, and the knife-point stopped before his forehead, trembling. Yisi stood up from the corner, went slowly over, squatted, and said: Governor, this humble monk drank the medicine first; this humble monk is drowsy too, his eyelids are heavy too; feel this humble monk's pulse, Governor; it's slow too. He held out his empty hand. The knife-point trembled before the old officer's forehead, trembled until the lamp-wick sputtered, then slid out of Huaien's hand and fell on the felt; he fell back and breathed in great gasps, gasped until dawn, and fell asleep again.

---

That night Yisi did not close his eyes; every little while he leaned over to feel Huaien's breath at his nostrils, as he had once kept watch over that child in the Yining Ward. What he feared was the poppy.

When it was light, Huaien woke, somewhat clearer, passing less; he could hold a bowl of rice water himself. Pugu Hong was so glad he stood outside the tent rubbing his hands. Yisi took his pulse again; it was steadier than in the night; he said they would give the medicine once more today, a little less again; for the next three days, once a day; if he could get through these few days, there was hope.

Huaien lay back against the felt, looking at the tent roof, and when he spoke his voice was very low. "I haven't slept like that in a year. Sleeping like that, anyone could come into the tent and cut off my head and I'd never know. You say I can get well. And after I'm well? After I'm well, Guo Ziyi's men will come, the men from Chang'an will come, they'll lift me into a cart and carry me all the way to Chang'an, the same as Lai Tian; once into the capital, never out again. Guo Ziyi sends a *hu* monk to save my life so as to keep my life for Chang'an to take." He spoke very slowly, word by word, as if the more he said, the more he believed it. Yisi said: "The road after you're well, this humble monk can't see. This humble monk can only see these few days. What Lord Guo said, this humble monk has told the Governor." Huaien gave a cold laugh and said: "You're a *hu* too. You've seen the road for them all your life; see whether they trust you." Yisi did not answer this; he thought of that bowl of cold gruel in the Qinren Ward. That evening, Yisi dissolved the second dose, and as before drank a mouthful of it himself first, then brought it over. Huaien looked at the bowl and put out his hand. His hand shook very badly; his fingers touched the rim, the bowl rocked once and did not tip; his hand rested a moment on the rim, and then pushed the bowl away. He did not push it far, only a foot; the bowl turned half round at the edge of the felt and stopped, a little rice water slopping out and soaking into the sand. He drew his hand back and laid it on his chest, closed his eyes, and said: "I won't go. Go, and I won't come back."

After that he would not take another mouthful of theriac. Yisi said they need not use that ingredient, only plum broth and rice water; he would not take those either, would hardly even drink the rice water. Pugu Hong knelt and pleaded; he had Pugu Hong dragged out and given twenty blows with a staff, and when it was done, had him dragged back and made to kneel at the tent door, and not allowed to leave. In the middle of the night he began to rave, in Pugu speech; Yisi could hardly understand it, could only make out that he was calling names, the same two names over and over. Baye the Third listened with his head bowed, and afterwards said softly to Yisi: his two sons, one he killed himself, one someone else killed. He said too: "Great Virtue Yi, I've followed him thirty years. When my brother died on the field back then, it was he who carried the body back on his horse; if it weren't for that, I'd have left long ago. Now I don't know whether I stay to repay him, or to suffer this with him."

Two more days he held on. On the third day, just before light, Huaien woke once, turned his head, and saw the bowl still there by his head; the medicine in it had long gone cold, a thin skin formed on its surface; his eyes slowly closed, and did not open again.

---

The day he died was early in the ninth month; the exact day Yisi could not afterwards remember. By the custom of the Pugu, when a man died his kin rode their horses round his tent and cut their faces with knives, weeping till blood and tears flowed together; the retainers rode round that tent seven times, and not one wept; only Baye the Third got down from his horse, drew his knife, and cut a line down his own face. Without a sound they went out, cut down the willows by the river, broke up a few carts, and piled the wood into a stack higher than a man; carried Huaien out and laid him on the stack, dressed him in that old robe, and laid his sword beside him. Baye the Third stood before the stack with a torch, the cut on his face still running blood; he looked back once at Yisi, and threw the torch on. The willow wood was wet, and at first would not catch, giving off white smoke; then the dry wood from the carts caught, and the fire leapt all at once higher than the stack, roaring, mingling with the sound from the sand hills. The retainers stood round the fire in a circle, and someone began to sing something low in Pugu speech, very slow, very long, no words, only a tune. Pugu Hong, the wounds on his back not yet healed, knelt at the very front. Yisi stood outside the circle. The fire burned all day; toward evening the stack fell in, leaving only a heap of red embers, glowing and dimming in the wind. The retainers waited till the embers cooled, picked the bones out of the ash, put them in a leather bag, and buried it under the sand by the river; no stone was set up, no mound heaped; when it was buried, someone led a horse back and forth over it a few times, trampling the ground flat.

That night Yisi did not leave, but sat by the river, not far from that trampled sand. The moon came out and lit the river; the water was very swift, rushing north. The small clay jar was still in his medicine bag; he had sealed the wax over its mouth again; there was a little less in it again. Baye the Third came over and sat down beside him, and said: "Great Virtue Yi, in that religion of yours, when a man dies, you say something over him, don't you? He didn't believe in your religion; he believed in nobody; but he's dead, and there's no one to say anything over him. You say something over him."

Yisi laid his hand on his chest, and did not know what to say. He thought of Huaien in the tent laughing at him as a trick-performer; thought of Huaien that day at the Xiangji Temple leading his men into the ditch through the fog; thought of the gateway the old Lingzhou soldier had told of; thought of that bowl pushed away; and at last he thought of those words: I won't go; go, and I won't come back. He bowed his head and said: "O Lord, have mercy on him. Have mercy on his fear."

---

Note: That Huaien "fell ill and was carried back, and died at Lingwu on the ninth day of the ninth month," and that his retainers "burned and buried him by the custom of his people," follows his biography in the *Jiu Tang shu*; Lingwu is Lingzhou, and Mingsha county belonged to it. Huaien's being "appointed in absentia Grand Preceptor, concurrently Director of the Secretariat, Prince of Daning, the rest of his posts suspended" follows the same biography. The custom of riding round the tent and cutting the face follows the account of the Turks in the *Zhou shu*. The Pugu tribe's submission in the Zhenguan years and hereditary office as Governor-General of Jinwei, Huaien's beheading of his son Pugu Fen, his two daughters married to the Uighurs, and the forty-six of his brothers, sons and nephews who died in the royal service follow his biographies in both Tang histories and his *Petition in His Own Defence*. Zhang Shao's death follows the *Zizhi tongjian* (his shins broken, left at the town of Mi'e to die); the *Jiu Tang shu* has him killed by Xu Huangyu after Huaien's death. Huaien's mother pursuing and cursing him, the raid on Fengtian in the tenth month of the second year of Guangde, and the invasion by the various peoples in the ninth month of the first year of Yongtai follow the *Tongjian*. Theriac appears in the *Xinxiu bencao* ("comes from the Western Rong," said to be made of various galls); western theriac recipes contain poppy juice, as did Galen's. Coptis and smoked plum were standard Tang treatments for the flux. "If a physician's line is not three generations deep, take not his medicine" is from the *Liji*, "Quli"; "ten out of ten cured is the highest" is from the *Zhouli*, "Tianguan, Yishi"; the stele's "his skill as high as three generations, his art as broad as ten out of ten" is said by some to draw on these. The lightning striking the flagpole, the whipping of the servant, Xin Yunjing's shout from the wall, Pugu Hong, Baye the Third, Yisi's going to Mingsha, and the treatment are the novel's invention.
