# Volume Three　The Pamir Road

## Chapter 9　The Oxus

> They strike the wood, and the sound of kindness and mercy rings out; they worship toward the east, and hasten on the road to life and glory.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

Pa had said: whatever you see on the road, write it down, and when you come back show it to me.

Jingjing took this as an errand he had been given. Every evening when they made camp, while the others lit the fire and cooked, he squatted by the fire and unrolled a length of the roll of paper from his bundle, and by the firelight wrote down what he had seen that day.

The characters came out crooked. Where there was a character he could not write, he drew a circle.

---

The first day, he wrote:

"Left Lingzhou. Strong wind. The camels go slow. Uncle Duan's scar goes red when the wind blows on it."

The second day, he wrote:

"Crossed the Yellow River. Walked on the ice. Uncle Xu says you can't stop on ice, if you stop you sink. I didn't stop."

The third day, he wrote:

"Uncle Xu talks a lot. Uncle Duan talks a lot too, but when we come to a fork in the road he stops talking. The elder Ahad talks least, but when he opens his mouth Uncle Xu stops talking."

Xu Jing really did talk a lot. From opening his eyes in the morning to closing them at night he talked, and all of it was about the rules of the road. No open fire at night: the light shows a long way, and robbers can see at a glance that there are people here. Don't talk to strangers: when a stranger smiles at you, most likely he wants your things. Don't keep all your money in one place: hide it in different places, a little in your shoe, a little at your waist, a little in your hat.

He had served on the frontier in Hexi in his early years, and had travelled the road west once. Which fork led where, which well was bitter, which post-master liked to skim the fodder—he remembered it all.

Jingjing asked him: Uncle Xu, how did your fingers get frozen?

Then Xu Jing stopped talking.

It was a good while before he said: "That year in Hedong I was manning a beacon tower. There was a heavy snow and the firewood ran out. The beacon mustn't go out, so I shielded it with my hands."

Jingjing asked: "And then?"

"And then the beacon didn't go out," said Xu Jing, and tucked his right hand, the one missing two fingers, into his sleeve. "And two fingers froze off."

That night Jingjing wrote: "Uncle Xu's fingers—he gave them to the beacon."

---

Old Duan rode at the front all the way.

He always walked half a step faster than anyone else, and on horseback was always half a length ahead. At a fork in the road, when Xu Jing said which way, he went over first to look, and when he had looked he turned and beckoned to those behind, and only then did they follow. At the camping place he would walk round it first, seeing where men could hide, where horses could be ridden, where there was water. Only when he had looked would he dismount.

Once he had dismounted, he was another man.

He would sit by the fire with his boots off warming his feet, and as he warmed them talk about things in camp: who snored like a saw, who talked in his sleep and called out his wife's name and was laughed at for half a month after. Xu Jing, listening beside him, said he was making it up. Old Duan said: and that business of you and the beans that year, did I make that up too? Then Xu Jing said nothing.

Jingjing laughed till he had to rub his belly.

Old Duan looked at him sidelong. "Laugh all you like. Paid any empty respects today?"

Jingjing's face went red at once. "No!"

"Good," said Old Duan. "And if you do, don't hold it in. Hold it in and do yourself a mischief, and Uncle Xu will have to boil you ginger tea."

That night Jingjing wrote: "Uncle Duan asked if I'd paid empty respects. I hadn't." When he had written it he thought about it, and blotted the line out.

But Jingjing noticed that every night Old Duan slept closest to him. When Jingjing turned over, Old Duan opened his eyes. When Jingjing got up to relieve himself, Old Duan got up too and stood a little way off with his back to him, and waited till he had finished, and came back with him.

One night Jingjing could not keep it in any longer and asked him: "Uncle Duan, why don't you sleep?"

Old Duan said: "I do sleep."

"You don't. As soon as I move, you wake up."

Old Duan was silent a moment, and said: "I promised your pa. I'd take you there for him, and bring you back."

"You still have to sleep."

"I am asleep," said Old Duan. "I sleep with one eye open. All soldiers do."

Jingjing thought about it and said: "Then that scar on your face—did you get it with your eyes open or shut?"

Old Duan stopped short, then laughed. When he laughed, only half his face moved.

"Shut," he said. "So after that I kept one open."

---

The elder Ahad was the slowest of the four.

He did not ride a horse but an old camel, and the camel went slowly, and he did not hurry it. He was a small man, wrapped in an old sheepskin robe too big for him, its hem hanging down under the camel's belly. In his hand he always held that knotted cord, turning it as he went: one knot, and his lips moved once.

In the morning he rose earliest. While it was still dark he knelt outside the tent, facing east, and said the praises of the first hour. When he had finished his knees were stiff and he could not get up, and had to hold the camel's leg and push himself up bit by bit. The first few days, when Jingjing saw this, he ran over to help him, and the old man waved him off and said there was no need; after Jingjing had helped him a few times, he let himself be helped.

At night he went to sleep latest. He felt each of the four water skins one by one to see whether any leaked, and scrubbed the pot and set it upside down by the fire to dry. His Chinese was very good—forty years—slower and steadier even than Xu Jing's, only there were a few sounds he would never get right, old as he was. He always said *fan*, rice, as *wan*, which means ten thousand. Once Old Duan mimicked him—"four men, four bowls of ten thousand"—and Xu Jing laughed so hard he sprayed water all over the ground. The elder Ahad laughed too, and when he had finished laughing he said: you two share one bowl of ten thousand, and the boy and I will split the rest.

Old Duan had a foul mouth. When he was pleased he swore, when he was displeased he swore, and once his mouth was going he could swear three times in a sentence.

The first few days the elder Ahad listened and said nothing. On the fifth day Old Duan put his foot in a pile of camel dung and let fly with a whole string, and Jingjing, beside him, copied it line by line; when he got to the second line, the elder Ahad put down the cord in his hand.

"Seventh Son Duan," he said.

His voice was not loud, but Old Duan shut his mouth at once.

"Curse your camel dung," said the elder, "and the boy is listening."

Old Duan looked at Jingjing. Jingjing's mouth was open, the third line not yet out of it. Old Duan's face went red. He mumbled "The elder's right," and led his horse up to the front.

After that, Old Duan would often stop dead halfway through a curse—"You dog… you dog-thing," "Your mother… your mother's camel." Jingjing, hearing it, would put his hand over his mouth and laugh. The elder Ahad pretended not to hear.

Xu Jing got it once too.

That was in an abandoned post-station west of Liangzhou. There was nobody in the station, but the fodder store at the back still had half a roomful of grain. Xu Jing went in and had a look round, and came out with a sack in each hand, very pleased, saying the camels would have something good to eat tonight.

The elder Ahad looked at the two sacks and asked: "Whose?"

"The government's," said Xu Jing. "Everyone's run off. It'll only rot."

The elder said nothing more. He went into the fodder store, put a small string of coins on the stove, came out again, took up his cord, and went on turning it.

Xu Jing looked at the string of coins on the stove, and his pleasure went out of his face. "Elder," he said, "leave money there, and the first traveller who comes in will pocket it."

"That's his business," said the elder.

Xu Jing opened his mouth and nothing came out. That night as he fed the camels he did not speak a single rule.

---

On either side of the Hexi corridor: on one side the Qilian mountains, snow on their peaks; on the other the gravel desert, with nothing on it but stones and wind. The road ran between, like a line, drawn out westward with no end in sight.

Of the post-stations along the way, some were still open and some already empty. In the open ones, when the post-master saw the bronze tally of the Headquarters of the Military Governor of Shuofang in Old Duan's hand, his face changed, and he hurried to clear a room, boil water, cook. In the empty ones the gate stood open, the stove was cold, there was horse dung on the floor and the marks of sword cuts on the walls.

Xu Jing said the troops of Hexi had all been sent east to fight, and there was no one to look after the stations.

Jingjing asked: And the robbers?

Xu Jing said: The robbers weren't sent anywhere.

The nights at the stations were long. When Old Duan and Xu Jing were asleep and Jingjing could not sleep, he would creep over to the elder Ahad's felt and listen to him talk.

The elder said he was born beside the two rivers, in a village on the upper Tigris. It was not a big village, a few dozen households, with a small church that had only one priest. At seven he was lighting the lamps in the church: he ran there before dawn and lit them one by one, and when he had finished he knelt at the very back and listened to the praises of the morning hour. The priest saw how faithfully he came and taught him his letters. Syriac letters, one by one, written on a board smeared with wax; when it was full he scraped it off with his fingernail and wrote again.

"About like you now," said the elder. "Crooked, and the ones you don't know, left blank."

Jingjing asked: "And your pa and ma? Did they believe too?"

"They believed," said the elder. "My mother wanted me to stay in the village, marry, farm, take over my father's boat. My father fished on the river."

"Then why didn't you stay?"

The elder thought about it. "That year someone from the Patriarchate came to the village and said that far, far to the east there was a great kingdom, where the people had never yet heard the Way of the Luminous Lord. When I heard it, I didn't sleep all night."

He said he went to the Patriarchate at twenty and studied the scriptures there three years. At twenty-three he went east with a party of men, along this same road, only the other way. They travelled more than a year and reached Chang'an. In those days the one who had charge of the monastery in Chang'an was the Great Virtue Jilie; there were more people in the monastery then than now, and more incense burned.

"In the ninth year of Zhenguan, the Great Virtue Alopen came by this road too," said the elder. "With the scriptures, with the images, all the way to Chang'an. In those days your pa's pa's pa had not been born."

Jingjing counted on his fingers, could not make it come out, and gave up.

"And then? Were you in Chang'an the whole time?"

"No," said the elder. "I went to Luoyang, to Liangzhou, to Shazhou. Wherever our people were and needed someone to preach, when the letter came from the Patriarchate, I went. Then I came to Lingzhou. The monastery at Lingzhou is small; the roof leaks every year and there's no money to mend it. I've lived there more than ten years, and I didn't go on."

"Why didn't you go on?"

The elder did not answer at once. The wind went past outside the tent and lifted a corner of the felt and let it fall again.

"I got old," he said. "I couldn't walk any more."

Jingjing thought that was not right. He was walking now, plainly. But he did not say so; the elder had already closed his eyes.

---

They met the robbers beyond the Jade Gate.

That evening, just after they had crossed a dry riverbed, twenty-odd horsemen came out from behind a sand ridge and charged at them, shooting. Xu Jing shouted "The mound!" and Old Duan seized the camels' halters and hustled them toward an earth mound by the roadside, to shelter behind it. Jingjing was tucked under Xu Jing's arm and carried along at a great pace, his face pressed into Xu Jing's sheepskin coat, smelling mutton fat and sweat.

They hid behind the mound the whole night.

Old Duan and Xu Jing kept watch, one at the front and one at the back. The band tried twice to work round the side, and both times Xu Jing's arrows drove them back. Once a man crept right up to the foot of the mound; Jingjing heard a *shk* from Old Duan's side, then a grunt, then no more sound. When Old Duan came back his hands were covered in blood. He rubbed them in the sand and went back to his watch.

The elder Ahad sat beside Jingjing with a roll of scripture held against his chest, and prayed all night. He prayed very low, like someone breathing in your ear. Once an arrow struck the top of the mound and earth came pattering down over the elder's head and face. He did not stop; he only held the scripture tighter, and with his other hand pressed Jingjing's head down on his own knees.

Near dawn the robbers went away.

Jingjing had not slept all night. When it was light he saw that a strip of cloth was wound round Old Duan's arm, with blood seeping through it.

He asked: "Uncle Duan, were you hit?"

Old Duan glanced down and said: "Just a graze."

That night Jingjing wrote: "Uncle Duan was hit by an arrow. He said it was a graze. I saw the arrowhead was still inside. Uncle Xu dug it out for him. He didn't make a sound."

When he had written that he added after it: "I didn't make a sound either. Last night I was so scared I wanted to wet myself. I didn't."

He rolled up the paper and saw the elder Ahad sitting on the other side of the fire, brushing the earth off that roll of scripture bit by bit.

"Elder," he asked, "is that scripture any use?"

The elder Ahad thought about it and said: "It won't stop an arrow."

"Then why did you hold it all night?"

The elder looked down at the scripture, looked a while, as though he was thinking about it himself.

"Holding it," he said, "I'm not so afraid."

Jingjing unrolled the paper again and added a line: "The elder Ahad says if you hold the scripture, you're not so afraid."

---

The next day, beside a stream of bitter water, they met a Hu herding sheep.

The elder Ahad rode over on his camel and talked with the man a long time, in a foreign tongue. Jingjing could not follow all of it, only that the two of them went back and forth bargaining, and at the end the man laughed and the elder laughed too. The elder took a small cloth bundle from inside his robe and undid it; there were a few pieces of broken silver in it. He took out two and put them in the man's hand.

He came back leading a sheep.

That day they made camp early. The elder killed the sheep with his own hands, skinned it and boned it, quicker with his hands than even Xu Jing. From the saddlebag on his camel he dug out a small clay jar with salt in it, and a little paper packet with a pinch of dried leaves, green going black. He crushed the leaves and scattered them into the pot.

When the broth came to the boil, a smell drifted out a long way. Old Duan sat by the fire, his nose twitching, and said: "That's it. That's the smell. That bowl in Lingzhou."

The elder ladled out the first bowl, and did not give it to Jingjing; he carried it to Old Duan. The second bowl he carried to Xu Jing.

"Last night," said the elder, "you two—one at the front, one at the back—kept watch all night."

Having said that one thing he said no more, and went back to ladle out Jingjing's broth.

Old Duan held his bowl and looked into the broth, and for a long time did not drink. Then he took a great gulp, burned himself and sucked in air, and said: "Elder, this bowl's better than the wine Lord Guo gives."

Xu Jing said: "When did you ever drink wine Lord Guo gave?"

Old Duan glared at him and drank again.

Jingjing, holding his bowl, asked the elder what the leaves were. The elder said it was mint, bought in the foreign market at Lingzhou. It was not the same kind that grew by the river at his old home, but dried, the taste was much the same.

"So the broth at your home tastes like this?"

The elder took a spoonful of broth, blew on it, drank it, and after a while said: "Not quite."

---

In the fourth month they reached Xizhou.

Xizhou was once the kingdom of Gaochang—Turfan, as some call it now. The city stood in an oasis with gravel desert all round, yet inside it there were water channels everywhere, and grape trellises, and white poplars. The walls were of rammed yellow earth, very thick and very high. There were Han people in the city and Hu people, people who spoke Chinese and people who spoke foreign tongues and people who spoke half one and half the other. The market sold raisins, flatbread, mutton, felt carpets—and there was a man selling books.

Jingjing stopped in front of the bookseller's stall. On it lay a few rolls of scripture written in Syriac letters.

He stood there looking a long time.

The bookseller was an old Hu, and seeing him so absorbed, asked him in a foreign tongue: You can read this writing?

Jingjing said: A little.

The old man said: These are from the church at the north of the city. The elder of that church died last year and there's no one to look after it; his disciple brought out the scriptures to sell.

Jingjing asked: Is the church still there?

The old man said: The church is still there. The people are gone.

At some point the elder Ahad had come to stand behind him. He said nothing, but took up the rolls on the stall one by one, opened them and looked, and put them down. When he came to the last roll he looked at it a long time, and then asked the old man how much.

The old man named a price. The elder did not bargain, and undid the little cloth bundle again. There were fewer pieces of broken silver in it than the day before.

That afternoon the elder went alone to the north of the city. Jingjing wanted to go with him, and the elder said there was no need. He came back after dark, covered in dust, with burrs on his knees. He wrapped the roll of scripture he had bought in oilcloth and gave it to Jingjing.

"You keep this," he said. "On your way back, when you pass through here, put it back in that church."

"You won't put it back?"

"I may not come this way again," said the elder.

Jingjing did not quite understand that. He pushed the scripture into his bundle, with Pa's letter.

That night Jingjing wrote: "There is a church in the north of Xizhou. Like ours, a cross carved on the gate. The people are gone. The elder bought back a roll of scripture and told me to put it back on my way home."

He thought, and wrote: "When I come back, I'll go and look again."

---

Out of Xizhou, westward, came Yanqi, Qiuci, Kashgar.

That stretch was long, and one day much like the next. Only a few things were written down on Jingjing's paper.

"Very hot by day. Very cold at night."

"A camel died. Uncle Xu killed it. We roasted the meat and ate it for five days. I couldn't eat it. Uncle Duan said, if you can't you must, if you don't the next one to die is you. I ate it."

"The elder Ahad had a fever and talked nonsense. He talked about the two rivers. When he woke up he saw me and said, child, I dreamed of the Tigris. I gave him water. He drank three mouthfuls and went back to sleep."

"In the city of Qiuci there was a Hu merchant come from the west. The elder talked with him all evening, asking about the two rivers. When he came back the elder was very happy, and also seemed not very happy."

Past Kashgar were the Pamirs.

The Pamirs are the highest mountains under heaven. They travelled more than half a month. There were no trees on the mountains, only stone and snow; it was the fifth month and the snow in the passes still had not melted. The road was very narrow, a cliff on one side and a deep gorge on the other, whose bottom could not be seen. The wind was strong enough to blow a man away. Jingjing rode on the camel with a rope round his waist, tied to the pack frame. He did not dare look down. He dared look only at Old Duan's back ahead.

Old Duan's back was always ahead. Slow, but always there.

The elder Ahad was behind him. Jingjing did not dare turn his head, but he could hear. The elder was breathing very hard: breathing a while, stopping a while, and when he stopped he turned a knot.

One day Xu Jing said, panting, that this was the first time he had ever been so high. On the road west, he had only ever been as far as Kashgar.

That night they slept under a rock face out of the wind. It was too cold to sleep; the four of them huddled together, with the camels lying outside to keep off the wind. Jingjing curled up in the elder Ahad's arms. Inside his sheepskin robe there was a smell of mutton fat and mint.

"Elder," Jingjing whispered, "do you miss home?"

"No," said the elder.

So Jingjing did not ask any more.

A long time later, so long that Jingjing thought he was asleep, the elder suddenly spoke again.

"There was a fig tree outside our door," he said. "Every autumn my mother picked the fruit and dried it on the roof, and when it was dry she strung it in strings and hung it from the beam, and we ate it all winter. The year I left, she wrapped me a bundle of them to eat on the road. I couldn't bear to eat them. When I got to Ctesiphon and opened it, they had all gone mouldy."

He stopped.

"Forty years," he said. "I don't know if the tree is still there."

Jingjing said nothing. He put out his hand and found the elder's hand. It was very cold, the knuckles thick as knots in a tree, and in the palm it held the knotted cord.

---

Past the highest pass the road began to go down.

They went down for two days, and the valley widened, and at the bottom of the valley there was a river. The water was a bluish white, fast and cold, breaking white over the stones. There was grass by the river, and a few little houses of piled stone, with smoke rising from their roofs.

The elder Ahad got down from his camel by the river and beckoned Jingjing over.

"This is the head of the Wuhu," he said. "The Oxus. Follow it down and you come to Tokharistan. Your pa's home."

The Wuhu is what the Tang called the Oxus.

Jingjing got down from his camel and, copying the elder, squatted by the river and put his hand into the water. It was as cold as a knife, and stabbed him so that he snatched his hand back at once. The elder watched him and laughed.

He remembered Pa saying he was a man of Tokharistan, born in the city of Balkh, and that outside Balkh there was a river too, and when Pa was little, every spring, he and Uncle had crossed it on the ice.

He did not know whether it was this river Pa meant.

That night he wrote: "Saw the Oxus. When Pa was little, maybe he put his hand in this river too. The water is very cold. The elder says the river by his door is wider than this, and the water is yellow, and not cold."

---

Note: In this chapter the outward road goes by Hexi, Xizhou, Yanqi, Qiuci and Kashgar, crosses the Pamirs from Kashgar, and comes down by way of Tashkurgan and Wakhan to Tokharistan. The Wuhu is the Oxus (Amu Darya), in Tang sources also called the Fuchu; the river in the valley in Wakhan is its upper course. Balkh lies south of the Oxus, and coming down west from the Pamirs to Balkh there is no need to cross it. Jilie was a Great Virtue of the Luminous Religion in Chang'an in the early Kaiyuan years; his name appears on the stele and in the *Cefu Yuangui*.
