# Appendix 1　The Stele, I: An Overview

The stone that keeps appearing in this book is known in full as *The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom* (大秦景教流行中國碑). Today it stands in the second gallery of the Forest of Stelae Museum in Xi'an. Yisi, Jingjing, Hnanisho and Lü Xiuyan, who appear in the novel, are all named on it. It is dug up in the Prologue and goes into the ground in Chapter 75, and most of what happens in the decades between grows out of what is written on it. This appendix gives the basic facts about the stele and its fate. The full text and an English translation are in Appendix 2; the great benefactor Yisi is discussed in Appendix 3; the other people on the stele in Appendix 4; and the honours Alopen received on entering Chang'an in Appendix 5.

## 1. The stele in brief

The stele was raised on the seventh day of the first month of the second year of Jianzhong in the reign of Emperor Dezong of the Tang, that is, in 781. It is made of black limestone, with a crown of coiled dragons and a tortoise base, and stands a little over 350 centimetres high, about a metre wide and more than 20 centimetres thick. Its head bears the nine large characters of the title in regular script, in three columns of three; in the pointed peak above them is carved a cross standing on a lotus, surrounded by cloud patterns.

The inscription was composed by Jingjing, a monk of the Daqin monastery, and written out for carving by Lü Xiuyan, Gentleman for Court Discussion and former Administrator of Granaries in Taizhou. It runs to thirty-two columns of regular script, sixty-two characters to a full column, more than seventeen hundred characters in all. After the main text comes a hymn in verse, and after the hymn the date of erection and a long list of monks' names. At the foot of the stele and down both sides there is also an inscription in Syriac, recording the year of erection (the year 1092 of the Greeks, that is, AD 781), the patriarch then in office, and the Syriac names and ranks of some seventy clergy, many of them with their Chinese and Syriac names side by side.

The inscription falls broadly into three parts. First it sets out the teaching of the Luminous Religion, from the creation and the fall of man to the birth and ascension of the Messiah. Next it tells the history of the religion's first hundred and forty-odd years in the Tang, from Alopen's arrival in Chang'an in the ninth year of Zhenguan (635), through the reigns of Taizong, Gaozong, Xuanzong, Suzong and Daizong, down to the Jianzhong years of Dezong. Last, a long passage praises the great benefactor Yisi: it says that he followed Guo Ziyi in putting down the rebellion, "serving the Duke as claws and teeth, and the army as eyes and ears," and that he spent his wealth on repairing monasteries, and every year gathered the monks of four monasteries, so that "the hungry came and were fed, the cold came and were clothed, the sick were healed and raised up, the dead were buried and laid to rest."

## 2. The stele and the people of this book

The Syriac on the stele left a second name for several of the novel's people.

Yisi's Syriac name is generally read by scholars as Yazdbōzīd. The stele says that he was a priest and chorepiscopus of the capital, son of the late priest Mīlīs, and came from the city of Balkh in Tokharistan—the Balkh of northern Afghanistan today. Yisi's background in the novel, and the name of his father Milis, come from here.

Jingjing's Syriac name was Adam. The stele calls him priest, chorepiscopus, and "papash of Sinistan," father of the church of China. He is the author of the inscription.

The patriarch named in the Syriac is Ḥnanīšōʿ II, and the "Ningshu" (宁恕) in the last line of the Chinese inscription—"at that time the Lord of the Law, the monk Ningshu, oversaw the Luminous congregations of the East"—refers to him as well; the novel's Patriarch Hnanisho is taken from here. In fact he had died the year before the stele was raised, and Timothy I had already succeeded him, but the news had not yet travelled from Baghdad to Chang'an. So one stele carries the name of a patriarch who was no longer living. Chapter 49 of the novel is about exactly this.

Among the Chinese names on the stele there is also a "monk Jingfu." Jingfu of the novel's third generation, Jingjing's disciple, bears the same name.

## 3. The fate of the stele

The stele was probably buried in the Huichang years, when the foreign religions were proscribed, though the exact year can no longer be known. Around the third year of Tianqi in the Ming (1623; some say the fifth year, 1625) it was dug up in the western outskirts of Xi'an Prefecture; how it was found is told in the Prologue; for how Li Zhizao and the Jesuits studied it, see the note to the Prologue and Appendix 11, *Li Zhizao's "After Reading the Luminous Stele": Translation and Related Studies*. After it was unearthed it was moved into the nearby Chongren Temple and a pavilion built to protect it. In the ninth year of Xianfeng in the Qing (1859), Han Taihua of Hangzhou visited, found the inscription still intact, paid for a new pavilion, and had a colophon cut on the side of the stele. In the thirty-third year of Guangxu (1907), the Dane Frits Holm came to Xi'an meaning to buy the stele and ship it to Europe. He did not succeed, and carried off only a replica he had had carved. In the same year the Shaanxi provincial authorities moved the original into the Forest of Stelae in Xi'an, where it is kept to this day.
