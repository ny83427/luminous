# Volume Four　Xiangji Temple

## Chapter 14　The Qing Canal

> Their darkness piled up until they lost the road, and they wandered so long that they could not turn back.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

The spring of the second year of Zhide was slow in coming.

In the first month An Lushan died, killed by his son An Qingxu. When the news reached the camp, someone burned a few joints of bamboo that went off crack-crack-crack, and was cursed by a commandant: the rebels weren't wiped out yet, what was he setting off firecrackers for? The man who had burned the bamboo took the cursing without minding, and squatted behind the tents chuckling.

At the end of the first month Guo Ziyi led his troops out from Luojiao, took Fengyi, then took Hedong. Hedong lay between the two capitals; with it in hand, Chang'an and Luoyang were half cut apart. Everyone in camp said that this year they ought to be going back to Chang'an.

In the second month Guo Ziyi's son Guo Gan crossed the river to attack Tong Pass, with the field commander Li Shaoguang and the general Wang Zuo. They took Tong Pass and could not hold it. An Shouzhong and Li Guiren brought troops from Chang'an to relieve it; the two sides fought hard for two days, and the Tang army was badly beaten. Guo Gan, Li Shaoguang and Wang Zuo did not come back. Pugu Huai'en swam the Wei clinging to his horse's neck and escaped back to Hedong. Of the men he had led out, fewer than half came back.

Yisi had not gone on that campaign. He was in the city of Hedong, in charge of a few pack horses and a cart of fodder.

The news came at night. Early next morning Guo Ziyi took his seat in the command tent as usual, called the roll as usual, looked over the grain and fodder figures the camps sent in as usual. What deserved cursing he cursed; what deserved approving he approved. At noon he ate—a bowl of millet gruel, a dish of pickles, a steamed bun—and when he had eaten he went to see the wounded.

The common soldiers knew only that Tong Pass had been lost and many men had died. That Lord Guo's son had been among them—people passed this round, passed it round for two days, saw that over at the command tent things were as usual, and mostly stopped passing it round.

The few who had served him longest knew. A bodyguard officer saw him stand outside the tent a long time one night, through two changes of the watch. Hun Shizhi went into the tent to report and, coming out, squatted at the entrance a while before he went. Once Yisi took in a dispatch, and Guo Ziyi was holding a document; Yisi gave his report, and as he withdrew, the document was still open at the same page.

No one said anything.

---

In the fourth month Guo Ziyi received an edict to go to Fengxiang. North of Sanyuan, Li Guiren came with five thousand armoured horsemen to cut him off.

Guo Ziyi had five of his generals—Pugu Huai'en, Hun Shizhi and the others—lie in ambush on either side of the Liuyun Bridge over the White Canal. Li Guiren's horsemen came after them, came as far as the bridgehead; the ambush rose, the two sides closed, and the rebels turned and ran, leaving a good many horses along the way.

That battle was won clean. The camp talked of it for days, saying Li Guiren had run so hard he'd dropped his whip. Most of those who said so had not seen the whip, but everyone liked to hear it.

Later Guo Ziyi joined forces with Wang Sili at the West Wei Bridge and moved up to camp west of the Jue River. On the rebel side, An Shouzhong and Li Guiren brought out the troops from Chang'an and camped at the Qing Canal west of the city.

The two sides camped with a stretch of wheatfield between them, and did not move.

The wheat was nearly yellow. The ears hung heavy, and when the wind blew they bowed down field after field. Neither side dared go out and cut it.

---

For the first two days no one in the tent said anything.

On the third, some could not sit still.

Zheng Lin, field commander of the vanguard, was a little past thirty and had a big voice; in the chase at Sanyuan his men had run out in front. He said in the tent: "Stand at the camp gate on a clear day and you can see the city towers. How many men have the rebels got? Li Guiren ran like that at Sanyuan—the men under him lost their nerve long ago."

Guo Ziyi said: "General Wang's camp isn't fully set up yet."

Wang Sili's troops had come from inside the passes and had only just arrived; their palisade was not finished. Wang Sili sat to one side and did not take it up either.

On the fourth day and the fifth, more and more asked to fight. Guo Ziyi would not allow it, and gave no long reasons; he only told the scouting commissioner Shi Jingzhong to send more men out to look.

Shi Jingzhong divided the scouts into four parties. Yisi was one of the men in attendance before Guo Ziyi's tent, and Guo Ziyi had him go with the eastern party, with an old scout named Feng. Old Feng was past forty, had watched the roads of Shuofang for twenty years, spoke little and had a sharp eye; Yisi learned a good deal following him.

Every day before light the two of them left camp, worked round to the east of the rebel camp, found somewhere they could see men from without being easily seen, and lay on their bellies watching. They watched the cooking smoke, watched the horses, watched when the sentries changed. By the time they had finished, it was full day; they came back and reported to Shi Jingzhong, and Shi Jingzhong reported to the command tent.

One day among Yisi's points was this: when the sentries on the east changed, two men had squatted down to relieve themselves, squatted a long time, and no one had stopped them.

Shi Jingzhong reported it as given. Someone in the tent laughed out loud.

Zheng Lin said: "Master Yi has a fine eye. Tomorrow let him look whether what they leave is runny or firm, and then we'll know if there's enough grain in the rebel camp."

Everyone in the tent laughed. Guo Ziyi did not laugh and did not say anything; he told Shi Jingzhong to go on with his report.

Afterwards this reached Yisi's ears. When he came back the next day, he did not report men relieving themselves any more.

Old Feng heard of it, and said one thing from the saddle: "What should be reported, you still report. If a sentry can squat that long with no one minding him, it means the man in charge of the watch isn't there."

Yisi thought about it, and felt Old Feng was right. But the next day he still did not report it.

---

On the seventh day, just as the sky was paling, the rebel camp moved.

Yisi and Old Feng were lying behind a graveyard. All at once the rebel camp was in confusion: tents came down one after another, carts were harnessed one after another, soldiers shouldered their loads and went off in a disorderly crowd to the northeast, towards the city. Not one thread of cooking smoke was left.

"They're off," said Old Feng. "Go back and report."

Yisi did not move. He was looking at the carts.

"The carts are light," he said.

Old Feng looked too. After a while he grunted.

The carts were indeed light. A loaded cart sinks its wheels into the earth, and the horse pulling it keeps its head low and strains step by step. The horses pulling these carts went along easily, and the wheels bounced on the dirt road, as if there were nothing in the beds.

The two of them waited until the rebel rearguard was far off, crept out from behind the graveyard and over to the camp ground. The hoofprints were in confusion; most went northeast, but there was a great swathe of them that went a little way and then suddenly turned, turned into a hollow to the east. In front of the hollow a bank of earth stood in the way, and what was beyond it could not be seen.

"Let's go back and report," said Old Feng.

"Let's see it clearly first," said Yisi. "It might just be horses put out to graze."

Old Feng gave him a look and did not argue. The two of them mounted and rode towards the bank.

They had gone less than half a *li* when drums sounded behind them.

They were Tang drums.

---

That morning, in the command tent, it went like this.

Just after daybreak the western party of scouts came back first and said the rebels had struck camp and were going towards the city. Not long after, the northern party came back too and said the same. The third party came back, and it was the same.

Zheng Lin was already armoured and standing at the tent entrance. The officers in the tent said it one after another: the rebels had gone, go after them.

Guo Ziyi asked Shi Jingzhong: "And the eastern party?"

"Not back yet."

Guo Ziyi said nothing. He waited a while.

Someone ran in to report that a troop of the vanguard's horsemen had already left the camp, crossed the wooden bridge over the canal and gone after them—to bite the rebels' tail, they said.

Zheng Lin reddened and said he would go and call them back.

Someone else ran in to report that the troop of horsemen was already tangled up with the rebel rearguard.

If they sounded the recall now, the troop would turn and run, the rebel rearguard would wheel and chase them, and the whole troop would be lost over there. If they did not, they would have to send men to support it; send one troop and one troop wouldn't be enough, they'd have to send a battalion; and once a battalion was sent, it was not much different from throwing in the whole army.

"Pursue," said Guo Ziyi.

Guo Ziyi glanced at Wang Sili.

Wang Sili said: "I'll take the rear."

That was when the drums began.

---

Yisi wheeled his horse and galloped back. Old Feng followed.

Troop after troop of horsemen poured out of the camp gate and over the several wooden bridges across the Qing Canal in pursuit, with the infantry behind them, carrying banners, shouting their chants. Yisi and Old Feng dared not push into the ranks, and rode round the outer edge, a great circle, before they found the great banner of the centre.

The centre was already moving forward too. He found Guo Ziyi and pushed through to his horse's head.

"The carts are empty," he said. "The hoofprints turned east. There are men in the hollow to the east."

Guo Ziyi looked at him, for a moment. Then he turned his head and looked ahead. Ahead was all Tang backs, banner pressed against banner, surging forward.

"Order to the right wing," Guo Ziyi said to the man beside him. "Slow down, and keep watch to the east."

The messenger rode off. The right wing was going hard, its banners crowded one against the next; by the time the order reached them they were several *li* on.

"You stay with the centre," Guo Ziyi said to Yisi.

---

In the vanguard there was a squad leader named Dou who afterwards told people about that day.

He said they had chased for five or six *li* and caught up with a few carts the rebels had left behind, and sure enough there was nothing much on them—a few sacks of bran, a few bundles of rotten straw matting. Some men were still cursing, saying the rebels were that poor.

Then from the east a banner came up.

First one, then a string of them. Horses rose out of the hollow one by one, and as soon as they rose they galloped, galloped into a line, a very, very long line, no end to it in sight. The head of the line was pointed at their waist, and drove straight in.

He swung his men round to the east to meet it. They had not held it long when someone behind them shouted, shouted until his voice cracked. He looked back, and the tail of that line had somehow swung round in a great circle and was already behind them.

"The two ends met," said Squad Leader Dou, "and the men were like grain in a millstone."

He said that of the fifty men in his squad, when the roll was called afterwards, eleven answered.

---

In the centre, the drum stopped first.

The drummer took an arrow and pitched off the drum cart, the drumstick still clenched in his hand. The banner tilted, was set up again, and not long after it was set up it fell again.

Pugu Huai'en charged east with his horsemen twice. The first time he broke in and was squeezed out again. The second time he broke in, and when he came out he had half his men. He was going to charge a third time, and the men around him caught his horse by the bit.

The administrative assistant Han Ye had been with the baggage. One of the guards said afterwards that he saw his horse bolt and run north, into the rebel horsemen, and that he was not seen again. The army supervisor Sun Zhigu was also lost in that rout. Whether they were dead or alive, no one at the time knew.

Guo Ziyi's guards closed round him, kept him in the middle, and fell back south.

Yisi was swept along among the guards, falling back with them. He had a mace and a shield, but among so many men he could not use them, and could not see where to use them. All he could see was the back of the guard in front of him, half an arrow shaft stuck in it, bobbing with every jolt.

When they had fallen back to the edge of the Qing Canal, a young soldier was sitting in the ditch with an arrow in his leg, both arms round his leg, not crying out, only watching the men running past him.

Yisi leaned down, grabbed him by the collar, hauled him up onto the horse and laid him across the front of the saddle. Over the bridge there was a baggage cart fleeing back along the roadside, already packed with several wounded men, and he pushed the soldier up onto it.

The soldier looked at him from the cart and asked: "You a *hu*?"

"Yes."

The cart was pulled away.

---

In that battle all the army's stores and weapons were lost north of the Qing Canal. The administrative assistant and the army supervisor had both been taken by the rebels. How many men had died, no one at the time could count.

Guo Ziyi gathered the broken troops and fell back west. Wang Sili's rearguard covered the retreat, falling back a stage, making a stand, falling back again; it took two days to get back to Wugong.

The day they reached Wugong it rained. Not heavily—fine and cool, all day long. The city could not hold so many men, so the camp was set up on the slopes outside, where the grass was deep, and under the feet of tens of thousands it all turned to mud. The wounded lay in the tents, and there were not enough tents, so some lay under carts. The cooks set up their pots to boil gruel, but the firewood was wet, the smoke thick and the fire small, and the pots would not come to the boil for hours.

The officers of each camp went through their rosters under the cart awnings. A name was called, and someone answered; a name was called and no one answered, and they stopped a moment and called the next. A clerk beside them wrote, dipping his brush once for each name. When it was done they reported to the centre, and the man who reported, having given the numbers, just stood there and said nothing else.

Guo Ziyi went from tent to tent seeing the wounded. By dark he had not finished.

Yisi helped carry the wounded for most of the day. When it got dark he sat on the shaft of a cart, pulled off his boots and poured the water out. The water that came out was red. He looked at it a while before he remembered it was other men's blood, from the men he had carried that afternoon.

If Old Duan had been there, about now he would have handed him half a dry cake.

Old Duan was not there. Old Duan was in the west, and Jingjing was in the west too. Yisi did not know how far they had got.

He put his boots back on.

Old Feng had come back as well, with a sword cut in his leg, lying on a cart further back. Yisi went to see him; he was still awake.

"The eastern party," said Old Feng, "one of us two should have gone back first."

Yisi said: "Yes."

---

That night Guo Ziyi called his officers together in the command tent.

Two lamps were lit in the tent. Wang Sili, Pugu Huai'en, Hun Shizhi, Zheng Lin, Shi Jingzhong and the field commanders of several camps had all come. Zheng Lin's left arm hung in a cloth sling with blood seeping through it. Hun Shizhi had lost his helmet and had a strip of dark cloth wound round his head. Pugu Huai'en sat with his two hands on his knees and did not say a word.

At the head of the tent, on the right, the folding stool where the administrative assistant Han Ye usually sat was empty. No one went to sit on it. A young clerk of Han Ye's, a little past twenty, had spread a mat beside the stool with paper and brush on his knees.

Yisi, as one of the men in attendance before Guo Ziyi's tent, was in the tent that night too. He stood by the entrance, leaning on the tent pole, and did not sit.

Guo Ziyi sat at the head. He had taken off his armour and wore only an old robe, and the hem of the robe was muddy too.

"Tonight we don't apportion blame," said Guo Ziyi. "The blame I'll go to Fengxiang tomorrow and take. Tonight we speak of one thing only: how we lost. From the beginning."

The tent was silent a while.

Shi Jingzhong spoke first. He said that of the four parties of scouts, three had come back around the hour of *mao*, and all had reported that the rebels were withdrawing. The eastern party had not come back until the hour of *chen*.

"The eastern party," said Guo Ziyi. "Yisi, where were you two then?"

Yisi straightened up from the tent pole.

"Beyond the bank," he said. "We saw the carts were light and the hoofprints turned east, and wanted to see clearly."

"Did you see clearly?"

"No. The drums sounded, and we came back."

Pugu Huai'en raised his eyes and looked at him.

"You saw the carts were empty," said Pugu Huai'en, "and didn't come back and report first. What did you go to look at?"

He said it very slowly, and not loudly, but everyone in the tent heard.

"I wanted to see clearly," said Yisi.

"By the time you've seen clearly," said Pugu Huai'en, "the battle's over."

Yisi said: "Yes."

He said nothing else.

Shi Jingzhong said from beside him: "Two men to a party, and both of them went to look—that was my mistake in how I sent them. It should have been fixed: when one sees something, he rides back, and the other stays and keeps watching."

Guo Ziyi nodded, and glanced at the clerk. The clerk bent his head and wrote.

---

Zheng Lin stood up. His arm was in its sling, and he stood a little crooked.

"It was my men in the vanguard who went out first," he said. "The rebels left a few carts behind, and my men's eyes went red; they wanted to loot them. I couldn't stop them."

He paused.

"And I didn't want to stop them."

No one in the tent said anything.

Hun Shizhi said from the lower seats: "If the rebels run and we don't go after them, what are we for? I'd have gone after them too."

"Going after them wasn't wrong," said Wang Sili.

Wang Sili did not usually say much; this time he spoke at length. He said going after them was not wrong; what was wrong was that the whole army went after them. The vanguard went out in pursuit, the centre followed, the two wings surged forward with it, the rear kept close behind the centre, and he himself, leading the rear, had closed up too. The whole formation stretched forward, thin and long, and not one man was left on either side to watch.

"When the rebels came out of the east," said Wang Sili, "on our east, there was nobody."

Hun Shizhi did not say anything more.

---

After a while Pugu Huai'en spoke.

"That formation," he said, "I saw it when I was little, on the steppe."

Everyone in the tent looked at him.

He said it was the old way of fighting on the steppe. First you withdraw, and what withdraws is the empty carts, the old and weak, the things that don't matter, so it looks to you as if they've really run. The real good horsemen hide off to one side. You chase, you chase in under its belly, its head comes out from one side and bites your waist; you turn round to fight its head, its tail swings round behind you. Head and tail meet, and you're in the middle.

"The Türks fight this way, the Khitan and the Xi fight this way too," said Pugu Huai'en. "An Lushan kept all those *hu* horsemen in Fanyang for so many years; they all know this trick."

He paused.

"At Tong Pass that time, it was these same two men too."

The tent grew quieter still. At Tong Pass that time, Lord Guo's son had not come back. Pugu Huai'en himself had swum the Wei clinging to his horse's neck. Everyone in the tent knew it.

Nothing moved in Guo Ziyi's face. He said: "Go on."

"That's all," said Pugu Huai'en.

Hun Shizhi said: "A month ago at the Liuyun Bridge, it was us ambushing them."

"Li Guiren took a beating at Sanyuan," said Pugu Huai'en, "and went back and chewed on it for a month. And what did we do this month?"

No one answered.

The field commander of one of the camps said in a low voice that the rebels' nine thousand horse had been used all together in one place. Our horsemen were scattered through the camps, some with the vanguard, some with the wings, some with the centre, and at that moment all of them were chasing; they couldn't be pulled together all at once. Even if the wings had kept men back, they might not have held.

"The Uighur troops," said Guo Ziyi, "the court is pressing for them."

He said only that, and did not go on.

---

"Then what do we do next time," said Guo Ziyi. "Point by point."

Shi Jingzhong said: "Scouts go in pairs. When one sees something, he rides back first to report; the other stays and keeps watching."

The clerk wrote it.

Wang Sili said: "If the rebels withdraw, first check the wheel ruts, check the horse tracks. Carts light or heavy, which way the hoofprints go—check them, then pursue."

The clerk wrote it.

Pugu Huai'en said: "The wings keep horsemen back. They are not allowed to join the pursuit."

The clerk wrote it.

Zheng Lin had been standing all along. Now he said: "When the vanguard meets the rebels, it holds them, that's all. No pursuit. Anyone who pursues—"

He stopped there.

"Anyone who pursues, cut him down," said Pugu Huai'en.

The clerk's brush paused, and he looked up at Guo Ziyi. Guo Ziyi said nothing. The clerk bent his head and wrote that too.

When he had finished, the clerk read it all back from the beginning. When he came to the last point, his voice dropped a little.

Guo Ziyi heard it out, and said: "These points, we keep."

He said too: "Tomorrow I go to Fengxiang. General Wang, look after things in camp for now."

Wang Sili stood and acknowledged it.

The generals went out of the tent one by one. Hun Shizhi, reaching the entrance, looked back once at the empty folding stool before he lifted the curtain and went out.

---

Yisi was about to go too.

"Yisi," said Guo Ziyi. "Stay a moment."

Yisi stopped.

There were only the two of them left in the tent, and the clerk. The clerk was gathering up his paper and brush, and Guo Ziyi told him he should go and rest too. The clerk made his bow and went out with his papers in his arms.

Guo Ziyi did not tell Yisi to sit, and Yisi did not sit. Outside the rain was still falling, pattering on the roof of the tent.

"And Old Feng?" asked Guo Ziyi.

"A sword cut in the leg. He's on a cart further back. He's awake."

Guo Ziyi nodded.

After a while he spoke again.

"That Lord of yours," he said. "Did he ever lose a battle?"

Yisi did not answer at once. He stood there and thought for a long time.

"He died once," said Yisi.

Guo Ziyi looked at him.

"Died on wood. Nailed to it," said Yisi. "His men all ran away."

Guo Ziyi said nothing. He looked at the lamp on the table, looked at it a long time. The wick spat, the flame leapt, and sank again.

"Go," said Guo Ziyi.

Yisi made his bow and left the tent.

---

At the fifth watch the rain stopped.

Before it was light, Guo Ziyi set out. He took a troop of guards, and the clerk went too, a bundle on his back; in the bundle were the rosters, to be reported to the Son of Heaven.

Yisi sat on the cart shaft and watched them leave camp. Guo Ziyi rode his dappled grey, going very slowly, just as when he left camp on any ordinary day. At the foot of the slope he turned a bend and could not be seen.

In the camp the cooks had got the firewood to burn at last. The firelight shone on the rims of the pots, a red ring round each.

After daybreak Shi Jingzhong came to find Yisi.

He said the eastern party was still Yisi's. Old Feng was wounded, so he had been given another man, a young one just picked from the foot soldiers, who could ride and had good eyes.

"Whatever you see," said Shi Jingzhong, "send him back first to report. You keep watching."

Yisi said: "All right."
