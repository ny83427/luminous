# Volume Six　The Shuofang Army

## Chapter 29　Liu Heng

> The Way has no constant name; the sage has no constant form. Teachings are set up to suit each place, and quietly save the multitudes.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

The next day Yisi rose before light.

He did not put on the purple robe. He wore the old grey robe, its cuffs worn furry. The purple robe he folded, wrapped in a piece of blue cloth, and tucked under his arm.

Jingjing stood at the door watching him.

"Pa," said Jingjing, "what are you taking that for?"

"If they want to take it back," said Yisi, "there has to be something for them to take."

He went out. At the ward gate an old servant of the Guo household was waiting, who bowed to him and said Guo Ziyi had sent him with one sentence.

"What sentence?"

"Tell the truth," said the old servant. "That's all."

Yisi nodded, and went on towards the Imperial City.

---

The Ministry of Rites was inside the Department of State Affairs: through the Zhuque Gate, east, row upon row of galleries, room after room off the galleries. A minor clerk led Yisi there, and he stood in the gallery the better part of an hour before he was called in.

Three men sat in the room.

The one in the middle, about forty, square-faced, stiff-bearded, sitting bolt upright, was the censor who had submitted the memorial. The one on the left was from the Court of State Ceremonial, which had charge of guests from the four quarters; Yisi had met him once before and could not remember his surname. The one on the right, a little past fifty, was thin and very pale, his eyelids half lowered, his two hands tucked in his sleeves, as if he were dozing.

The clerk said in Yisi's ear: the one on the right is Vice-Minister Liu of the Ministry of Rites—Liu Heng.

By the wall stood an old Sogdian in *hu* dress, from the *sabao*'s office, which had charge of the fire-worshipping *hu* of Chang'an. The old man kept his head down all the while; when Yisi came in he raised his eyes once, then lowered them again.

Yisi bowed and stood. No one told him to sit.

---

The censor spoke first.

"You are Yisi?"

"I am."

"The Daqin monastery in the Yining Ward is in your charge?"

"It is."

"Those eleven who killed a man in the Liquan Ward are men of your monastery?"

"They are."

The censor seemed not to have expected him to answer so plainly, and paused before he went on: "Does your teaching teach men to destroy other men's temples and put out their incense?"

"It does not."

"It does not? Then where did they learn it?"

Yisi thought.

"They learned it at my monastery," he said.

For a moment the room was silent. The man from the Court of State Ceremonial raised his head to look at him. Liu Heng's eyelids stirred, and did not open.

"They heard stories from the scripture at my monastery," said Yisi. "They heard them, remembered them, remembered them wrong. I didn't keep watch over them. They went out from under my hand, and the water was drawn from my monastery's well."

A hint of a smile showed on the censor's face. He said: "So you admit it?"

"I admit it."

"Your scriptures," the censor asked again, "do they have words teaching men to kill those of other faiths?"

Yisi paused again. This time the pause was longer than before.

He thought of Ba'aluo standing at the door of his room, dripping wet, saying: Elijah slew the prophets of Baal, four hundred and fifty men.

"There are stories of killing," said Yisi.

The man from the Court of State Ceremonial drew a soft breath. The old Sogdian by the wall raised his head.

"Those were things of ancient times," said Yisi. "Our Lord never killed a single man."

He paused, and said again:

"He was the one who was killed."

The censor looked at him as if waiting for him to go on. Yisi did not go on.

---

After a while the censor took up the memorial from the table and read out a passage. What he read were his own words: *The* hu *teaching was never a law of the Middle Kingdom; if today they dare to kill a man of the fire temple, what will they not dare tomorrow?* When he had read it he put the memorial down and looked at Yisi.

"You received the purple from the Son of Heaven," he said. "Tell me yourself—are you still worthy of it?"

Yisi took out the blue cloth bundle from under his arm, untied it, held the purple robe in both hands, and laid it on the table.

"Whether I'm worthy," he said, "it's not for me to say. If it's to be taken back, I've brought it."

The censor was taken aback. He looked at the purple robe, a very deep purple, very neatly folded, lying among a heap of documents like a block of ink not yet ground.

At that moment, Liu Heng spoke.

---

Liu Heng spoke very slowly, and his voice was small, so small that everyone in the room had to lean forward to hear.

"Censor," he said, "what does the code say?"

The censor frowned. "What code?"

"The articles on affrays and suits," said Liu Heng. "Killing a man in an affray: strangulation. When several plot together and beat a man, the one who struck the heaviest blow bears the heaviest guilt, and the rest are each reduced one degree. That's all this humble official remembers. The censor reads the code more closely than I do; if I've remembered wrong, the censor will correct me."

The censor said nothing.

"The code says: whoever struck the blow, the guilt is his. Whoever went along, his guilt is reduced one degree," said Liu Heng. "I turned the pages all night, and did not find one article saying that when a man has killed someone, the temple he worships in is to be pulled down."

"The *hu* teaching is different," said the censor.

"How is it different?"

"The *hu* teaching was never a law of the Middle Kingdom."

Liu Heng nodded, as if he found this very reasonable. He drew one hand out of his sleeve and took up an old roll of paper from the table. The paper was yellow, its edges brittle.

"This is an old record from the Court of State Ceremonial," he said. "I borrowed it yesterday. In the twelfth year of Zhenguan, Emperor Taizong issued an edict, and it was about this very Daqin monastery."

He opened the paper, narrowed his eyes, and read it word by word:

"The Way has no constant name; the sage has no constant form. Teachings are set up to suit each place, and quietly save the multitudes."

When he had read it, he rolled the paper up and put it back on the table.

"What Emperor Taizong permitted," said Liu Heng, "if the censor wishes to abolish it, this humble official would not dare stand in the way. Only the censor's memorial must include one sentence: this edict of Emperor Taizong's was wrong. Once that is written, this humble official will sign it along with the censor."

No one in the room spoke.

The man from the Court of State Ceremonial looked down at his hands. The old Sogdian from the *sabao*'s office looked at Liu Heng without blinking.

The censor's face slowly went red.

---

Liu Heng tucked his hand back into his sleeve.

"There are two more things," he said, "that I also turned up yesterday. One is a case of the capital prefecture. In the spring of the first year of Qianyuan, starving people west of the city fought over relief, and the government itself trampled people to death; afterwards it was this *hu* monk who led the men of his monastery in giving out relief to six thousand, fifty to a group, sitting to eat, and not one was hurt. The other is a case of the Ministry of War. In the ninth month of the second year of Zhide, at Xiangji Temple, the man under Lord Director Guo who scouted out the ambush of horse on the eastern road was this man. Three arrows—one in the back, one in the side, one in the face."

He paused.

"These two things have nothing to do with today's case," said Liu Heng. "I merely came across them, and mention them in passing."

He glanced at the purple robe on the table.

"As for this garment," he said, "it was granted by the Son of Heaven. What the Son of Heaven grants, the Ministry of Rites cannot take back, nor can the Censorate. Today the three of us deliberate only on the code."

When he had finished he lowered his eyelids again, as if he had dozed off once more.

---

That day's questioning came to no conclusion.

The censor said he would memorialise again, the man from the Court of State Ceremonial said he would go back and ask for instructions, the old man from the *sabao*'s office said nothing. When Yisi was told to leave, the purple robe was still on the table. He stood at the door and looked back at it.

Liu Heng, without opening his eyes, said: "Take it with you. Leave it here, and I'll have to watch over it for you."

Yisi went back, took up the purple robe, wrapped it, and tucked it under his arm again.

How the capital prefecture judged the case afterwards need not be told in detail here. All that is known is that the seventeen-year-old boy never came out of prison again; Ba'aluo was given sixty strokes of the heavy stick and exiled to Lingnan; the other nine were given forty strokes each and released. Of the nine released, six were still at the monastery the following year; three left, and no one knew where they went.

The day Ba'aluo set out was in the twelfth month. Yisi went outside the Chunming Gate to see him off.

The stick wounds on his back had not yet healed; he limped as he walked, a cangue on his neck, two runners escorting him, one ahead and one behind. His white clothes were long gone; he wore prisoner's clothes, grey and dirty. He saw Yisi and stopped.

Yisi handed him a waterskin. The water had been boiled, and was still warm.

Ba'aluo took it, and did not drink.

"Master Yi," he said, "how is the boy?"

Yisi did not answer.

Ba'aluo lowered his head and looked at the waterskin in his hands. Looked a long time.

"I wasn't wrong," he said. He said it very softly, not as if to Yisi, but as if to himself.

He said it again: "I wasn't wrong."

The third time, he could not finish it. He clutched the waterskin to his chest and squatted down on the frozen earth at the roadside, his shoulders heaving. The two runners waited beside him and did not hurry him. After a while the one in front said "Let's go," and he stood up and went.

Yisi stood by the road and watched him go into the distance. He went very slowly, limping, and every little way he looked back.

The Daqin monastery was not pulled down. The purple robe was not taken back.

---

Yisi came out of the Department of State Affairs and walked towards the Zhuque Gate. Halfway there, someone behind him called his name.

It was Liu Heng. He was alone, with no attendants, his hands in his sleeves; he came slowly up to Yisi and walked beside him.

The two of them walked a way, and neither spoke.

Under the Zhuque Gate, Liu Heng stopped. He looked out at the broad Zhuque Street beyond the gate, the carts and people coming and going, running all the way to the south out of sight.

"You must be wondering," said Liu Heng, "why I spoke for you."

Yisi did not answer. He was indeed wondering.

Liu Heng smiled a little. When he smiled, his eyelids were still half lowered.

"My grandmother's surname was Kang," he said. "From the land of Kang, Samarkand. Our family has lived in Chang'an four generations; we've long been Tang. Only, in the back courtyard of our house, there's a little room, and in the room a basin of fire, and from my grandmother's day on it has never gone out."

Yisi turned his head and looked at him.

"Every year in the twelfth month," said Liu Heng, "someone comes from the fire temple in the Liquan Ward to bring our household new fire. He comes, but not by the front gate; he comes in at the back, and when he's brought it he goes. When I was little, that man always patted my head and gave me a sweet."

He paused.

"It was the fire-keeper."

Yisi stood there, and for a long time could not speak.

"If I spoke for the fire temple," said Liu Heng, "people would ask: Vice-Minister Liu, why are you speaking for the fire-worshippers? And asking and asking, they'd get as far as my back courtyard. When I speak for you, people just take me for a pedant who's read the code until he's gone simple."

He looked at Yisi.

"If today the court took back your purple robe and pulled down your monastery," he said, "tomorrow it would be the Liquan Ward's turn, and the day after the Buzheng Ward's. To protect the Luminous today is truly to protect the fire temples tomorrow. You needn't thank me. It isn't for you."

Yisi bowed his head and made him a bow.

Liu Heng did not return it. He turned, his hands back in his sleeves, and walked slowly back. After a few steps he turned his head and said one more thing:

"Tomorrow, go to the fire temple. When you go, don't talk about your Lord."

"I won't," said Yisi.

---

The next day Yisi went to the Liquan Ward.

Four men went with him. Haddad spoke Sogdian, and went to interpret. Ishaq took medicines, and a bundle of dry flatbread. Gabriel asked to go himself; it was he who had baptised Ba'aluo. Babai did not go; he counted the money in the monastery's accounts three times, counted it out coin by coin, put it in a cloth bag and gave it to Yisi, and made an entry in the accounts.

Narsai went too. But at the gate of the fire temple he stopped.

"I won't go in," he said. "That is a place where idols are worshipped. The Law forbids it."

Yisi looked at him once, and did not press him.

So Narsai stood at the foot of the wall outside the gate. The sun was very strong that day, and he stood all the while without moving from the spot.

---

The temple master received them in a small room in the front courtyard.

The temple master was the man who had driven the knife into his belly that day, about forty, with no expression on his face. He did not ask them to sit, nor have tea brought. Haddad explained why they had come, and when the temple master had heard it he nodded and had someone take the cloth bag. When it had been taken, he did not open it to look.

Four had been hurt, all lying in the rooms at the back. One had a broken arm, one a broken head, and two had been struck by buckets, their legs a great mass of bruises. Ishaq went from room to room looking at them and put salve on them. The young man with the broken arm cursed the whole time, in Sogdian; Ishaq could not understand, and nodded as he spread the salve, as though the man were thanking him. Haddad listened beside them with a strange look on his face, and did not translate.

The family of the old fire-keeper were an old woman and a boy of ten or so, the old man's grandson.

The old woman was very old, badly bent, sitting on a low stool, her two hands on her knees. She looked at Yisi, looked a long time, and said nothing.

Yisi stood before her, bent, and made her a bow.

As he bowed, his back was to the basin of fire in the courtyard.

Still the old woman said nothing.

After a long while she spoke. She spoke in Sogdian, very slowly, and Haddad beside them put it into Tang for Yisi sentence by sentence.

"Why did they want to put out the fire?"

Yisi thought and said: "They thought that putting out the fire was good for their Lord."

Haddad turned it. The old woman listened, and again looked at Yisi a long time.

"This basin of fire," she said, "his grandfather's grandfather brought it from the land of Kang. He tended it forty years. Forty years, and not one day did he let it go out."

She paused.

"The day he died, he didn't let it go out either."

Yisi kept his head bowed and listened while Haddad finished turning the sentence.

The old woman stood up, steadied herself against the wall, and went slowly into the inner room. At the door she turned and said something. Haddad did not translate it. Yisi did not ask.

The boy had been standing to one side all the while, watching Ishaq. Ishaq took a dry flatbread from his bundle and held it out to him. The boy looked at the bread, looked at the door of the inner room, put out his hand and took it, held it clenched in his fist, and did not eat it.

---

When they came out of the fire temple, it was past noon.

Narsai was still standing at the foot of the wall. The sun had beaten on him all morning; his forehead was covered with sweat, and under those high brow ridges his stone-like eyes looked at Yisi.

"The people inside," Narsai asked, "what did they say?"

Yisi stood at the gate and did not answer at once. He looked back. Through the crack of the gate he could still see the stone platform in the middle of the courtyard, and the bronze basin on the platform. The fire in the basin was very small, almost invisible in the strong sun; only the layer of heat above it made the wall behind shimmer and shimmer.

"She said," said Yisi, "the fire didn't go out."
