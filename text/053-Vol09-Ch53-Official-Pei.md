# Volume Nine　The Translation Hall

## Chapter 53　Official Pei

> It benefits creatures and profits men, and ought to be practised throughout the world.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

One day in the tenth month of the third year of Zhenyuan, nothing was written on Jingjing's board. That morning he stood in the middle of the courtyard as usual, told his knotted cord, went into the scripture room and lit the lamp, opened the psalms, and turned to "As for man, his days are as grass: as a flower of the field, so he flourisheth. For the wind passeth over it, and it is gone; and the place thereof shall know it no more." He read it several times and took up the charcoal, and before he had set it down, the old servant of the Pei family of the Guangde Ward came knocking at the monastery gate, saying the master asked the Great Virtue Jing to come; Shiyiniang had already gone ahead. Jingjing put down the charcoal; the board was empty; he leaned it at the head of his bed, woke Lingbao, and the two of them followed the old servant out of the Yining Ward.

The Pei house was in the northwest corner of the Guangde Ward, not far from the offices of the Metropolitan Prefecture, two locust trees at its gate. Official Pei had been thirty years in the Metropolitan Prefecture, rising from a clerk outside the regular ranks to administrator, and since retiring had grown flowers at home; the chrysanthemums in the courtyard were in flower, yellow and white, pot after pot set out along the gallery, and no one had had time to water them; their leaves had curled. The room was very dim, the brazier burning very high; Official Pei lay in bed, so thin he was only a handful of bones, his cheeks sunken, that high brow-bone of his the higher for it. Shiyiniang sat by the bed holding a bowl of medicine; the medicine was cold; she had held it all the while. She was a daughter of a distant branch of the Pei family, by reckoning a sister of Official Pei's clan; when she married Yisi, Official Pei presided and wrote the marriage document; Lingbao called Official Pei Great-Uncle, and coming in, knelt first before the bed and knocked his head on the floor. Official Pei saw Jingjing, sent his son and daughter out, kept only Shiyiniang, had the old servant pull the door shut, and told Jingjing to sit closer. His voice was very low; he rested two breaths after each sentence; Jingjing bent down and put his ear to his mouth.

He said he had something to give the Great Virtue. He had Shiyiniang take it from the chest at the head of the bed; Shiyiniang opened the chest, and on top lay a small book, several dozen sheets of tough paper folded and pasted together, the surface rubbed soft, the corners frayed. Official Pei said this was what he had recorded over thirty years: in the Metropolitan Prefecture, in the Bureau of Sacrifices, in the Court of State Ceremonial, in the Censorate, in the two counties of Wannian and Chang'an—which man had charge of which matter, which man could be trusted with things and which could not, which man would open his mouth only after a bolt of silk had been sent, which man would be offended if silk were sent, which man remembered grudges and which remembered kindnesses—all of it in there. He said this book was for his own use at first; that year when the Great Virtue Yi came into the city he ran errands to the Bureau of Sacrifices for him, ran a few times and felt it wouldn't do not to write things down, and so he wrote them down, wrote for thirty years; of the men in it, half were dead now, and half were no longer in the offices they had been in; but those remaining were still of use. Jingjing opened it and looked: page after page in that upright regular hand of Official Pei's: names of men, names of offices, names of wards, small notes beneath: "hasty; go early," "fond of *weiqi*," "his wife is Buddhist; do not speak of the monastery," "this man can be trusted, but not trusted much"; some names circled in vermilion, some ticked in black ink; those ticked were dead.

Official Pei said that year the Patriarchate's order said that dealings with the government should go by one line only, through the Great Virtue Yi and the official surnamed Pei; the first time he heard it read, he was glad and afraid at once: glad that the Patriarchate thought well of him, afraid because one line is one thread, and if the thread breaks, there's nothing at all. He said: with the Great Virtue Yi gone, that thread was half a thread, and now he too was going, and the half would be gone. He rested, and said: "From now on you can't have only one thread. You must have ten. Of ten, if three break, seven remain." Jingjing said he would remember it. Official Pei said further: the men in the book, don't go to them all; go to too many, and people will think you mean to do something; if you go, go to one at a time, and when you go, speak first of their affairs and after of the monastery's.

When he had said these things he lay resting with his eyes closed; Jingjing thought he was asleep and was about to rise when he opened his eyes again and said he wanted to tell the Great Virtue an old story. He said that in the spring of the first year of Qianyuan, when the Metropolitan Prefecture was to give out rice west of the Qujiang Pool, he had worried for days, afraid people would be crushed to death; that morning the Great Virtue Yi came to see him in the side hall of the prefecture and said, when all was said, only one thing: first have the people sit down, then give out the rice. He said he thought then: this *hu* monk is rather interesting; that day he stood in the overgrown garden all day, from the hour of *mao* to the hour of *shen*, watching six thousand-odd people sit on the grass by wards, not one crushed, not one claiming falsely; he stood till his legs went weak, and sat down on the grass himself. He said that as he sat down he remembered something the Great Virtue Yi had told him: that the line in the book, the first line, was a question his son had asked. He said: "That year you asked your Pa why people must first be made to sit. These thirty years I've run the offices for you, half for your Pa's sake and half for that child who asked the question." When he had said it he laughed a little himself, and said these words he had never said to the Great Virtue Yi; if the Great Virtue Yi had heard them, he'd probably have said he talked too much again.

Jingjing asked whether there was anything he wished the monastery to do for him. Official Pei thought and said: he was washed; all his life he had believed in your Lord, and this he did not regret; but this family of his—his wife believed in the Buddha, his son had studied the Confucian books and sat for the Classics examination, his daughter had married into a Buddhist family—when he died the family would invite monks to do the seven sevens, invite someone to choose the grave site; let them do all that; the Great Virtue must not contend with them. He said his grave would be on the Shaoling Plain, in the ancestral graves of the Pei; if the Great Virtue was willing, after the monks had gone, he might bring two men to the grave and recite a passage for him—that passage Elder Sabrisho recited the year he baptized him at the well—and when it was recited, leave; don't make it hard for the family. He said too that after he died his son would have someone write the epitaph, which would write what offices he had held, whose daughter he had married, and probably not mention you by one character; and you must not go and ask them to add any. Jingjing said he knew.

Then Jingjing said he had brought something today. He took a cloth bundle from his robe and undid it: the monastery's glass bowl, used at the offering once in seven days, with its rounds of Sasanian cut facets shining ring by ring under the lamp; and in the bundle too a small piece of the offering bread, broken off in the hall before he came out. Shiyiniang rose and barred the door, and stuffed the cracks of the window; Lingbao knelt by the bed and held Official Pei's head. Jingjing recited in Syriac the few lines of the offering once in seven, reciting a line and saying it in Tang, and when he came to "This is my body" broke the small piece of bread smaller and set it at Official Pei's lips. Official Pei could not open his mouth wide; the bread rested on his lip; he worked it once with his tongue and took a tiny bit, and the rest fell on the pillow; Shiyiniang picked it up with her finger and put it in her own mouth. Jingjing brought the glass bowl to his lips; in the bowl was only a mouthful of wine; Official Pei touched it to his lips and did not swallow. Jingjing laid his hand on his forehead; the forehead was cold; he said *Lord, have mercy*, and said it again, and at the third time Official Pei looked at him with open eyes, his lips moved, and what he said was: good, good.

Official Pei died on the night of the third of the eleventh month of that year, aged seventy. That day when Jingjing came back from the Guangde Ward it was already dark; he went first to the gate and lit the lamp, went back to his room, and the board was still empty, leaning at the head of his bed. He took up the charcoal and wrote one character, *cao*, grass, and did not wipe it off; the next morning he did not wipe it off either, and it stayed until Official Pei was buried.

---

The Pei family's funeral was conducted with great propriety. The spirit seat was set in the main hall; in the front courtyard a dozen-odd monks of the Jianfu Temple were invited, chanting from morning till night without pause, the chime struck stroke after stroke; in the rear courtyard a geomancer was invited, who went with his burial books to the Shaoling Plain to look at the ground. People came from the Metropolitan Prefecture, people came from the Bureau of Sacrifices, Official Pei's colleagues of those days, his subordinates, those who had asked him to do things, party after party; they came and bowed before the spirit, said a few words to the Pei son, and left. The day Jingjing went with Samaan they arrived just as the monks were holding the first seventh-day fast; the Pei son came out to meet them, forty or so, in the heaviest mourning, his face his father's face but his brow-bones not so high; he bowed to Jingjing and said his late father's last charge was: when the Great Virtue Jing comes, receive him with courtesy; and said the fast was being held in the hall just now; would the Great Virtue sit a while in the side hall, and when the fast was done he would ask the Great Virtue in. Jingjing said there was no need to go in; they would sit a while in the side hall and go. Samaan beside him folded his two hands in his sleeves, his face not very pleasant; Jingjing glanced at him, and he lowered his head.

Lingbao did not sit with them in the side hall. He was a nephew of the Pei family, in mourning clothes, kneeling before the spirit seat in the row of the Pei kin; when the monks recited a passage, the kin bowed once, and he bowed once too. Shiyiniang was in that row too, kneeling among the women, a white cloth bound round her head. Jingjing, looking through the lattice of the side hall's window, saw Lingbao rise with his Pei cousins and kneel with them, and when the chime sounded his forehead touched the ground like the others'. Jingjing drew his eyes back and took up the water bowl before him.

When they had sat more than an hour, Shiyiniang came out from the back with a bowl of water, hot; she handed it to Jingjing and said: cold you can't drink; and said that kneeling in there, what she recited in her heart was our scripture; the monks recite the monks', she recites hers, and neither gets in the other's way. Jingjing took the bowl and asked whether she was tired. Shiyiniang said she wasn't; all her life she had been used to standing among several families: the Cheng family, the Pei family, the Yi family—she had stood so among them all. When she had said it she went back to kneel. The Pei lady came out once too, past sixty, her hair all white, her eyes swollen with weeping; she came up to Jingjing and said she knew the old man believed in your faith; all these years on every Lord's Day he rose before light and said he was going to the monastery, and she had never once stopped him; she didn't understand that Lord of yours; she only knew that whenever the old man came back from your monastery his face was always good. As she said it she wept again, and Shiyiniang came out from inside and helped her back in.

The epitaph the Pei son asked Lü Xiuyan to write. Lü Xiuyan was nearly sixty that year and still sold calligraphy in the Chongxian Ward; when the epitaph was written, before it was carved, he came once to the Yining Ward, stood before the stele looking at the characters he had written in those days, and only then came in to find Jingjing. He said the Pei epitaph was in his hand; the text the Pei family had had a *jinshi* compose: it wrote that Master Pei came of Hedong, what offices his grandfather held, what offices his father held, that Master Pei passed the Classics examination, served in office as administrator of the Metropolitan Prefecture, married a lady of such-and-such a family, had so many sons, so many daughters—and not one character about you; he had written it as it was, in regular script, the same hand as this stele. He said there was only one place he had added himself: where the text came to Master Pei's late years, the original draft said "in his late years he lived at ease and amused himself with flowers and trees"; when he wrote it in vermilion, he added after it four characters: "in his late years he loved to give"; the Pei son saw it and said: well and good; and let it stay. He said those four characters were written for those who knew; those who did not know would read them and take them for just a kind phrase.

The burial was in the twelfth month. That day the wind blew on the Shaoling Plain; after the Pei family and the monks had gone, Jingjing, with Lingbao and Samaan, came up from the village below the plain to that new grave. The earth of the grave was still wet; there was no stone before it, only two cypress saplings newly planted by the Pei son, bent to one side by the wind. Jingjing knelt and recited a passage in Syriac: the passage Sabrisho recited the year he baptized Official Pei at the well; he recited it very slowly; the wind was strong, and the words were blown away as soon as they left his mouth. When it was recited he recited it once more in Tang. Lingbao knelt beside him, still in his mourning clothes, reciting with him; Samaan stood, and did not kneel, but his lips moved. When it was done, Jingjing took from his robe a small wooden tablet with a cross written on it in charcoal; he meant to stick it in at the foot of the cypress saplings, then remembered Official Pei saying don't make it hard for the family, and put the tablet back in his robe, stood up, and said to Lingbao: let's go. Back at the monastery he wiped the character *grass* off the board.

---

In the spring of the next year, the fourth year of Zhenyuan, a dispatch came to the city. The dispatch said that from now on the registers of the monks and nuns of the capital would be under the general charge of the newly established Commissioners of Merit for the Left and Right Streets, the Commissioners of Merit to be palace eunuchs holding the post concurrently. Samaan read the dispatch twice, and when he had read it looked at Jingjing and said: this Commissioner of Merit—does he have charge of us or not? The dispatch doesn't say; it only says monks and nuns; but the people of the city call us monks too. In the third month two men came from the Commissioner of Merit's office: one an inspector, very young, in a blue robe; the other a minor clerk carrying a stack of registers. The inspector said: by command of the Commissioner of Merit, all monasteries of the capital that have monks are to be inspected and registered anew: how many persons, how many rooms, how many *mu* of land, how many scriptures, how many images—each to be reported clearly. He spread a blank register form on the table and had the monastery fill it in itself.

Jingjing held the brush himself and filled it in item by item. How many persons: elders, priests, deacons, monks, guard brothers, the destitute old living in the monastery—he counted them one by one and wrote a number. How many rooms: the hall of five bays, the scripture room, the side courtyard, the felt tents of the back courtyard—he wrote them. How many *mu* of land: that stretch of burial ground outside the Kaiyuan Gate—did it count as monastery property? He thought, and wrote it in. How many scriptures: Syriac, Tang, half translated—he went through them cupboard by cupboard and wrote a number. How many images: in the hall there were no images, only the cross; in the column for images he wrote the character *none*, and beside it noted in small characters: in the hall, one cross enshrined. When he came to the end, the form had a column called "Other," and he stopped: that stele in the front courtyard—what did it count as? Monastery property, a furnishing, or something else? He held the brush and did not know which column to put it in. In the end in the "Other" column he wrote one line: In the front courtyard, one stele set up, erected first month of the second year of Jianzhong, permitted by the Bureau of Sacrifices.

The inspector watched him fill it in, looked at the hall, looked at the scripture room, looked at the stele in the front courtyard, sat down in the side hall, drank a mouthful of tea, and said with a smile that in this business of registering, every monastery had a little "courtesy"—paper-and-brush money, running-about money, as much as it pleased; let the monastery see to it. Samaan, standing behind Jingjing, said softly in Syriac that when Official Pei was alive he handled this sort of thing; it was in his book too: at the Bureau of Sacrifices, some men you send a bolt of silk first. Jingjing had Samaan bring the account book, opened it to this year's page, and pointed: in the common fund, after the stele money was moved, the column for repairing the hall was empty, the column for clothing allowances was empty too; what remained was only the column for gruel rice. He told Samaan: if this bolt of silk is to be sent, it can come only from this column. Samaan looked at that column and shut the account book. Jingjing turned and told the inspector: the paper and brushes for the registering, whatever the monastery should pay, the monastery will pay; how much, let the Inspector write a figure, and the monastery will pay that figure into the office, and Samaan will enter it in the accounts; a courtesy, the monastery cannot provide. The smile on the inspector's face did not go away; he said the Great Virtue Jing was a sensible man, and said too that this stele of the monastery's was well set up; all Chang'an had been to see it; the stele says six Sons of Heaven have protected you; with the Son of Heaven protecting you, naturally you don't care about a few coins. When he had said it he left, and did not even leave the registers. Samaan watched him go far off and said to Jingjing: that man has marked it down. Jingjing said he knew.

Half a month later the Metropolitan Prefecture passed on a dispatch quoting a statement from the Commissioner of Merit, saying: the West Market is a place where merchants and travellers converge; for a *hu* temple to set up pots in the market and give out gruel gathers crowds in the market and may lead to disturbance and obstruct the shops; it should at once be stopped. Jingjing, holding the dispatch, stood before that great pot of the gruel kitchen in the West Market; the fire beneath the pot had not yet gone out; those queuing stood half a street long: the old, the sick, those carrying children; one old man he knew, who had received rice west of the Qujiang Pool that year, lame now, leaning on a stick. Jingjing had the fire put out and the pot carried back, and himself stood where the pot had been and told those queuing one by one: tomorrow it won't be here; from now on come to the monastery gate in the Yining Ward to receive it; the space at the monastery gate is small, and only one pot can be boiled a day; the old, the sick and those carrying children first. That evening the gruel kitchen in the West Market was taken down, and those queuing dispersed; halfway through dispersing someone cursed: *hu* monks—who knew whom he was cursing. That night Samaan entered a line in his account book: today so many came, so many did not receive; he had kept accounts all his life, and the three characters *did not receive* he wrote for the first time.

---

That same month Jingjing went looking for people. As Official Pei had said, he went to one at a time, and began with the book. In the book there was a clerk of the Bureau of Sacrifices beside whose name Official Pei had noted "this man can be trusted"; Jingjing went to the Bureau and asked the men at the gate, and was told that clerk had been transferred to the eastern capital the year before last. At the Court of State Ceremonial there was a receptionist noted as "fond of *weiqi*; can be met over a game"; Jingjing had someone pass in his card, waited three days, and the answer came back that the receptionist was ill and saw no visitors. At the Metropolitan Prefecture there was a recorder noted as "mindful of old times; the Great Virtue Yi once treated his mother's illness"; Jingjing went; the recorder did see him, and talked with him half an hour in the little room by the gate, said he remembered the Great Virtue Yi, remembered his mother's leg, said that dispatch was the Commissioner of Merit's statement, the Metropolitan Prefecture only passed it on; a mere recorder, he could not speak to it; and saw him to the gate and said that in future if the Great Virtue had business he might still come and sit. Jingjing came back to the monastery, spread out the book, and wrote beside the name of the clerk of the Bureau of Sacrifices "transferred to the eastern capital," beside the name of the receptionist of the Court of State Ceremonial "ill, sees no one," beside the name of the recorder of the Metropolitan Prefecture "mindful of old times, cannot speak." Of three threads, two broken, and the one left was hollow.

Those few mornings he found that one thing in himself was not as before. Standing in the middle of the courtyard telling his knotted cord, what he was thinking of as he told it was that line in Samaan's account book, "did not receive, so many"; when he opened the psalms his eyes were on the scripture and his heart was still on that line of figures. Before, he read the scripture first and met the day's business after; these days the business came in first and the scripture was crowded behind it. One morning he simply shut the scripture and sat, reading nothing, waiting for that line of figures to withdraw of itself; he waited till the window paper whitened, and it withdrew, and only then opened the book again, and turned to the passage where the Lord sends out his disciples: Behold, I send you forth as sheep in the midst of wolves: be ye therefore wise as serpents, and harmless as doves. He wrote on the board the character for *serpent*, and beside it the character for *dove*, two characters crowded onto one board.

Then he went to Li Su. Li Su was at the Directorate of Astronomy, which had nothing to do with monks and nuns; but the Directorate was in the palace, and he knew a few men in the palace. Li Su heard him out, reckoned once on his fingers, and said the Commissioner of Merit was a palace eunuch holding the post concurrently; of the eunuchs' affairs he could not speak; but among the Commissioner's administrative assistants there was one surnamed Yang who was fond of astrology and often came to the Directorate to ask about auspicious and inauspicious days; he could pass a word to him for the Great Virtue; whether it would reach, he dared not say. He went too to Mi Jifen; Mi Jifen was at that time a general in the Shence Army, already very old; he heard Jingjing out and said that eunuch was one of the Shence Army's superiors; on superiors' affairs, a subordinate could not well speak; but he knew many men in the Shence Army; he would find out whose man that inspector was. A month later Mi Jifen had his son Siyuan come to the monastery with word: that inspector was the nephew of an officer in the Shence Army, and that officer was a fellow countryman of Luo Haoxin's. So Jingjing went to Luo Haoxin. As soon as Luo Haoxin heard, he struck the table and said he knew that young fellow, presuming on his uncle's place in the Commissioner of Merit's service to go about soliciting courtesies; he'd go and speak; and said too that that year he had got the Great Virtue a sheet of scolding; this time, let him count it as repaying the Great Virtue once.

Each time Jingjing went to someone, he came back and added a page at the back of Official Pei's book, writing the man's name, writing what office he was in, writing what he had been asked to do and how he had answered. His writing and Official Pei's side by side: Official Pei's upright, every stroke standing; his leaning to the right—his Pa had said it was like a man hurrying forward on a road. On the third of the eleventh month that year, the first anniversary of Official Pei's death, the matter was not yet settled. That day Jingjing went once to the Shaoling Plain; of the cypress saplings on the grave one had lived and one had died. He came back and turned the book to the back; what he had added was only three pages: one Li Su, one Mi Jifen, one Luo Haoxin.

---

When spring opened in the fifth year of Zhenyuan, Luo Haoxin brought word that the Commissioner of Merit's office had given way: the gruel kitchen might open; not in the West Market, but in the ward street of the Yining Ward, a shed set up beside the monastery gate; two pots might be opened; people of the ward might come, people of other wards might come, but only those the ward headman had checked could be given it. Samaan heard it and said: this is half. Jingjing said: half is open too. He said too that the inspector's courtesy the monastery had not given, and would not give in future either; but General Luo had gone and said this word for the monastery, and the monastery must thank him; the way to thank him: this winter, soldiers of the Shence Army hurt by frost, send them to the monastery, and the monastery would treat their hurts without charge. Luo Haoxin said that was good; what the men in camp feared most was frostbite; lose your fingers to frost, and you can't draw a bow.

The day the gruel shed went up in the ward street, the ward headman came with the ward's household register and stood by the pot; to each who came he asked, which ward are you from? If it was this ward, he gave; if another ward, he leafed through the register, and not finding the name, shook his head. The first day his head-shaking sent away a dozen-odd people; one old woman from another ward, carrying a child, stood by the pot and would not go; the ward headman said it wouldn't do; Samaan looked at Jingjing; Jingjing went over and poured his own bowl of gruel out for the child; Samaan squatted down and asked the child his name, and the child said he was called A-Nu, six years old, come from the Guangde Ward with his granny; and then Jingjing told the ward headman that from tomorrow his own bowl every day would go to people from other wards; whether the ward headman checked them or not, he didn't care. The ward headman looked at him and did not stop him. After that every day Jingjing's bowl was poured out for people from other wards; Lingbao saw it, and the next day poured out his own bowl too; Samaan made an entry in the accounts: Came from another ward: the grandson A-Nu, six, of a certain granny of the Guangde Ward; given from the monks' own share. When he had written it, his own bowl he poured out too.

That winter the Shence Army sent a dozen-odd frostbitten soldiers, all young men, the chilblains on their hands and feet ulcerated and running yellow. Jingjing had Lingbao go to the son of Shopkeeper Wang in the West Market to make up medicine, boiled a chilblain salve, and put it on them one by one; he put it on half a month, and nine got well, three lost toes, and one lost two fingers. The one who lost two fingers held out his hand to show Jingjing and said: Master, this hand of mine—will it draw a bow again? Jingjing thought of Xu Jing: Xu Jing, keeping the beacon tower, had lost two fingers of his right hand to frost, and said his fingers went to the beacon fire, and afterwards loaded the crossbow all the same; he said: it will; there was a man who lost two, and loaded the crossbow all his life.

---

In the twelfth month came Official Pei's second anniversary. Jingjing went once more to the Shaoling Plain, this time taking only Lingbao. The cypress that lived had grown taller; grass had grown on the mound, withered now, a yellow layer pressed flat to the earth by the wind. Jingjing knelt before the grave and recited that passage, and when it was recited took the book from his robe and opened it; the last page was one he had added a few days before; it said: the Commissioner of Merit's inspector so-and-so; and after it a line of small characters: courtesy not given; through General Luo's intercession the gruel kitchen moved to the ward street, two pots; those from other wards given from the monks' own share. He turned this page to show the grave, as if showing someone the accounts. Lingbao beside him asked: Brother, this book—will you go on writing in it? Jingjing said he would write in it till he could write no more, and then you go on writing.

When they got back to the monastery it was dark; the lamp at the gate Samaan had lit for him; the flame steady, a thin frost on the stone block. Jingjing stood a moment under the lamp, went into the scripture room, took up the board, wiped it, opened the roll of Ecclesiastes, turned to "And if one prevail against him, two shall withstand him; and a threefold cord is not quickly broken," and wrote one character on the board: *xian*, thread.

---

Note: In the fourth year of Zhenyuan the Commissioners of Merit for the Left and Right Streets were established, "in general charge of the registers of monks and nuns and their labour service," with eunuchs holding the post—see *Tang huiyao* juan 49, the *Xin Tang shu*, "Treatise on Officials," and Zanning's *Da Song seng shilüe*; whether the monks of the Church of the East came under the Commissioners of Merit history does not say, and this chapter is the novel's inference. Official Pei's family, his epitaph, and Lü Xiuyan's writing of it are invented.
