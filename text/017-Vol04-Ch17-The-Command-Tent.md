# Volume Four　Xiangji Temple

## Chapter 17　The Command Tent

> He first served loyally at the vermilion court, and then enrolled his name in the royal tent.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

In the ninth month the grand army marched out of Fengxiang.

The Marshal was the Prince of Guangping, the Son of Heaven's eldest son, a little past thirty that year, pale-faced, sparing of words, who sat very straight in the saddle. The Deputy Marshal was Guo Ziyi. The men of Shuofang, of Anxi, the Uighurs, the men from the south, the Arabs—two hundred thousand, it was claimed—marched east for days, the banners one unbroken sheet, so that from the head you could not see the tail.

The scouts went ahead of the grand army.

Shi Jingzhong's scouts were still divided as they had been since the Qing Canal, two men to a party. Yisi did not come under Shi Jingzhong; he was one of the men in attendance before Guo Ziyi's tent, and Guo Ziyi had him go with the eastern party, and whatever he saw he reported first to Guo Ziyi and then told Shi Jingzhong. With him went the young man Shi Jingzhong had given him after the Qing Canal.

His name was Wei, from the capital district, third among his brothers, and the camp called him Wei Three. He was eighteen or nineteen, his face not yet grown into itself; he had been a foot soldier in the vanguard, could ride, and had good eyes. He had been with Yisi from the fifth month until now, and he still talked a lot; whatever he saw he had to ask about. Yisi was slow to answer, so he would wait, and when he could not wait any longer he would guess, and if he guessed wrong and Yisi said "No," he did not mind, but guessed again.

The two of them left camp before light and went ahead of the vanguard, came back after dark, and went first to the command tent. Where there was a river, whether there was a bridge over it, whether the bridge would take carts. Where there was a village, whether there was anyone left in it, whether the water in the well was fit to drink. Which roads would sink after rain.

Before the grand army left Fengxiang, the Arab troops had arrived.

They came from the west, not many, a few hundred horse, the horses tall and the men tall too, their heads wrapped, speaking a language no one in camp could understand. Guo Ziyi had made arrangements long before: the Court of State Ceremonial had sent two interpreters from Fengxiang, one a Sogdian, the other a Persian whose family had lived in Chang'an three generations, both fluent in Arabic and Persian. Where the Arab troops would camp, what they ate, when they prayed, which things in camp they would not touch—the interpreters went and asked, and reported to the centre.

Guo Ziyi had Yisi go along twice too.

Yisi's Arabic was not as good as the interpreters', but his Persian came more easily than theirs; the speech of Balkh was much like that of the men from Khurasan among the Arab troops. When he went, the leader of the Arab troops at first paid him little attention, but when he had heard him say a few things about Balkh—which street east of the city sold what—his face eased somewhat, and he had someone bring a bowl of curds.

Yisi kept note of what was asked, one thing at a time, and when he came back checked it against the interpreters before reporting it to the centre. There were two places where the interpreters had misheard, and he said so; there were several things too that the interpreters had asked and he had not.

In the last part of the ninth month the grand army reached the west bank of the Feng River and made camp.

East across the Feng lay Xiangji Temple; north of Xiangji Temple was a great stretch of harvested wheatfields, the stubble still standing, yellow and hard, as far as the eye could see. Further north was the city of Chang'an. The rebels had pitched camp south of the city, their backs to the walls.

---

The day they made camp, Yisi and Wei Three crossed the Feng and went a long way east.

East of the wheatfields there were several canals.

Farmers had dug them years ago, to bring water for the fields. Now it was autumn and the fields needed no watering, and the canals had dried, leaving only a layer of mud in the bottom of each ditch with moss growing on it. Willows were planted along the banks, their leaves half yellow, and when the wind blew they rustled down.

As soon as Wei Three saw these canals he sat up in the saddle.

"These are our canals," he said. "My home's twenty *li* south of the city. When I was little I used to catch loaches in them with my elder brother."

"How many?" asked Yisi.

"Three. Running north–south, each deeper than the one before. The easternmost one has a sluice at the north end; the sluice gate's long gone, you can get a horse through." Wei Three thought and added: "North of the sluice, you're nearly at the rebel camp."

Yisi dismounted and led his horse down into the first canal.

"You watch from up there," he said.

He stood in the bottom of the ditch a while, let the horse lower its head to crop the grass in the bottom, then called up to the bank: "Can you see?"

Wei Three looked down. He looked a while, moved two steps, looked again.

"I can see your head," said Wei Three. "I can see a bit of the horse's back."

The two of them went east to the second canal. The second was deeper than the first. Yisi went down again and Wei Three watched again from above. This time when the horse lowered its head it could not be seen.

Further east, the third. The third was deepest and widest, and the willows on its banks thickest. Yisi led his horse down, and standing straight, the bank came level with his eyebrows; when the horse lowered its head, not even the tips of its ears showed.

Wei Three stood on the bank a long time.

"When I was little it didn't seem this deep," he said.

Yisi went a little way north along the bottom of the ditch, came back, and climbed up the bank.

"How many horses could you hide in it?" asked Wei Three.

"I don't know," said Yisi. "A lot."

The two of them mounted and rode back. Halfway, Wei Three looked back. The setting sun shone on the wheatfields, and every stalk of stubble trailed a long shadow. The canals were hidden in the fields, the willows standing in rows; seen from here, they looked like a few low hedges.

"From this side," said Wei Three, "you can't tell at all."

Yisi said "Mm."

---

On the way back to camp they passed the gate of Xiangji Temple.

Xiangji Temple was a Buddhist temple, with a brick pagoda in it, very tall and very old, grass growing on its eaves. The temple gate stood half open, and on the stone steps before it sat an old monk in a grey cotton robe, patch on patch, stripping a handful of wild greens. Most of the monks of the temple, it was said, had fled; a few old ones, who could not run, had stayed.

The old monk looked up and saw them—saw Yisi's *hu* face, saw the mace at his waist—and the greens in his hands paused.

Yisi dismounted and put his palms together to him. He had lived in Chang'an more than ten years; he knew this courtesy.

The old monk put his palms together too and asked: "Whose troops is the benefactor with?"

"Shuofang."

"Is the benefactor also one who has left the household life?"

Yisi thought and said: "I am a monk."

"Of which house?"

"The Luminous Teaching," said Yisi. "The Daqin monastery in the Yining Ward of Chang'an."

The old monk said "Oh." He bent his head and went on stripping his greens, stripped two stalks, and said: "I've heard of it. A heterodox path."

He said it very evenly, as if saying today's wind was from the west.

Up on his horse, Wei Three's face darkened at once, and he opened his mouth to speak. Yisi looked back at him, and he shut his mouth.

Yisi stood a while, and saw that beside the old monk was a cracked earthen jar with the water in it nearly at the bottom. He took his own waterskin off the saddle, poured half of it into the jar, put the stopper back, and hung it on the saddle again.

The old monk watched him pour, and neither stopped him nor thanked him.

Yisi mounted. When they had gone a dozen paces, the old monk behind them said something, not loudly: "In the next few days there'll be fighting hereabouts. Take care, benefactor."

Yisi turned his head. The old monk had already bent his head and was stripping greens again.

They were nearly at the camp gate before Wei Three asked: "He called you heterodox, and you still gave him water?"

Yisi did not answer.

---

The next day, before light, fog rose.

Yisi and Wei Three lay on the west bank of the first canal and watched the fog spread over from the Feng. It was white and very thick, and it spread without a sound, like a pot of rice-water slowly brimming over the rim. When the fog came, at ten paces you could not see a man.

The two of them lay on the bank, and since they could not see, they listened. They could hear the drums in the rebel camp far off, hear water dripping in the bottom of the ditch, hear birds in the willows, unseen in the fog—only a flutter, and then settling again.

Not until the hour of *chen*, when the sun came out, did the fog clear little by little. In the east it cleared late; over the canals, a good part of an hour later than over the wheatfields.

On the morning of the third day there was fog again.

The other scouting parties came back and reported fog too. Yisi reported the canals and the fog to Guo Ziyi, and Shi Jingzhong went through the reports of every party.

---

On the night of the third day, a council of war was held in the Marshal's tent.

The tent was full of lamps, bright as day. The Prince of Guangping sat at the head, Guo Ziyi on his left. Below were two rows, all generals who led troops. Li Siye sat at the very front; he was so tall that even sitting he was half a head above everyone. Wang Sili sat beside him. Pugu Huai'en sat opposite, and beside Pugu Huai'en sat the Uighur *yabghu*, with an interpreter standing behind him; the *yabghu* said a sentence, the interpreter turned it. Hun Shizhi sat further back, his arms folded.

Shi Jingzhong stood just inside the tent entrance, waiting to answer.

Yisi stood behind Guo Ziyi. There was no seat for him in the tent, nor did he want one; when he was asked, then he spoke.

Wei Three was outside the tent, squatting by a fire with the men of the various scouting parties, craning his neck towards the tent entrance; whenever the curtain moved, he moved.

The council went on a long time.

Fight tomorrow or not? Fight. How? Li Siye said he would lead the Anxi troops and the *mo*-blade companies in front, and hold the centre. The rebels had many horsemen; when the horsemen charged, the blade men would spread out and press forward rank by rank, and smash man and horse together. Wang Sili said that once the vanguard held, the centre would follow, and the rear would steady the line.

The *yabghu* heard the interpreter and said a great string of words. The interpreter turned them over: the *yabghu* meant that the Uighur horses were fast and ought to be in front, charging with the blades, to break the rebel formation at one stroke. The Uighurs had come a long way, they had come to fight; put them at the back to watch, and they would not agree to it.

A few men in the tent laughed. Pugu Huai'en did not laugh, and did not speak.

---

Guo Ziyi glanced at the Prince of Guangping and said: "In the fifth month at Wugong we held a council and settled a few points. I'd like someone to read them to you all."

The Prince of Guangping said: "Read them."

In the lower seats sat a young administrative assistant. He had been the clerk under Han Ye; on that night at Wugong, when Han Ye's folding stool stood empty, it was he who had sat beside it with the brush and taken it all down. Han Ye had not come back, and he had filled his place.

He rose and made his bow, took a sheet of paper from inside his robe, and read.

Scouts go in pairs: when one sees something, he rides back first to report, the other stays and keeps watching. If the rebels withdraw, first check the wheel ruts, check the horse tracks, and pursue only when they are checked. The wings keep horsemen back and are not allowed to join the pursuit. When the vanguard meets the rebels, it holds them and does not pursue. Anyone who pursues, cut him down.

He read very slowly, one point at a time. When he had finished he folded the paper and sat down.

*Qing Canal*—two words no one said aloud. But many of the men sitting in the tent had been there, and many had had their men die there. Li Siye and his Anxi troops had not yet arrived at that time; he looked about him and made no sound.

---

"The east," said Guo Ziyi. "Shi Jingzhong, have your men been to look?"

Shi Jingzhong stepped forward and said they had. In the east there were three canals running north–south, dry, with willows on the banks. The first was shallow, the third deepest, and at the far north end there was a sluice a horse could get through. Of these three days, on two mornings there had been fog that did not clear until the hour of *chen*, and over the canals it cleared later.

"Who went down to look?" asked Li Siye.

"He did," said Guo Ziyi, tilting his head back over his shoulder.

All the eyes in the tent turned to Yisi at once. He stepped half a pace forward from behind Guo Ziyi and made his bow.

"The third canal," said Li Siye. "How deep?"

"A little more than a man," said Yisi. "A man standing straight, the bank comes to his eyebrows. A horse with its head down can't be seen from outside."

"How wide?"

"Three horses abreast."

"How long?"

"Going north, we walked more than two *li* and didn't reach the end. The sluice at the north end—my man told me; he's from south of the city and played in it as a boy."

Li Siye said "Mm," and asked no more.

Wang Sili asked: "Which way does the fog come from?"

"Up off the Feng, spreading east. It clears by the hour of *chen*, a little later in the east."

Wang Sili nodded.

Yisi stepped back behind Guo Ziyi and said nothing more.

---

The tent was silent a while.

Pugu Huai'en spoke.

He said that this time the rebels had their backs to Chang'an, with the city behind them; if they could not win in front, they would look for a way to make us pursue. In the fifth month that time, nine thousand horse hid in a hollow; this time there was no hollow in the east, there were canals. Canals would hide horses, and fog would hide banners. If he were An Shouzhong, he would put men in the canals.

"How many," said Pugu Huai'en, "I don't know. But he'll put them there."

No one in the tent contradicted him.

Li Siye said: "Then we don't pursue. I hold in front and let him come and run into me."

Wang Sili said: "Since we've guessed it, let's send a battalion tonight to take those three canals first."

Pugu Huai'en shook his head.

"Take them," he said, "and he won't put men there. He'll put them somewhere else, and then we won't know where."

Guo Ziyi said: "Let him put them there."

Wang Sili thought, and argued no further.

"Once they're in," said Guo Ziyi, "we must have someone watching. When they're seen, we must have someone who can go."

He was looking at Pugu Huai'en.

Pugu Huai'en said: "The Uighur horse are fast."

---

What was settled in the end was this.

Li Siye would lead the Anxi troops and the *mo*-blade companies in front, Wang Sili in the rear, Guo Ziyi the centre. When the vanguard met the enemy, it would hold and not pursue. Anyone who pursued, cut him down.

The horsemen of the two wings would each stay with their own general and were not to move with the vanguard.

The four thousand Uighur horse would be given to Pugu Huai'en and posted west of the centre, by the Feng, where the rebels on the walls could not see them. They would not be used first. When there was movement in the east, they would go round behind the centre.

Those canals in the east belonged to Shi Jingzhong's eastern party, who would lie up there before light. Whatever they saw, one man would ride back first and report, report to the centre and to Pugu Huai'en too; the others would stay and keep watching. If the rebels withdrew, it would again be the scouts who went first to check the ruts and tracks and report to the centre, and until the centre gave the order no one was to move.

The *yabghu* heard the interpreter turn it, looked not best pleased, and said another great string of words. The interpreter turned them over: he still would not agree to being put at the back.

The Prince of Guangping had said little all along; now he spoke. He addressed the *yabghu* very courteously, saying the Uighur troops were the most important troops in this battle and must be used in the most important place. Where the most important place was, no one yet knew; once it was known, the *yabghu* would be the first to be asked to go.

The interpreter turned it, the *yabghu* thought, and nodded.

There the council ended.

---

As the generals left the tent, Pugu Huai'en, reaching the entrance, stopped a moment in front of Shi Jingzhong.

"If there's movement in the east," said Pugu Huai'en, "send someone running to me first. Don't wait for your side to finish looking."

Shi Jingzhong said: "Yes."

As he lifted the curtain he looked back once at Yisi, said nothing, and went out.

Hun Shizhi came after, and as he passed Yisi he put out his fist and thumped him on the shoulder. Not hard. He did not speak; he grinned, his half-missing ear twitched, and he went.

---

Yisi went back to the command tent with Guo Ziyi.

Only one lamp was lit in the tent. Guo Ziyi sat beside it and spread out a map; on it, in ink, were drawn the city of Chang'an, the Feng River, Xiangji Temple. The canals in the east were not on the map.

Guo Ziyi took up a brush and, in the east of the map, drew three short lines. He drew them very slowly; when he had finished he put the brush down and looked at them a while.

"Tomorrow you don't go with the centre," said Guo Ziyi. "Take Wei Three, and pick one more steady man, and before light lie up west of the third canal."

"Yes."

"When you see something, send Wei Three running back first, to report to me and to Pugu too. You stay and keep watching."

"Yes."

"And be steady yourself."

Yisi said: "Yes."

Guo Ziyi bent his head and went back to the map.

Yisi made his bow and withdrew. As he reached the tent entrance he saw Guo Ziyi's saddle hanging on the tent pole, and behind the saddle the bronze basin, the dent in its rim a small black spot under the lamp.

He looked at it once, lifted the tent curtain, and went out.

---

Yisi did not go back to his own tent.

He walked to the east edge of the camp, right to the foot of the outermost ring of palisade, and stopped. Beyond the palisade was the Feng, and beyond the Feng the wheatfields, black, nothing to be seen. Far off in the rebel camp a few firelights flickered, like stars fallen out of the sky.

He tilted his head and listened.

The wind came from the east, cool. In the wind was the smell of wheat stubble, the river smell of the Feng, the smell of horse dung far off.

After a while there were footsteps behind him. Quick footsteps, half a step quicker than most men's.

Old Duan came up beside him and stopped too. He fished half a dry cake out of his coat, broke off a piece and handed it to Yisi.

Yisi took it.

"You're going east tomorrow?" asked Old Duan.

"I am."

Old Duan asked nothing more. He broke off a piece of cake for himself.

The two of them stood at the foot of the palisade, and neither spoke.

Before dawn, the fog rose.
