# Volume Five　Chang'an

## Chapter 21　Relief

> The hungry came and were fed; the cold came and were clothed.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

On the third day after Chang'an was recovered, the day before the grand army marched east, Yisi went to Guo Ziyi to ask for carts.

The wound on his face had not healed. The patch a little left of the middle of his brow, where the skin had been torn off, the surgeon had sewn with needle and thread, a few crooked stitches like a centipede lying there. Half his left eyebrow was gone. The two arrow wounds in his back and side were bound round and round with white cloth, bound so that he could not bend. The surgeon had told him to lie still, lie a full month. He had lain two days.

Guo Ziyi was living in an empty mansion in the city. Who its former owner was, no one knew; while the rebels held the city a false official had lived in it, and the false official had fled, leaving a courtyard full of broken porcelain and a parrot no one fed. The parrot could say one thing, in a Youzhou accent, and no one could understand it.

When Yisi went in, Guo Ziyi was eating gruel. Millet gruel, a dish of pickles, a steamed bun. He gave Yisi a look, and the look was at the centipede.

"You should be lying down."

"I've lain down," said Yisi.

Guo Ziyi said no more about it. He broke the bun open, soaked it in the gruel, and said: "What do you want?"

"Carts."

"What for?"

"South of the city," said Yisi.

Guo Ziyi's chopsticks paused.

Thirty *li* south of the city, north of Xiangji Temple, east of the Feng, that stretch of field where the battle had been fought had still, to this day, not been cleared. The Tang army's own dead the camps had gathered some of, and could not gather all; the rebel dead no one had gathered. The histories say sixty thousand heads; sixty thousand is a number, but lying on the ground it is not a number. There had been a few days of clear weather; the sun had been on them, the wind had been blowing north, and in the wards in the south of the city you could smell it.

"How many?"

"As many as there are."

Guo Ziyi thought and said: "I'll give you two. Old Duan goes with you. He's idle, and when he's idle he drinks."

---

Old Duan in fact was not drinking. He was sitting at the camp gate with a whetstone, grinding that short sword of his; he had been grinding it all morning, the blade had long since been sharp, and he was still grinding. When he heard they were going south of the city, he put the sword back in its sheath, stood up, and slapped the dust off his seat.

"Hauling the dead?"

"Mm."

"The rebels too?"

"Them too."

Old Duan gave Yisi a look, said nothing, and went to yoke the carts.

Two carts, two oxen, four iron spades, a bundle of straw mats. The mats had been found in a half-burned shop in the West Market; their edges were scorched. Old Duan drove one cart, Yisi the other. Yisi could not bend, and to get up onto the cart he needed Old Duan to give him a lift. Old Duan held his arm and said: "The state you're in, when we get there is it you hauling the dead, or the dead hauling you?"

Yisi said: "They don't move."

Old Duan thought a moment and laughed, and when he laughed only half his face moved.

Out of the Mingde Gate they went south. In the fields on either side of the road the wheat stubble was still standing; no one had brought in the autumn harvest. Some farmhouses had fallen in, with black marks of smoke on the walls. A dog followed the carts a way, so thin its ribs showed one by one; it followed as far as a ditch, then stopped following, and sat there watching them.

When they got there, neither of them spoke.

---

Yisi had been back and forth across these fields several times, the first two times in fog. What he remembered was the canals. The first, the second, the third. He remembered warm horse dung in the canal, remembered the canal where Shi Six went down, the sluice where Wei Three went down, remembered the Uighurs whooping like wolves.

He did not remember there being so many people on the ground.

In the fog you could not see. Now the fog was gone, the sky was very high and very blue, and an old crow was circling slowly overhead, circling, coming down, flying up again. The men on the ground lay some face down, some face up, some only half there. The bright armour of the Tang soldiers had been stripped off, and the leather armour of the rebels too. Who had stripped it, there was no knowing—farmers from nearby, perhaps, deserters, perhaps, perhaps other men who had lived through it. With the armour stripped, there was no telling which were Tang and which were rebels.

Old Duan squatted down and looked at one dead man's face, then another's.

"Can't tell," he said.

Yisi said: "Look at the hair."

There were many *hu* in the rebel army—Tongluo, Xi, Khitan—and they braided their hair differently. But not all of them. There were *hu* in the Tang army too—Uighurs, Tiele, Arabs—and they braided theirs differently too. Old Duan squatted there looking a while, stood up and said: "Still can't tell."

Yisi thought and said: "Those we can tell, we bury apart. Those we can't, we bury together."

"Bury them together—would they want that?"

Yisi did not answer. He took up a spade, stood at the edge of the first canal, and began to dig.

He dug very slowly. With the cloth wound round his waist he could not bend, and could only work with his arms, a spadeful at a time. He had not dug many spadefuls before the cloth on his back was wet, a small patch gone red. Old Duan saw it, came over and snatched the spade away, and said: "You drag. I'll dig."

So Yisi dragged.

Dragging needed bending too. He thought of a way: lay a straw mat on the ground, roll a man onto the mat, then take one end of the mat and pull it to the edge of the canal. The mat rasped over the stubble. He went very slowly, went a little, stopped, drew breath, went on.

The first day, the two of them buried forty-one.

---

The second day three men came.

They were farmers from south of the city, an old man and his two sons. The old man was past sixty, bent-backed, earth in every wrinkle of his face. He stood on the ridge between the fields watching a long time, watched Yisi dragging, watched Old Duan digging, then came over and asked: "Is the government paying?"

Old Duan said: "No."

The old man said "Oh," squatted down, and smoked his pipe a while. He did not leave. His two sons stood behind him and did not leave either.

When he had finished his pipe, the old man stuck it in his belt, stood up, took a spade from Old Duan's hands and said: "You're digging this pit wrong. Too shallow. Shallow, and the dogs dig them out."

He dug a pit to show Old Duan. It was dug square and true, its sides straight, as deep as his chest.

Old Duan looked at it and said: "You've done this before, uncle?"

"I have," said the old man. "In the Kaiyuan years there was a plague in Guanzhong. I buried people then."

He did not say how many.

That day the five of them buried a hundred and thirty.

The third day a dozen or more came. The fifth day, more than forty. Some were farmers, some were from the city, some no one knew where from; they came and took up a spade and said nothing. Yisi asked a young man where his home was. The young man said in the Changle Ward. Yisi asked why he had come. The young man said: my elder brother was in the rebel army—they took him—I don't know where he died. I've come to look.

He came every day, and every day he looked. He looked for half a month, and did not find him.

---

With more people, there had to be food.

On the third night Yisi went back into the city, to the Yining Ward.

The Yining Ward was in the west of the city, by the Kaiyuan Gate. In the ward there was a monastery built by imperial order in the Zhenguan years, and Emperor Taizong had given it a tablet. When Yisi came from the west at seventeen, it was this monastery's gate he had walked through. He had lived here more than ten years; here he had been made a priest, here he had married, here he had carried home a child in his arms.

The tablet was gone. On the lintel there had been a cross carved, its four ends rounded like four leaves. Now someone had chiselled away those four leaves, leaving only a cross-shaped hollow in the middle, with dust collected in it.

Horses had been stabled in the courtyard; the ground was covered with dried dung. The main hall was half burned; the pillars on the east still stood, those on the west had fallen in, and there was a great hole in the roof through which you could see the sky. The jujube tree in the back courtyard was still there, its leaves all fallen, its branches spread like an open hand. The well under the jujube tree was still there too.

Yisi drew up a bucket of water. The water was clear, and very cold.

He stood by the well a long time.

Then he went back to the front courtyard and, in a half-burned side room, found a big cauldron. There was a crack across its bottom, but it would still serve. He carried it out and set it up at the monastery gate, then went and pulled down a few charred rafters and split them for firewood.

Early the next morning, smoke rose at the monastery gate.

---

The gruel was millet, thin. The millet was Guo Ziyi's. When Yisi went to ask for it, Guo Ziyi asked how much he wanted. Yisi said: as much as there is. Guo Ziyi looked at him a while and said: "You—when you want something, it's always that one sentence."

Yisi said: "That sentence works."

Guo Ziyi gave him twenty *dan*. Later he gave him another twenty.

The first day only seven or eight people came for gruel. All were people of the ward who had not got away—the old, the sick, women with children. They stood outside the monastery gate looking at the cauldron from a distance, not daring to come near. They could see that chiselled hollow on the lintel; they could see the *hu* by the cauldron with a centipede sewn on his face.

Yisi ladled out a bowl, carried it over and handed it to the woman nearest him. She had a child in her arms; the child did not cry, did not move, its eyes were open.

The woman took the bowl and fed the child first.

The second day more than thirty came. The third day, over a hundred.

One cauldron was not enough, and Yisi found another. One man could not keep watch over both, so Old Duan, who dug pits south of the city by day, came back at night to help him with the cauldrons. Old Duan kept watch over a cauldron the way he kept watch over a camp gate: stood by it, sword in his belt, and glared at anyone who pushed. When he glared, the scar on his face pulled, and no one dared push.

Yisi said: "Don't frighten them."

Old Duan said: "I'm not frightening them. I was born looking like this."

---

It grew cold.

In the eleventh month Chang'an had its first snow. Among those who came for gruel some were barefoot, some wore only a single layer, their lips purple with cold, their hands shaking so that half the gruel spilled from their bowls.

When Yisi went south of the city, he began bringing back a big basket.

Old Duan asked him what was in the basket, and Yisi would not say. Old Duan lifted the lid and looked. It was clothes. Torn, dirty; some had dried blood on them, some had gashes cut by blades.

Old Duan put the lid back on.

He said nothing. That night he saw Yisi at the well in the back courtyard washing those clothes one by one. The well water was very cold, and Yisi's hands were soaked red. He washed very slowly, one garment, wrung it out, hung it on the branches of the jujube. The branches were hung full, as if the tree had grown a whole new crown of leaves—grey, brown, black.

The next day those clothes were on the backs of some of the people at the monastery gate.

The people who wore them knew where they had come from. No one said.

---

The sick came too.

Some had fevers, some had the flux, some had sores breaking out on them, some had been cut down in the chaos and their wounds had festered and ran yellow. Yisi was not a physician. All he knew was how to change a dressing.

He had learned that at Changshan. The cut on his right shoulder had gone to the bone, and Du De had changed his dressing for twenty days. Du De's hands were very rough, every knuckle cracked, but when he changed a dressing they were very light. First soak the old cloth soft with warm water and peel it off bit by bit; where it would not come, don't force it, soak it again. Then wash the wound with salt water, and when it is clean, sprinkle on the powder, and bind it with new cloth. When you bind it, not too tight—too tight and the blood won't move; not too loose either—too loose and it falls off.

Yisi had lain on his front on the bed then, unable to see anything, only able to listen. He had listened for twenty days, and remembered.

Now he did as Du De had done. First soak with warm water, then peel bit by bit; what would not come, he did not force. Salt water to wash, powder sprinkled, new cloth to bind. Not tight, not loose.

The powder he begged from the surgeon in Guo Ziyi's camp. The surgeon gave him a small packet and said: that's all there is, the camp hasn't enough itself. Yisi said: it's enough. The surgeon looked at the centipede on his face and said: that wound of yours ought to have its dressing changed too.

Yisi said: it has been.

It had not. He used the whole packet at the monastery gate.

---

The fields south of the city were all buried.

How many were buried in all, Yisi did not count. The old man counted. The old man said: of the Tang army, more than two thousand three hundred; of the rebels, over four thousand; of those no one could tell, more than a thousand. And there were some who could no longer be buried, and could only be covered with earth where they lay.

At the end of the last day's work the old man sat on the ridge between the fields smoking his pipe. His two sons were patting the last pit flat by the canal. Yisi sat beside him. Old Duan was washing the spades some way off.

It was nearly dark. The sky in the west was red, a red going purple. The old crow was still circling overhead.

The old man finished one pipe, filled another, and did not light it. He looked at that stretch of new-turned earth, looked at it a long time.

"*Hu* master," said the old man. "Which house are you of?"

Yisi did not answer at once.

"I've heard folk in the ward say," said the old man, "that monastery of yours isn't a Buddhist temple, and it isn't a Daoist shrine either. That thing carved on the gate isn't the swastika."

"It's a cross," said Yisi.

"What's a cross?"

Yisi thought and said: "It's where a man died."

The old man was taken aback and turned his head to look at him.

"Died on a cross?"

"Mm."

"That's how criminals die."

"Mm."

The old man said nothing. He lit the pipe, drew on it once, and again, and the smoke came slowly out of his nostrils and in the cold wind was gone at once.

"You worship a man who died like that?"

Yisi said: "Afterwards he rose again."

This time the old man was not taken aback. He looked at Yisi, looked at him a while, and his eyes narrowed, as if he were looking at a young man talking big.

"Rose again?"

"Rose again."

The old man bent his head and looked at the new earth. It was yellow, lumpy, with bits of stubble mixed in. His two sons had finished patting the pit and stood there waiting for him.

"And these men?" said the old man. "These men—does he take care of them?"

Yisi looked at the earth too.

"He does," he said.

"The Tang ones, and the rebels too?"

Yisi did not speak at once. The wind came from the north and drew tight the centipede on his face.

"They're all his," he said.

The old man asked no more. He stood up, stuck his pipe back in his belt, nodded to Yisi, and went off with his two sons. After a few steps he looked back and said: "I'll come again tomorrow. That cauldron at your monastery gate—you're short of firewood. I've got some."

Yisi said: "All right."

---

That night, when Yisi got back to the Yining Ward, he did not go in at once.

He stood at the monastery gate and looked up at the chiselled hollow on the lintel. The dust in it had been soaked by snowmelt and become a black streak running down the doorframe.

The ward was very dark. All Chang'an was very dark. It was more than a month since the city had been recovered, and still few houses lit lamps. Those that had oil could not bear to burn it; those that had none could not. Standing at the monastery gate and looking east, ward after ward of roofs, black, dense, black all the way to the edge of the sky; only very far off, over by the Imperial City, a few points of fire—the night watch.

Yisi went in, searched the side rooms a while, and found a little oil saucer that had once held a lamp for the monastery's offerings—bronze, green with verdigris. He wiped it with his sleeve, poured a little lamp oil into it, twisted a wick and laid it in.

He took his fire-steel from his coat and struck it three times, and it caught.

The flame was very small, the size of a bean, tilting this way and that in the wind. He cupped his hands round it, cupped them a while, and the flame steadied.

He set the lamp on the step at the monastery gate, under the lintel, right below that cross-shaped hollow.

Then he went in.
