# Volume Seven　A-Lian

## Chapter 40　The Letter

> Thereupon one Person of our Trinity, the Luminous Lord Mishihe, folded away His true majesty and came into the world in the likeness of men.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

Yonan looked after the porter's child for seven days.

The first three days he went every day. The child's fever went down, came up, went down. On the fourth day the child sat up and clutched at his mother's clothes for the breast. On the fifth day the child smiled at Yonan and showed two little teeth. On the seventh day Yonan felt the child's forehead, and it was cool, and he closed his bag of medicines and told the porter there was no need for him to come again.

Those seven days, every day when he came out of that lane, he turned east, went two streets, and sat a while in that bread shop.

She brought him a bowl of hot water, broke a flatbread, half for him, half for herself. They did not talk much. She told of things in the shop—which porter had put it on the slate again, which *hu* merchant complained that her bread was baked too dark. He told of things at the monastery—Babai's eyes were worse again, Ishaq was fatter again. And as they talked they stopped talking, and the two of them sat listening to the charcoal crackling in the oven.

When the drum beat its first round, he got up and went.

On the seventh day he went to the door, turned his head and said: "Tomorrow I won't come."

She was stacking bread; her hands paused, and went on stacking.

"Mm," she said.

---

Yisi came back to Chang'an at the beginning of the fourth month.

He was fifty that year, his hair still cut very short, but in the short hair there was a great deal of white now. He had come from Hezhong to take a memorial of Lord Guo's into the palace, and was staying in Chang'an only five days.

On the first evening he was at the monastery, Yonan came to find him.

Yisi was washing his face at the well outside the monastery gate. The well was under the old locust tree, and the tree had just put out new leaves, very small and tender, grey in the dusk. Yisi drew a bucket of water, plunged his face into it, kept it there a while, raised his head and shook off the water.

Yonan stood behind him and waited until he had finished.

Yisi wiped his face with a cloth and turned round. He looked at Yonan once, asked nothing, and moved the wooden bucket aside to leave a space on the well curb.

Yonan went over and sat down on the well curb.

Yisi sat down too.

The two of them sat with their backs to the well under the locust tree, sat a long time. Yonan spoke first.

He told what had happened this month. The porter, the child, that lane, that bread shop, that little plank of accounts. He told it very slowly, sometimes in Tang, sometimes in Syriac, and by the end it was all Syriac. Yisi listened all the way through and did not put in a word.

When Yonan had finished he sat a while longer.

"I want to write to the Patriarchate," he said, "and ask to be released from my vow."

Yisi did not answer at once. He bowed his head and looked at his two hands. The backs were all old callus, and on the knuckles were a few old scars left from these years in the army.

After a while he raised his head.

"Does she want it?" he asked.

Yonan was struck dumb.

"I haven't asked," he said.

Yisi nodded.

"Ask her first," he said, "then write."

He stood up, picked up the wooden bucket, and poured the water left in it into the earth under the locust tree. He poured it very slowly; the water soaked in bit by bit, and the earth darkened in a ring.

"When you write," he said, "write it as it was."

He paused.

"What's shameful is the lie."

---

The next day Yonan went to the West Market again.

She saw him come in the door and was taken aback. He did not sit, but stood by that low table and said what he had to say. He said it haltingly; there were several words he had to think a long time to find. When he had finished, he stood there with his two hands hanging at his sides, like that man of thirty years before, new to Chang'an, unable to say one whole sentence in Tang.

She sat by the oven and heard him out, and for a good while said nothing.

The charcoal in the oven crackled once.

"I'm fifty," she said.

"I'm seventy," said Yonan.

She looked at him.

"Write, then," she said. "If they allow it, I'll marry you. If they don't, you'll come and eat bread just the same."

She stood up, broke a flatbread, and handed him half.

---

Yonan wrote that letter over three nights.

He shut the door, lit a small oil saucer, spread out the paper, and wrote in Syriac. He began from coming to Chang'an in the third year of Tianbao: wrote of that porter, the coughing in the lane, that lamp, that basin of warm water, the four letters on that wooden tablet and the character *heart*. Wrote of her coming to find him, of what she had said, of her carrying the child to knock at Yisi's door. Wrote that the day he left it was snowing, and he had not gone to see her.

He wrote of the child.

He wrote where the child was now, what his name was, what he was doing. When he came to this, he stopped a long time; the oil in the saucer was filled once. He wanted to strike this passage out. He lifted his pen, looked at it a while, and put it down again.

He did not strike it out.

He wrote of these thirty years. Wrote that after he went back to the Patriarchate he asked for the lowest office; that he copied scripture at the Patriarchate, ten years of copying; that when he heard Chang'an had fallen he asked to go east; wrote of the Pamirs, of Xizhou, of how one night in Xizhou he heard a child's footsteps pass along the corridor.

At the end, he wrote: I broke my vow, and hid it thirty years. I do not ask to be pardoned; I ask only to set the truth before you. If it is not allowed, I will keep my vow as before, unchanged until I die. If it is allowed, I beg the Patriarch to pray for her; she is one of the Lord's sheep.

When he had finished, it was nearly dawn.

He folded the letter, tied it with fine cord, and dripped wax on it. He pressed the seal down, sat there, and looked at the letter a long time.

Then he got up and washed his hands.

---

All the elders of the monastery came to know of this letter.

It was Yonan himself who told them. He took the sealed letter to the elder Azarel and, before several of the elders, told the gist of what was in it. He said this letter was to go with the monastery's official letters; he did not want to do it behind anyone's back.

When Azarel had heard it, he sat there a long while without speaking.

The elder Marya kept his head down, his fingers tapping on his knee, once and again. The elder Samaan looked out of the window. Ishaq opened his mouth, kept it open a long time, and at last took a dry flatbread from his coat, broke off half, and laid it by Yonan's hand.

At last Azarel spoke. He said only one word.

"Send it."

That afternoon in the east wing, Jingjing, Babai and Gabriel translated as usual. Narsai sat under the window cutting paper as usual.

Narsai had heard about Yonan at table. When he had heard it he put down his bowl and said one thing.

"The vow was made to the Lord," he said.

When he had said it he never opened his mouth again. Everyone at the table looked at him, and he looked at no one, picked up his bowl, and drank the rest of his gruel.

Now, in the east wing, he was cutting paper. The knife point against the bamboo rule, drawn from one end to the other, one stroke, another stroke. Jingjing raised his head and glanced at him; he did not raise his.

The paper he cut that day still had edges as straight as the surface of water.

---

The letter went at the beginning of the fifth month.

That year the Hexi road was impassable; Liangzhou had fallen to the Tibetans some years before. Letters going west had to go round through the Uighur lands, across the steppe in the north, to Suyab, then south over the Pamirs into the Two Rivers. Going round like that, it was half a year there and back at quickest, a year or two at slowest, and some went on and on and were never heard of again.

The day the caravan left the city, Yonan did not go to see it off. He said the morning office in the front hall, and when it was done went and sat by the door of the east wing writing in his sand tray.

What he wrote that day was not *heart*. Jingjing passed by him and glanced down.

In the sand tray were written two characters: *weakness*. The 軟 was still written all in pieces.

---

The summer passed.

Yonan did not go to the West Market again. As always he washed his hands three times a day, ate half a flatbread at each meal, and sat in the courtyard at night, looking east, looking south.

But every seven days the porter came to the monastery.

The first time, he carried a bamboo basket with a cloth over it, stood at the monastery gate, and said the auntie at the West Market bread shop had sent him with it. Yonan lifted the cloth; in the basket were four *hu* flatbreads, still warm.

Under the cloth was a small plank, on which a few Syriac letters were written in charcoal. Not Syriac words—the sound of a Tang word.

*Hot.*

Yonan held the plank in his hand and looked at it a long time.

From then on, every Lord's Day, the porter came. In the basket sometimes there was bread, sometimes a jar of mutton soup, the jar wrapped in cotton wadding, still too hot to hold when it reached the monastery. What was written on the plank was different every time. Once it said: *The weather's turned cool.* Once it said: *The child can walk.* She meant the porter's child.

Once nothing was written, only a curving hook and three dots.

Yonan kept all the planks, one by one, stacked on the windowsill of his room.

When he ate the bread, he still ate only half. The other half he crumbled and laid under the locust tree outside the monastery gate for the sparrows.

Ishaq saw it and said: "Yonan, she sent it for you."

Yonan said: "Half is for her."

Ishaq did not understand. Nor did he ask again.

---

The answer came in the eleventh month.

That day the first snow fell. Not heavy—flake by flake drifting down, melting as it touched the ground. A deacon who had come with a caravan, wrapped in wind and snow, came in at the monastery gate, took an oilcloth packet from inside his robe, and gave it to the elder Azarel.

In the oilcloth packet were several letters. Some for Azarel, some for the monastery, one for Yisi. The one at the very bottom had Yonan's name on its cover.

The letter had been sent to Ctesiphon. But the answer came from Baghdad.

The deacon said the Patriarchate had moved this year. The Patriarch Hnanisho had moved the patriarchal see to the new city of Baghdad. The new city was very large, its wall round, its streets straight. The people of the Patriarchate had moved in, and many still did not know their way about.

Yonan took the letter.

The wax seal on its cover was new, stamped with the Patriarch's seal. He touched the seal with his finger, and did not break it.

He put the letter inside his robe and went back to his room. The door shut.

He was in his room all afternoon. No one went to knock.

After dark, the door opened. Yonan came out and went to the door of the east wing. Jingjing was inside putting the scrolls away, sorting the half-translated drafts sheet by sheet and pressing them under the paperweight.

Yonan stood at the door holding the opened letter.

"Read it to her for me," he said. "In Tang."

Jingjing's hands stopped.

He raised his head and looked at Yonan. Yonan stood in the dark at the door, his face not clear; all that could be seen were the sheets of the letter in his hand, white.

Jingjing set the paperweight in its place and stood up.

"Yes," he said.

---

A-Lian came the next evening.

The porter had taken word to the West Market. When she came it was already dusk, and the lamp at the monastery gate had just been lit. She stood under the lamp, stood a while, and did not come in at once.

She wore a clean blue cotton jacket, her hair coiled up afresh, a new wooden pin in it. There was no flour on her hands; they were washed spotless.

She bowed her head and stepped over the threshold.

It was the first time in twenty-nine years she had come through this gate.

They were in the front hall. Two lamps were lit in the hall, and the wooden cross hung on the east wall. Yonan sat on one side, Yisi on the other. Yisi had only that morning ridden in from Hezhong, and the mud on his boots was not yet dry.

Jingjing stood beneath the cross with the sheets of the letter in his hands.

A-Lian came in and stopped near the door. Yonan rose and showed her to a rush cushion. She sat down, sat in the place of the last row.

There was no one else in the hall.

Jingjing looked down at the letter. Syriac, line after line, right to left. He knew the Patriarch's hand: very steady, not large, every stroke finished very clean.

He began to read. As he looked, he turned it into Tang in his head, and read it out sentence by sentence.

"Hnanisho, servant of the Lord, by the Lord's mercy in the new city of Baghdad, to our brother Yonan in Chang'an: the peace of the Lord be with you.

"Your letter I have read three times. The first time I read it as Patriarch. The second time as your brother. The third time as an old man, as old as you.

"You say you broke your vow, and hid it thirty years. The hiding was your sin. The telling is the Lord's grace.

"When a man makes a vow before the Lord, the Lord remembers. When a man is weak before the Lord, the Lord remembers that too. God has regard to man's weakness. For he knoweth our frame; he remembereth that we are dust.

"I release you from your vow."

When Jingjing had read this far, he paused.

The hall was very quiet. The lamp wick spat.

He did not raise his head, and read on.

"You might have stayed at the Patriarchate and copied scripture in peace to the end of your days. Instead you walked ten thousand *li* through fire and sword, to go to the east. The church in the east has not scattered amid the swords, its scriptures have not been burned, its lamps have not gone out, and the merit is yours with theirs. The church owes you more than you owe the church.

"In your letter you speak of that child. I hear he is now translating scripture in Chang'an. May the Lord keep his hands and his eyes."

Jingjing's voice did not change. Still the same, reading it out word by word, not fast, not slow.

But when he had read this sentence, his fingers pinched the edge of the paper and creased it.

A-Lian, sitting in the last row, raised her head and looked at him.

He did not look at her. He read on.

"Greet the sister A-Lian for me. I do not know her face, nor can I speak her tongue. I hear she knows our letters. I have written her name below, in our letters. If she can read it, let her know there is an old man in Baghdad who prays for her every day.

"I am old, and cannot walk far. The streets of the new city are very straight, and still I often take the wrong one. The peace of the Lord be with you all."

---

When Jingjing had finished, he turned the sheet over.

At the very bottom of the sheet, below the Patriarch's signature, a new line had been begun, with four letters written in it. Right to left.

He looked at those four letters a while. Then he came down from under the cross, went to the last row, and squatted down before A-Lian.

He held the sheet out to her and pointed at that line with his finger.

"Sister," he said, "this is your name."

A-Lian bowed her head.

She looked at those four letters a long time. Then she put out her finger and traced along them, right to left, one by one. She traced very slowly.

"*Alaph*," she read, "*Lamadh*, *Yudh*, *Nun*."

She read in a Chang'an accent. The tail of the last turned up.

Jingjing squatted before her without moving. He was very close to her, close enough to see the scars on the backs of her hands, the lines at the corners of her eyes, her eyes.

Her eyes were very black.

When she had finished reading, she raised her head and looked at him.

Jingjing stood up. He folded the letter and held it out to Yonan with both hands.

"Elder," he said, "I've finished reading."

---

Yisi stood up, went to the door, and pushed it open.

The snow had stopped outside, a thin layer of white on the ground. The lamp at the monastery gate was still burning; a little snow had settled on its paper cover, and the light came through yellow and fell on the stone block before the gate.

Someone was standing in the gallery.

It was Narsai. How long he had been standing there no one knew; there was snow on his shoulders. He saw the door open, saw Yisi, said nothing at all, turned and went towards the east wing. Afterwards a little lamplight showed in the window of that room in the east wing, and stayed lit half the night.

Yisi watched his back a while, then turned and said to those in the hall: "It's late. The ward gate will be shutting."

Yonan rose to see A-Lian out. The two of them went to the gate and walked out under the lamp, their shadows trailing on the snow, one long, one short.

---

Jingjing did not see her out.

He went out of the monastery gate alone, to the foot of the locust tree.

The ward was very dark, the branches of the locust bare, a little unmelted snow hanging on them. He went to the well, let down the wooden bucket, and drew up a bucket of water.

The water was very cold. A thin crust of ice clung to the rim of the bucket.

He cupped a handful in his hands and drank it.

He cupped another handful and drank it.

That night his stomach hurt half the night. He curled up alone under the quilt, biting the corner of it, and made no sound.

The next morning he went to the east wing to translate as usual. Babai asked him why he was so pale, and he said he had not slept well.

He told no one.
