# Volume Nine　The Translation Hall

## Chapter 59　Li Su

> Its words are not prolix; its reasoning, once grasped, can do without the trap.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

In the spring of the seventh year of Yuanhe, Jingjing began to translate the *Sutra of Profound Peace and Joy*. Of the Syriac scriptures the Patriarchate had sent, this was the hardest: it was the Messiah in question and answer with his chief disciple Simon; Simon asked how a man might come to peace and joy, and the Messiah answered at great length, saying a man must put away desire, keep stillness, not contend, cling neither to being nor to non-being; when the heart was empty, peace and joy would come. Elder Babai had once read it through to him, and when he finished had said: this one, translate when you're old. He was in his thirties then and did not understand why Babai said it; now he was sixty-six, opened it, read it through once, and understood: for every word this scripture said, the Tang books already had characters ready-made; in the Daoist books they were waiting line by line; if he used those characters, what he translated a Tang reader would understand at a glance, and, understanding it, would take it for one more Daoist scripture; if he did not use them, a Tang reader would understand not one line.

Lingbao held the brush for him. Lingbao was forty-five that year; David had laid hands on him and made him priest; the monastery's accounts and the keys of the scripture cabinets were all in his hands, yet the brush in the scripture room he still meant to hold. The two brothers sat facing each other across a long table; Jingjing read the Syriac, read a line, thought, said a line in Tang; Lingbao wrote it down, and when he had written it read it back to him, and he listened and matched it against the Syriac; what matched stayed, what did not, he thought again—the method in Alopen's notebook of walking back the other way, which he had used for more than twenty years. When they reached the line "without desire, without striving," Jingjing stopped, and Lingbao's brush hung waiting for him. He thought for as long as a meal takes, and said: write it—without desire, without striving; Lingbao wrote it, and when he had written it put a dot beside it with the brush. When they reached "who would have peace and joy must first put away desire," another dot. When they reached "as a mirror reflects a thing: the thing gone, the image vanishes," another dot. When one page was done, Lingbao counted: that page had a hundred-odd characters, and beside them were eleven dots. Lingbao said: Brother, in this one there'll be more dots than characters. Jingjing said: dot them; dot them, and those who come after will see them and know this was borrowed; if they can find characters of their own, they'll change them; if they can't, let these stay for now.

That year the character he wrote most often on his board was *quan*, the fish-trap. That line on the stele he had written himself—its words are not prolix; its reasoning, once grasped, can do without the trap—praising, as Taizong's edict did, Alopen's teaching: its words not many, its reason reached, the trap could be forgotten. When he wrote it he took it for a fine phrase; now every morning facing that character he thought: the trap is a bamboo basket for catching fish; when the fish is caught you can throw the basket away; but in his whole life the fish had not yet been caught, and the basket was borrowed; throw away a borrowed basket, and there's nothing left at all.

---

In those years, the monastery's old men went one by one.

The first was Haddad. He was the one of the seven who had dealt with the outside—dark-skinned, a man of the southern seacoast, who spoke several tongues; Yonan's secret he had known since Xizhou, and all his life said nothing. When he died he was past eighty; his hearing was still good, he heard everything; before he died he called Jingjing and told him: that year in Xizhou, your Ma's business, he knew; your Pa Yonan's business, he knew too; he didn't tell you because Elder Yonan begged him not to; Elder Yonan said: wait till the child asks. He said he'd waited his whole life, and you never once asked him; what you asked about was your Pa Yisi. When he had said it he smiled, and said that all his life what he was best at was talking, and what he did most was not talking.

The second was Gabriel. The burn on his left cheek had puckered into a knot in his old age; his voice had gone years ago, and he could no longer tell stories. Before he died he called Jingfu; Jingfu was seventeen that year and sat by his bed; Gabriel took his hand and, in Syriac, word by word, told him the story of the prodigal: how the younger son wasted his substance and ate the husks the swine ate in the sty, came to himself, rose and went to his father, and when he was yet a great way off his father saw him, and had compassion, and ran, and fell on his neck, and kissed him. When Gabriel came to this he could not go on, and Jingfu went on in Tang; he had studied Syriac ten years and still did not speak it well, but his Tang was better than anyone's. When he finished, Gabriel was already asleep, and the burn on his face had smoothed out a little.

The third was Babai. He had been blind several years, and every day sat at the door of the scripture room in the sun, listening to Lingbao read; when he came to the rolls Babai had copied in his day, Babai read along, and knew them better than Lingbao. He died at the door of the scripture room, in the afternoon, the sun just right; Lingbao, halfway through a reading, turned to look, and he was leaning against the doorframe, the cracks of his nails still black—a lifetime of ink.

The fourth was Marya. He was the elder of preaching who had come with Azarel and Samaan; round-faced, round-bellied, with a voice like a bell; he had preached his voice hoarse, and stayed hoarse forty years; before he died his voice suddenly cleared, and lying in bed he sang a hymn, sang it in Tang—*In the highest of the heavens, deep reverence and praise; the great earth turns again to universal peace*—and when he had sung it he told Jingjing: this one was yours to translate, his to test; when he tested you, you were only twenty, and that day he'd thought: this child's Tang is better than his own Syriac.

The last was Sabrisho. He was the one of the seven who kept the rule, his beard like felt; all his life when he said no it was no; Jingjing had been Father of the Brethren thirty years and argued with him thirty years. When he died he was nearly ninety, lying in bed remembering nothing—not people, not events, not his own name—remembering only the rule; he recited it article by article: the rule of the offering once in seven, the rule of baptism, the rule of the council; and at last he came to that time in the winter of the first year of Yuanhe, and said: you break it. Jingjing sat by the bed, thinking he had recognized him, and called him Elder; Sabrisho looked at him and said: you break it. Jingjing took from his robe a small piece of the offering bread, left over in the hall that morning, broke it, and put half in his mouth; Sabrisho held it in his mouth, and holding it, closed his eyes.

Of the seven who had come east from Ctesiphon, by the tenth year of Yuanhe not one was left. In the burial ground outside the Kaiyuan Gate, the monastery's people lay at the eastern end, in a row: Yisi, Azarel, Narsai, Yonan, A-Lian, Shiyiniang, Xu Jing, Isaac, Samaan, Haddad, Gabriel, Babai, Marya, Sabrisho, David; and further east, Baaluo. Jingjing went once each year at Cold Food, stood a while at each grave in turn, and by the time he stood at the last one the sky was nearly dark.

---

In the eleventh year of Yuanhe, Jingjing began to write a list of scriptures. The scriptures the Patriarchate had sent, in Syriac, numbered five hundred and thirty in all, heaped in the scripture room in a few cases of *beipi*, of bark-skin; the cases Alopen had brought from the west long ago; the leather had stiffened, the brass clasps rusted, and when one was opened it gave a creak. How many he had translated in his life he had never counted himself; Lingbao counted for him, and the count came to thirty. He wrote them out one by one as Lingbao had counted them: the scripture's name, its Tang name, the number of rolls; and when he came to the end he stopped, thought, and wrote one line after: "The scriptures of the original teaching of Daqin number in all five hundred and thirty, all on palm leaves in the Brahmin tongue. Jingjing has translated the above thirty; the great remainder lies in the bark-skin cases, not yet translated."

Lingbao beside him looked at that line and said: Brother—five hundred; thirty. Jingjing said: thirty; five hundred. Lingbao said: the five hundred to come—who'll translate them? Jingjing said: you will; when you can't finish, Jingfu will; when Jingfu can't finish, Jingfu's disciples will. Lingbao looked at those few bark-skin cases. Jingfu was twenty-one that year and sat in the doorway teaching Tang characters to a few children of the ward—water, fire, gate, road—with the board Jingjing had once whittled for him.

Those few mornings, what Jingjing wrote was *yu*, remainder. What he read was Ecclesiastes: "What profit hath a man of all his labour which he taketh under the sun?"

---

In the autumn of the twelfth year of Yuanhe, the scripture room was sealed. The business began in the palace. In these years the Son of Heaven loved immortals and sought long life, and many Daoists went in and out of the palace; one, surnamed Wang, had come out of the Zhongnan mountains saying he could make the golden elixir, won favour, and often spoke before the throne. In the seventh month he offered a word before the throne: that in Chang'an there was a *hu* monastery whose *hu* monks had copied the scriptures of Laozi, changed their faces and heads, and written them out as their own scriptures; the scriptures spoke of non-action, of peace and joy, of putting away desire and keeping stillness, every word and line the Daoists' language, and yet said to come from Daqin; this was stealing Laozi's Way and passing it off as a *hu* teaching; he asked the Son of Heaven to send down an edict to examine and ban the Tang-language scriptures transmitted by the *hu* monastery. The Son of Heaven said neither yes nor no, only that the office concerned should go and look. The office concerned was the Commissioners of Merit. At the beginning of the eighth month a judge came from the Commissioners' office with six men, came in at the monastery gate, went straight to the scripture room, carried the Tang-language scriptures out cabinet by cabinet and heaped them in the courtyard, and after most of a day's turning over turned up that roll of the *Sutra of Profound Peace and Joy*—the very roll Jingjing and Lingbao were halfway through. The judge held it, read a few lines—"without desire, without striving; able to be clear, able to be pure"—and when he had read them looked at Jingjing and said: Great Worthy Jing, these lines—are they Laozi's words or your Lord's? Jingjing said they were their Lord's words, in Laozi's characters. The judge said: use Laozi's characters, and they're Laozi's words. He had his men pack all the Tang scriptures in the scripture room back into the cabinets, pasted seals on the cabinet doors and a seal on the scripture room door, and said they must wait for the palace's decision.

That night no one in the monastery slept; they sat at the scripture room door. Lingbao said he would go and find people; there were still a few names in Official Pei's register; General Luo's son was in the Shence Army, and the Mi family's grandson too. Jingjing said: find them; only one at a time. He himself went to no one; he sat at the scripture room door all night, and when the sky was nearly light he got up and stood in the middle of the courtyard and told his knotted cord, and when he had told it, he could not get into the scripture room; but he could recite the scriptures, so he stood in the courtyard and recited; he recited that psalm, "I will lift up mine eyes unto the hills, from whence cometh my help"; and when he had recited it he had no board; the board was locked in the scripture room; he wrote with his finger in the frost on the well curb the character *shan*, hills, and when he had written it the frost melted, and the character was gone.

After half a month the judge came again, and said the palace wanted the monastery to explain itself: which were your words and which Laozi's. Jingjing asked him to come to the scripture room door; he said the room was sealed and he could not go in; if the judge wished to see, let him break the seals, and he would show him roll by roll. The judge had the seals broken; Jingjing carried the scriptures out roll by roll and spread them on the long table in the courtyard, and turned them over roll by roll for the judge; at every page he pointed to the little dots beside the characters and told the judge: every one with a dot is a borrowed character—borrowed from the Buddhist monks, borrowed from the Daoists, borrowed from the Confucians; he had dotted them thirty years and not missed one; those without dots were their own words—Aluohe, Mishihe, the Luminous Honoured One, the Three-in-One, Pure Wind—these characters were not in Laozi's scriptures. He said he had never said these characters were their own; he borrowed them, and noted that they were borrowed; borrowing characters is not stealing; a thief doesn't put dots. The judge held that roll of the *Sutra of Profound Peace and Joy*, looked at the eleven little dots on that page, turned on page by page, and at last looked up and asked Jingjing: what do you put these dots for? Jingjing said: as marks, so those who come after will know that here it's borrowed; later, when they find characters of their own, they'll change them. The judge asked nothing more; he had the scriptures put back in the cabinets; the seals had been broken, and were not pasted again; he said he would go back and report.

After the scripture room was unsealed Lingbao told him: a few days before, he had gone to the Directorate of Astronomy, and the people there said Lord Li was sick, but had heard of this business, and had had a word passed into the palace: that this autumn Mars was trespassing on the Heart lodge, and by the stars it was not fitting to destroy books and scriptures; destroy them, and it portended sickness in the palace. Lingbao said whether that word reached the Son of Heaven he did not know; he knew only that the people at the Directorate said that when Lord Li sent the word he could no longer get up, and had his son hold him up to write it.

The night the scripture room was unsealed, the two brothers quarrelled in it for the first time. Lingbao said: Brother, this time we've escaped it, but there'll be more; that Daoist is still in the palace; this year it didn't work; next year he'll say it again; he said from today on, borrowed characters—we won't borrow them; Aluohe we write Aluohe; non-action we don't write as non-action; we write the sound; if the Tang can't read it, they can't read it; those who can't read it at least won't say we stole Laozi's goods. Jingjing said the scriptures speak to those who have come in at the gate; those who have come in have someone to teach them, and can read even the transcriptions; but those outside the gate read once in a lifetime; if they can't read a single character, they'll never in their lives come in. Lingbao said: then the ones outside, reading it, read it as Laozi, and go in at Laozi's gate, not ours. Jingjing could not answer; after a few breaths he said that this was how he had translated all his life; he couldn't change now. Lingbao said: then he would change it. The two brothers sat facing that lamp, neither looking at the other; then Jingjing said: then you change it; the remaining rolls of the *Sutra of Profound Peace and Joy*, translate by your method—transcription, no borrowed characters; the rolls before, by his method—let them stay; put both kinds in the scripture room; those who come after can read whichever kind they understand. Lingbao agreed. That night when Jingjing got back to his room—the board, locked a night in the scripture room, had just been brought back—he wrote on it the character *liang*, both, and when he had written it thought: this character too is borrowed; borrowed from Lingbao.

In the winter of the twelfth year of Yuanhe, Li Su fell ill. He was seventy-four that year; he had spent his whole life at the Directorate of Astronomy, risen to Director; at home one son carried on watching the stars for him at the Directorate, one was in the Shence Army, one in the Court of Imperial Sacrifices; every son's name had the character *jing* in it; when he named them he had told Jingjing: that character on your stele is a good one; I'm borrowing it. He lay at home; the room was still heaped with books, Tang, Persian, Indian, with only a crack left to walk through, and the bed was set up at the end of that crack. Jingjing went to see him on a stick, edging sideways between the heaps of books, edging up to the bed; Li Su saw him and smiled, and said: you're old too, and you can still squeeze in. Jingjing told him about the scripture room and thanked him for the word he had passed on; Li Su waved his hand and said Mars trespassing on the Heart was true; this autumn Mars truly was moving by the Heart lodge; he hadn't made it up; he'd only picked a day to say a true thing.

Li Su said these few days he couldn't sleep; at night he lay with his eyes open; the room had no window, he couldn't see the sky, so he looked in his heart: where the Dipper was, where the Purple Palace was, where the Wolf Star was, every one in place, the same as forty years ago when he watched them in Guangzhou, and the same as thirty years ago sitting on the roof of your monastery watching them with Elder Yonan. He said Elder Yonan told him that day: watch them here or watch them elsewhere, they're the same stars; he hadn't understood then—Elder Yonan, a Persian, running to Chang'an to watch the stars: was he watching Persian stars or Chang'an stars? Later he understood: the stars aren't divided; the ones watching them are. He said that all his life at the Directorate he had watched the stars for the Son of Heaven, watched for good and ill fortune, watched for calamities and portents; when he read them right the Son of Heaven rewarded him; when he read them wrong the Son of Heaven said nothing, and he knew the Son of Heaven remembered it in his heart; he had watched the stars forty years, and only in old age knew that the fortunes he read were all men's affairs; the stars themselves cared nothing about them at all.

Jingjing sat by his bed and said he was the same; he had translated scriptures all his life, borrowed characters all his life, borrowed thousands, put thousands of dots, and only in old age knew that those characters—when the Tang read them, they cared nothing whether they were borrowed or their own; if the Tang understood, they understood; if they didn't, they didn't. Li Su looked at him and said: then do you regret it? Jingjing said he didn't; only he sometimes thought: if Elder Alopen hadn't borrowed that Heavenly Worthy in his day, what would he have borrowed? Li Su said: you'd have borrowed something else; whatever you borrow is borrowing; your Pa said it—living in someone else's house. They both laughed.

Li Su died in the twelfth month of that year. The palace sent men to condole; the funeral was handsome; a great many came, some in purple, some in scarlet, most of whom Jingjing did not know. He stood at the back of the crowd on his stick, waited for those in purple and scarlet to go in group by group and come out group by group, and only then went in, stood a while before the coffin, made the sign of the cross, turned, and came out. On the way back Jingfu held him up, and he told Jingfu: of those my age, only I am left. A few steps on he said again: Siyuan doesn't count; Siyuan's younger than me. Jingfu said Brother Siyuan was nearly fifty too. Jingjing said: still younger than me.

That night, what he wrote on his board was *xing*, star. What he read was the Psalm: "The heavens declare the glory of God; and the firmament sheweth his handywork. Day unto day uttereth speech, and night unto night sheweth knowledge. There is no speech nor language, where their voice is not heard. Their line is gone out through all the earth, and their words to the end of the world." When he had written it he put the board down and went out to light the lamp; when it was lit he looked up at the sky; there was cloud; not one star could be seen.

---

Note: The *Sutra of Profound Peace and Joy* and the *Book of Honoured Ones* both come from Dunhuang. The *Book of Honoured Ones* ends: "The scriptures of the original teaching of Daqin number in all five hundred and thirty, all on palm leaves in the Brahmin tongue… Jingjing has translated the above thirty rolls; the great remainder lies in the bark-skin cases, not yet translated." This colophon is generally held to date from after Jingjing's lifetime; this chapter borrows it as his own hand. Li Su, a Persian and Director of the Directorate of Astronomy, died in the twelfth year of Yuanhe at seventy-four; his epitaph was excavated in 1980, and all his sons' names begin with the character *jing*.
