# Volume Two　Li Guangbi

## Chapter 7　Football in the Camp

> The gardens have passed the new festival; wind and blossom swirl round the high tower. Far off I hear the beating of drums: football, the army's pleasure.
>
> —Wei Yingwu, "Written in the North Tower after the Cold Food Festival"

In the fourth month, Guo Ziyi came out through Jingxing.

On the day the main Shuofang army reached the walls of Changshan, Shi Siming broke camp in the night and fell back some dozens of li to the north, and the siege of Changshan was lifted. When the gates opened, the soldiers in the city crowded up onto the walls. Some wept, some laughed, some bawled out the marching songs of Shuofang, every note off key. They had held the city forty days and more. When the arrows ran out they had pulled down roof beams to whittle shafts; when the grain ran out they had boiled the leather of their saddles. This year's Cold Food festival they had spent on the walls too, with no new fire and no cold food, only cold wind.

On the day the Shuofang army entered the city, Yisi was at the gate watching the troops and horses come in. He saw the grooms of Guo Ziyi's camp driving in a great herd of pack horses, and the last man driving them was stooped, with a face that looked as if someone had kneaded it and never smoothed it out.

Yisi stopped short and called out: "Chounu!"

The man started, looked up, saw who it was, and the blood went out of his face all at once.

"You're alive," said Yisi. "Outside the empty village, Stone said your horse went down and they closed round you."

"They caught me," said Chounu. His eyes did not look at Yisi; they looked at the whip in his hand. "In the night… I got away in the night. Ran west, ran back to camp."

Having said those two things he said nothing more, raised his whip, and drove the pack horses on into the city, very fast, and did not look back once.

Yisi stood at the gate and watched his back a good while. He wanted to ask how he had got away, who had caught him, where they had taken him; but Chounu went off in such a hurry that the question never came out.

The armies of Guo and Li joined, more than a hundred thousand men. A few days later they fought Shi Siming south of the city of Jiumen, and Shi Siming was badly beaten and fell back toward Zhao and Boling.

In that battle Hun Shizhi's son, Hun Jian, shot the rebel general Li Lijie dead in front of the lines with a single arrow.

The news went round toward evening. Yisi was sitting in camp having his dressings changed when he heard men shouting outside the tent: what a shot the young Hun was—more than a hundred paces, one arrow clean through Li Lijie's neck, and Li Lijie pitched off his horse, and his men could not even get the body back.

When Yisi heard it, he did not move for a long time.

The things on the Jingxing road came back to him one by one. The Hu soldiers on the ridge, the jar of fire in the shrine, the hoofprints close-packed on the big road and the little road, the riverbed by the Mianman. Feng Shouyi's head hung on the Tumen Pass. Old Iron against the rock, his two hands over his belly, saying *can't hold it in now*.

All these things that man had arranged. A man Yisi had never once seen, and now he was dead.

He had thought he would feel some satisfaction, and he did not. He sat there with the Kaiyuan coin in his fist, held it a long time, and inside him it was empty, as if something had been taken away, or as if there had never been anything there.

The battle over, both armies were to rest a few days, put their men and horses in order, bring up grain and fodder, and wait for the next battle.

It was in those few days that someone suggested each army put out seven men for a game of football.

---

Who suggested it, nobody could say afterward. Some said a captain under Guo Ziyi, some said a company commander in Governor Li's camp. At any rate, once the word got about, the soldiers of both armies were eager for it. They had been fighting for months, so many had died, and everyone had a breath bottled up inside him; play a game of football, work up a sweat, shout yourself hoarse, and some of that breath would come out. The men from Changshan were keenest of all. We never got our game at Cold Food, they said; we'll make it up now.

When Guo Ziyi heard of it, he smiled and said, "Let them play."

When Governor Li heard of it, he did not smile, and said only: "Let them play."

The men in camp said among themselves that these two governors had once not spoken to each other.

In the Tianbao years both had served as household generals under An Sishun, military governor of Shuofang, and ate in the same tent, and never spoke; when they met, neither let his eyes fall on the other. Then An Lushan rebelled, the court made Guo Ziyi military governor of Shuofang, and Li Guangbi became his subordinate. Li Guangbi, fearing that Guo Ziyi would use his office to pay off a private grudge, went to Guo Ziyi's tent and knelt on the ground and said: *I accept death gladly; I ask only that you spare my wife and children.* Guo Ziyi came down from his seat, took his hands and raised him up, and the two of them wept face to face. Guo Ziyi said: *The state is in trouble now; to go east and fight the rebels, there is no one but you. Is this a time for private grudges?* Afterwards he memorialised the court and recommended Li Guangbi as military governor of Hedong, to lead the first force out through Jingxing.

Everyone in camp knew this story, so when they heard that the two governors were to sit together and watch a game, they all found it interesting.

The two men's tempers were known in camp too. In Guo Ziyi's camp, when a soldier made a small mistake, Guo Ziyi would most likely say only, "Be careful next time." In Governor Li's camp, the same mistake got twenty strokes of the rod first, and *next time* afterwards. Guo Ziyi's men said Governor Li's soldiers were made of iron; Governor Li's men said Guo Ziyi's soldiers were steeped in honey.

But when it came to fighting, the soldiers of both armies said the same thing in private: Governor Li fought better. His orders were strict and his formations exact; when to advance and when to fall back, not one step was out of place. Changshan had been besieged forty days and more and the rebels had not got one inch onto the wall. There were men in Guo Ziyi's camp who granted it too: Guo Ziyi was a man everyone was willing to die for, but for setting out troops and making the call in battle, among all the generals of the court there was probably not one to match Governor Li. When the talk reached Guo Ziyi's ears he was not offended. "It's simply true," he said. "Why else would I have recommended him?"

In this game of football, each side wanted to show the other what its men were made of.

---

The pitch was dug out for the occasion outside the city of Jiumen.

East of the city there was a village whose people had all fled before the fighting, and at its edge they had left a threshing floor. A threshing floor is where farmers roll their wheat every year, so the earth was long since packed flat and hard; but it was only some thirty paces square, not big enough. Each side sent a hundred soldiers with spades and rammers to enlarge it.

The man in charge of the work was an old squad leader from Governor Li's camp, a Hedong man who had farmed at home. First he had them pick the stones, grass roots and wheat stubble off the ground round the floor; then he had the newly added strip loosened; then water carried and poured on it until it was soaked through, and left overnight. Early next morning he had two stone rollers dragged from the village, put horses to them, and rolled them back and forth over the pitch until the ground shone and a foot left no print; and in the corners where the rollers could not reach, they rammed it hard, blow after blow. When the ball rolls on the ground, he said, if the ground isn't level the ball won't do what you tell it; if the ground isn't firm, a little play and the dust is up, and when men collide nobody can see anybody.

Yisi came to help with the work too. His right shoulder still had little strength in it, so he carried water with his left hand. When he had carried two buckets he stood at the edge looking at the lie of the ground a while, and told the old squad leader that the north end of the pitch was lower than the south; if it rained, all the water would run toward the goal at the north end. The old squad leader squatted down to look, swore, and had a drainage ditch dug outside the north end.

A shallow ditch was dug all round the pitch, and the earth from it piled on the inner side and patted into a knee-high wall, its top smoothed with the backs of spades. This was the wall of the ball city. A ball that hit the wall would bounce back, so on this pitch there was no such thing as out of play. An old clerk from Changshan who could read stood by the wall wagging his head and reciting: "A round ball and a square wall, in the image of yin and yang." The soldiers did not understand it, but felt it sounded right.

In the middle of the wall at each end a gap was left, and in each gap two willow poles were set, bent into the shape of a half moon, both ends sunk deep in the earth: that was the goal. Inside it a shallow pit was dug and its bottom lined with wheat straw, so that a ball that rolled in would drop into the pit and stay there; that was called "entering the chamber." The two ball chambers stood some fifty paces apart, one north and one south, facing each other.

The ball was sewn by a cobbler from Changshan: eight pieces of oxhide cut into pointed petals and stitched closely together with hemp thread, with a washed pig's bladder inside. The blowing up was left to Cheng Chongyi of Guo Ziyi's camp. Cheng Chongyi puffed out his cheeks like a toad and blew a long time, his face going purple, before the ball filled out. He tied off the neck, slapped it on the ground, and the ball bounced up high with a *boom*, and everyone round the edge cheered.

---

The rules were settled by men from both sides together.

Each side put out seven men and chose a "captain" to lead them. Each army also provided a provost, and the two together were the "umpire," to judge the scoring. If the two umpires disagreed, the two governors would be asked to decide. No hands—except that the rover, within three paces of his own goal, could knock the ball away with his hands, but not hold it; no grabbing players, no kicking a man's knees. A ball kicked into the other side's chamber scored one point, and for every point the drum at the edge would roll once. Time was kept by incense sticks set up at the edge, five in all; when the five had burned out, the side with more points won. If the incense had not burned out and one side reached five points first, that side won.

The seven positions used the old names they had for football in Chang'an. The man at the very front, who attacked the goal, was called the *captain*—a side's captain was usually its striker; the one behind him, who fed him the ball, was the *feeder*; the two in the middle were the *main wing* and the *head wing*, who won the ball and ran up and down; the two guarding the willow posts of their own chamber were the *left net-post* and the *right net-post*; and the one who stood in front of his own goal was the *rover*, who covered wherever there was a gap, and when the ball came to the goal could use his hands to stop it.

Guo Ziyi's captain was Hou Siyuan. Hou Siyuan was one of Guo Ziyi's personal guards, whom the camp called Hou Two, eighteen or nineteen, not tall, with long thin legs; he ran like a deer. The feeder was Cheng Chongyi, Hou Siyuan's fellow villager, whom the camp called Cheng Four, a few years older than Hou Siyuan and the surest-footed of them; when the ball came to his feet it stuck as if glued. The rover was a Hedong soldier called Big-Eye Sun, who had manned a beacon tower three years and had sharp eyes and quick hands; the men in camp said that if a sparrow flew overhead he could count how many times it beat its wings.

Governor Li's captain was an old soldier of forty or so. He was not tall, a little stooped, and ordinarily looked listless and droopy. Someone at the edge knew him and said that when they were holding Changshan he had spent every day in the wounded men's quarters changing dressings.

Yisi knew him. In the twenty days when his shoulder wound was worst in Changshan, this was the old soldier who had changed his dressings. Every time, when he had finished, Yisi had asked his name, and every time he had said: I'll tell you when the fighting's over and we're drinking.

---

That afternoon several thousand people gathered round the pitch, rings of heads outside the low wall, and some simply climbed the old locust tree at the edge of the village. Ma Six came too, on a crutch, one leg bound up thick as a log, and pushed his way to the very front, his voice louder than anyone's; Thirteen, his arm in a sling, was dragged along by him, his face still white but his eyes bright. Of those who had come back from the Jingxing road, only Chounu was missing. The two governors sat on an earthen platform on the west side of the pitch, a small table between them with a pot of tea on it. Guo Ziyi drank tea and talked with those around him; Governor Li said not a word, his eyes fixed on the pitch, his back very straight.

The drummer beat three rolls, the two umpires set the ball in the middle of the pitch, and the game began.

From the start Guo Ziyi's men attacked hard. Hou Siyuan had quick legs; as soon as the ball came to him he put his head down and ran it forward, past one man, past another, and Governor Li's men could not catch him. Before half an incense stick had burned Hou Siyuan had scored once; Cheng Chongyi fed him, and Hou Siyuan scored again. At the edge the soldiers of Guo Ziyi's camp shouted themselves hoarse, and the drummer beat until the sweat ran down him.

Governor Li's men were in no hurry. Their captain, the old soldier, stood in the middle of the pitch and did not run much; he only shouted. When he shouted, his men drew in together, into a knot, and when Hou Siyuan tried to run the ball into it, it was like charging headlong into a thorn bush: a foot came out on the left, a shoulder pushed in on the right, and the ball was gone. Having won the ball they were in no hurry to go forward either. They passed it among themselves, foot to foot, slowly, so slowly that the crowd swore at them. But as they passed it, Hou Siyuan and the others grew tired, and once they were tired there were gaps, and the old soldier sent the ball through a gap with one kick, low along the ground, fast, and it rolled into the chamber.

Two to one. A while later, two to two.

Guo Ziyi watched the pitch and said with a smile to those around him: "They're Old Li's men all right. Even their football's like that."

Governor Li heard it, and did not turn his head or say anything.

---

When the third stick was half burned, Cheng Chongyi was hurt.

He went up with Governor Li's main wing for a high ball; the two of them jumped at once and their shoulders collided. As Cheng Chongyi came down, one foot landed on the other man's instep, his ankle turned, and there was a crack. He sat down hard on the ground clutching his foot, his face white.

The two umpires ran over to look and both said it was not deliberate; no foul. Cheng Chongyi was helped off, his foot already swelling. Guo Ziyi's side had only six men left.

Hou Siyuan went round the edge of the pitch and could find no one who could play. Everyone in Guo Ziyi's camp who could play was already on the pitch; the rest were either on duty or lying in the wounded men's quarters. Hou Siyuan stamped with frustration, turned his head, and saw Yisi.

Yisi was standing below the platform, watching the game. His right arm was out of its sling now, only he still could not raise it high.

"Master Yi!" Hou Siyuan rushed over. "Can you play football?"

Yisi hesitated a moment and said: "I played as a child."

"Where?"

"In Balkh," said Yisi. "Children there kick a sheep's bladder about."

Hou Siyuan did not care where Balkh was, and seized his sleeve. "That'll do! Come on, come on!"

Yisi looked back at the platform. Guo Ziyi, holding his teacup, looked at him, then at the pitch, smiled, and said, "Go on." Governor Li looked at him too, and said nothing.

---

When Yisi went onto the pitch there was a burst of laughter from the edge. Someone shouted: "A foreign monk can play football?" Someone shouted: "Mind you don't kick it all the way to Arabia!"

Yisi took off his outer robe and stood in a short tunic, sleeves rolled to the elbow, and took Cheng Chongyi's place as feeder.

He did not play prettily. He did not know the tricks: he could not tap the ball with his heel, could not juggle it on his knee, could not turn in the air. The first time the ball was passed to him he saw that the head wing behind him was running into open space, so he did not touch the ball but opened his legs and let it go through, leaving it for the man behind. But the head wing was playing with him for the first time, thought the ball was meant for Yisi, and was half a step slow coming up, and one of Governor Li's men nipped in and took it. All the crowd saw was the ball rolling past the monk's feet, and they thought he could not even stop a ball, and laughed again.

But before long the crowd stopped laughing.

Yisi did not run much. He seemed not to be watching the ball but the men: who was standing where, whose feet were steady, who was tired, which way a man's eyes were glancing. When Governor Li's men drew in together, he did not push into the knot; as soon as the ball came to him he passed it out to one side, to whichever man had run into space. He passed accurately; whoever he passed to, the ball arrived at that man's feet, neither high nor low, neither fast nor slow. Twice he did not touch the ball at all, but only stepped to one side and blocked a man's way, and Hou Siyuan went through the gap beside him.

Hou Siyuan told people afterward that the monk played football the way he looked at the road in battle: he could see everything.

By the time the fifth stick was half burned, Guo Ziyi's side had scored twice more, both times Yisi feeding and Hou Siyuan shooting. Governor Li's side had scored twice too, both times the old soldier, once along the ground and once off the wall, bouncing back into the chamber.

Four all. Whoever scored next would win.

---

In the last passage of play, the ball was with Guo Ziyi's side.

Hou Siyuan took it in the middle and was squeezed between two men. He did not try to force through, but flicked the ball out to the right, to Yisi. Governor Li's men had all pressed over to the left, and the right was wide open. Yisi took the ball and ran it forward along the low wall on the right. He did not go fast. When he was seven or eight paces from the chamber, Governor Li's rover had come out to meet him and set himself against the willow post on Yisi's side.

The rover was a young soldier. He spread his arms and crouched, and blocked the space between the near post and himself completely. Yisi was now at the right-hand corner of the pitch, only two or three paces from the wall, at a sharp angle to the half-moon goal; of the goal he could see only a crack.

The crowd went silent at once. Several thousand people, not one sound; you could hear the wind in the leaves of the locust tree.

Whether to shoot, Yisi was not sure. His eyes flicked across the middle: Hou Siyuan was cutting in from the middle, with no one near him.

Yisi swung his right foot back as if to shoot. The rover dived toward the post, just as he should. Yisi's foot came down, but not toward the goal; with the inside of his foot he pushed the ball gently across the middle, to the place Hou Siyuan was running to.

But Hou Siyuan had stopped half a step. He had thought the monk was going to shoot, and had checked his run, and was waiting to see whether it went in. The ball rolled past a pace in front of him, and when he put out his foot it was already out of reach.

There was an *aiya* from the edge of the pitch, as if several thousand people had sighed at once. Hou Siyuan covered his face and squatted down on the ground.

The ball rolled across the middle and came to someone's feet. The someone was Governor Li's captain, the old soldier. Nobody had seen when he ran back. The moment Yisi's foot checked, he had already been running back.

He stopped the ball, turned, and set off forward with it.

Guo Ziyi's head wing was running back and met him head-on, and swept a foot at him. The old soldier flicked the ball left and stepped right, and the sweeping foot caught nothing, and the man slid away a long way. The main wing threw himself at him; the old soldier dropped a shoulder as if to go right, and as the man moved right to block him, he flicked the ball up with his toe, round the man's foot, and went round after it.

Past two men in a row. The whole crowd was on its feet.

Ahead of him there was only Big-Eye Sun. The old soldier was still more than ten paces from the chamber. He did not run it any further. He swung his right foot back and hit it. It was low and vicious; the ball flew skimming the ground with a whine of wind, straight for the bottom left corner of the chamber.

Someone at the edge had already shouted half of "*In*—"

Big-Eye Sun dived. He did not use his feet—there was no time to use his feet. He flew out sideways, both fists together, and punched the ball hard. *Boom*: the ball flew back the way it had come and bounced high into the air. Big-Eye Sun hit the ground and rolled twice.

The half "*In*—" at the edge turned into a long "*Aaaah*—". The soldiers of Guo Ziyi's camp slapped their thighs and cheered; the soldiers of Changshan clutched their heads and groaned.

The ball was still in the air.

The old soldier did not stop. When he had struck that shot he was still running forward, and the ball had flown back over his head. He turned and chased two steps, and by now the ball was beginning to fall. He had his back to the chamber. He looked up once, and suddenly threw himself backward, his whole body lying flat in the air; his left foot kicked up first, and his right foot whipped up after it, and over his head he struck the falling ball away.

The ball flew back the way it had come, over his own head, into the chamber. Big-Eye Sun was only halfway up off the ground and had not even time to turn his head before the ball dropped onto the straw behind him.

The crowd went silent all at once, so silent you could hear the ball rolling on the straw.

The old soldier fell flat on his back on the gleaming ground with a thud, and for a long while did not get up. He lay there with his mouth open, panting, panting and coughing, and as he coughed, he laughed.

The drum rolled.

Five to four. Governor Li's side had won.

The crowd erupted. The soldiers from Changshan vaulted the low wall and rushed onto the pitch, pulled the old soldier up off the ground, lifted him, and tossed him into the air. Some were weeping as they tossed him; some were weeping and laughing at once.

On the platform, Governor Li, who after a victory in battle showed nothing on his face, now brought his palm down on the little table so hard that the teacups jumped, and cried: "Good!" Having cried it, he sat bolt upright again and reached for his cup of tea, and only when he had it at his lips did he find that it was empty.

Guo Ziyi pushed his own cup across and said with a smile: "A man of forty, and he kicks it in backwards. In your place I'd have cheered too."

Governor Li took the cup and drank. His face was the same as ever, but the corners of his eyes eased a little, and the men who had followed him many years could all see it: the governor was happy today.

---

When the crowd broke up, Yisi did not go with it. He crossed the low wall, went to the foot of the platform and stood some way off, and waited until the men around the two governors had all withdrawn; then he went forward a few steps, joined his hands, bowed, and made a deep obeisance.

Guo Ziyi looked at him and smiled. "Monk, your afternoon wasn't wasted."

Yisi bowed again. "Lord Guo is too kind. This humble monk's feet were clumsy. He spoiled Hou Siyuan's ball and brought shame on Lord Guo."

Guo Ziyi waved a hand and laughed. "Losing to Old Li is no shame."

Yisi did not straighten up. He kept his head bowed, and after a while said: "This humble monk has a request to make of Governor Li. I don't know whether Lord Guo will allow me to speak it."

Guo Ziyi looked at him, then at Li Guangbi, and nodded. "Speak."

Yisi turned to Li Guangbi, lifted the front of his robe, and knelt.

Li Guangbi sat looking down at him, eyelids half lowered, and did not tell him to rise.

"Governor," said Yisi, "on the Jingxing road this humble monk travelled six days with Tie Wanquan of Hedong. He died beside the Mianman, and before he died he gave this humble monk the letter, and entrusted him with one more thing. He has a son called Tie Qianshan, who is a crossbowman in the governor's camp. He asked this humble monk to look after his son from now on."

He paused, his forehead almost touching the ground before the platform.

"This humble monk makes bold," he said, "to beg the governor's grace: to transfer Tie Qianshan to Lord Guo's command, so that this humble monk may look after him at his side, and his father may rest easy under the earth. This humble monk knows it is presumption, and begs the governor's pardon."

For a moment the platform was quiet. Guo Ziyi held his teacup and said nothing.

Li Guangbi did not answer at once. He looked out at the soldiers still not all gone from the pitch, looked a while, and only then spoke.

"No."

Yisi knelt there and did not move.

"Tie Qianshan is my crossbowman," said Li Guangbi. "Forty-odd days in Changshan, and he didn't come down off that wall one day. Of my thousand crossbowmen, he shoots straightest."

He stopped, and went on, his voice still not raised, the words falling one at a time:

"His father asked you to look after him. He didn't ask you to take him out of my camp. My men—I don't give one away. Give you one today, and tomorrow someone comes for a second. If he were afraid to die, I wouldn't keep him. He's not afraid. So he stays with me, and finishes his father's war."

Yisi lay prostrate on the ground and did not say another word. He knocked his head on the ground once.

"This humble monk was presumptuous," he said. "He thanks the governor."

Li Guangbi said "Mm," turned his head away, and did not look at him again.

Yisi knocked his head on the ground once more to Guo Ziyi, and only then rose, backed away several steps, turned and left. Guo Ziyi watched his back, and shook his head slightly; whom he was shaking it at, nobody knew.

---

He found Tie Qianshan at the edge of the pitch.

Tie Qianshan was standing outside the low wall with a group of crossbowmen from Changshan; when Du De was tossed in the air just now, he had gone in and helped lift him. When he saw Yisi coming he pushed his way out of the crowd.

Yisi drew him aside and told him what had happened at the platform.

When Tie Qianshan had heard it he was not angry, nor disappointed; his face was the same as ever. "The governor won't let men go," he said. "I knew that already." He paused, and said, "My pa sent me here so I'd be here."

Yisi looked at him a long time without speaking.

He thought of the first night on the Jingxing road, under the cliff, Old Iron gnawing a flatbread, breaking it in two, saying that father and son in one camp, either you watch me die or I watch you die; saying Guo Ziyi was a good man, but if you were talking about who could fight, it was Governor Li, and following him the boy would live a few years longer. At the time Yisi had taken it for idle talk. Kneeling just now before the platform, he had not remembered a word of it. He had wanted to look after Old Iron's son for him, and Old Iron himself had long since chosen his son's place for him.

"Then listen," said Yisi. "From now on, when you go into battle, protect yourself first. Your pa told me to watch over you; I can't watch over you, so you'll have to watch over yourself."

"Mm."

"When your squad leader tells you to lie flat, you lie flat."

"Mm."

"And if there's ever a chance," said Yisi, "wherever I am, come and find me."

Tie Qianshan looked at him and nodded. He thought about it and asked: "Where will you be?"

Yisi thought a moment and said: "Wherever Lord Guo is, I'll be."

Tie Qianshan nodded again, turned and went back among the crossbowmen. A few steps on he looked back and bared his teeth at Yisi, very quickly, exactly the way his father had when he laughed, only without a sound.

---

That night the two governors gave a feast in the city of Jiumen.

The governors drank in the main hall; the fourteen footballers had a table of their own in a side hall. A table, they called it, but really it was two long tables pushed together, Guo Ziyi's men down one side and Governor Li's down the other, with jars of wine and several great basins of mutton between them.

Cheng Chongyi came too, leaning on a stick, one foot swollen like a steamed bun. As soon as he sat down he called for wine; the man next to him told him to go easy, and he said his foot wasn't growing out of his mouth.

When the wine had gone round twice the talk got going. Governor Li's men said: that monk of yours, that last move—made like he was really going to shoot, fooled our rover flat onto his face, and fooled your own man too. Guo Ziyi's men said: that old soldier of yours, looks so droopy most days, turns out to be a football master, kept it well hidden—can put the ball in the chamber lying on the ground. Big-Eye Sun drank with his head down and said he'd already thrown both fists, how was he to know the old devil would kick it in backwards? The two sides went back and forth, and before long they were all laughing in a heap.

Yisi sat at one end of the long table and did not drink wine, only broth.

The doorway of the side hall was crowded with onlookers, and Ma Six was among them, on his crutch, clinking bowls with this one and that one, his face flushed red with drink. Someone asked him: the groom who came back with you on the little road, where is he? Why didn't he come to watch? Ma Six said: I asked; said the horses were sick and he couldn't get away. And then he muttered to himself: "Those horses of his are dearer to him than his own mother."

Yisi heard it, and said nothing.

The old soldier came down from the other end of the long table carrying a bowl of wine and sat down opposite Yisi.

"Monk," said the old soldier, "you don't know me any more?"

"I know you," said Yisi. "You changed my dressings for twenty days."

The old soldier chuckled. "Back then you asked my name, and I said I'd tell you when we were drinking. Now we're drinking."

He set his bowl on the table, straightened himself, and saluted with his fists together. "My surname is Du, my name De. From Duling, in the capital district."

Yisi returned the salute. "Yisi, of Balkh."

Du De nodded, drank the bowl of wine down in one, and wiped his mouth.

Cheng Chongyi, beside them, could hold it in no longer. He turned his head and said to Yisi: "Master Yi, that last bit—a chance like that, why didn't you shoot?"

"There was only a crack of goal left," said Yisi. "I wasn't sure. Hou Two was in the middle with no one near him."

Hou Siyuan kept his head down a long time before he said: "I didn't read it. I thought you were going to shoot."

Cheng Chongyi was about to say more when Du De laughed across the table. He laughed very slowly, the lines on his face smoothing out one by one.

"If he'd shot, you'd have lost quicker," said Du De. "Our rover was just waiting for him to shoot. That pass was right. It was your man who didn't follow."

Cheng Chongyi glared at him. "How did you know he'd pass?"

"I watched his feet," said Du De. "When he swung his leg, the foot he was standing on wasn't planted dead, and his eyes flicked to the middle once. A man who really means to shoot looks only at the goal. As soon as I saw that, I knew the ball was going to the middle, so I started back first."

He poured himself another bowl of wine, took it up, and raised it to Yisi.

"This monk of yours," he said to Cheng Chongyi, "plays football like he fights. He doesn't gamble. A ball he's not sure of, he won't shoot. It's your men who haven't caught up with him yet."

Yisi raised his bowl of broth to him too.

"You're the same," said Yisi. "You saw it, and you didn't gamble either. You came back."

Du De stopped short, then burst out laughing, laughing so hard he spilled his wine.

---

The side hall went quiet a moment, and then grew lively again.

Cheng Chongyi did not press any more. He poured half the wine in his bowl into Du De's and said: Old Du, that kick of yours backwards into the goal—first time in my life I've seen it. I give you that. Du De said: if your ankle hadn't turned, who won today would still be anybody's guess. The two of them clinked bowls and drained them.

Hou Siyuan edged up to Yisi and asked quietly: "Master Yi, next time there's a game, will you play?"

Yisi thought about it and said: "If you're short of men, I'll play."

"Then next time, if you're going to pass, give me a look first."

Yisi said: "Next time, watch my feet."

Hou Siyuan thought about it and nodded.

---

The night grew late, and the feast broke up.

Du De had drunk too much and was a little unsteady on his feet. Yisi helped him out of the side hall into the courtyard. There were no lamps in the courtyard; the moon was very bright and lit the ground white. Du De stopped in the courtyard and looked up at the moon, looked a long time.

"Monk," he said, "that goal I scored today was the best goal I've ever scored in my life."

Yisi held him up and said nothing.

"I joined the army at sixteen," said Du De. "Twenty-odd years I've played football. Played in Liangzhou, played in Lingzhou, played in the garrison towns of Hexi. Never once has one gone in so sweet."

He sat down on a stone in the courtyard and patted the place beside him for Yisi to sit too. Yisi sat.

"Let me ask you," said Du De. "This football of yours—who did you learn it from? In Balkh, is there someone who teaches it?"

"My brother taught me," said Yisi.

Du De said "Oh," and waited for him to go on.

It was a while before Yisi spoke. He said his brother was eight years older than he was; before he was as tall as a cartwheel, his brother had taught him to kick a ball made from a blown-up sheep's bladder on the open ground outside the monastery gate. His brother taught him fiercely: if he kicked it badly, he made him fetch it and kick it again, and in half a day would make him fetch it a hundred times. When the two brothers played they forgot the time; the sun would set and they would not go home; they played till it was too dark to see the ball, and then played by the sound.

"My father was a priest at the monastery," said Yisi. "He was busy in the daytime, and only came out to look for us after he had said the evening prayers. Sometimes he was very angry, and took one of us in each hand, and dragged us home by the ears. Sometimes he wasn't angry, and just stood at the edge watching, watched a long while, sighed, said nothing, and turned and went back first."

Du De laughed out loud.

"My mother didn't come looking," said Yisi. "She waited at home. When we came back she was always smiling, standing in the doorway, smelling of baked flatbread, and she'd say: go and wash your hands, there's mutton stewed with apricots."

Here he stopped, and then went on, his voice no different from before: "My father has been dead seven or eight years. My mother died the winter before last. Both times I wasn't there. My brother's between the two rivers, I'm here; we haven't seen each other in more than ten years. I don't know whether he still plays football."

He thought, and added: "He's lost an arm. You don't need arms for football, though."

Du De was startled, and turned to look at him, looked a long while, and then said: "If he taught you all you know, then his football must be better than yours."

"Better than mine," said Yisi. He thought, and said: "That last move today—only a crack of goal left. I wasn't sure, so I passed to Hou Two. If it had been my brother, sure or not, he'd have shot. Ten times out of ten, eight or nine would go in."

Du De gave a grunt of a laugh. "Then it's lucky today it was you on the pitch. If it had been him, my run back would have been for nothing."

Du De nodded, and for a long time said nothing. Then he said: "My football—my brother taught me too."

He said his family lived in Duling, two brothers, his elder brother five years older than he. In those days the world was at peace, and in Chang'an football was all the rage. In the mansions of the princes and nobles, every house kept a few good players; at Cold Food and Clear Bright they played in the mansions and played by the Qujiang Pool, for the whole city to watch. Play well, and you could get into a great man's house; play better, and you might be summoned into the palace to play once before the Sage himself. Please the Sage, and there was money, there was silk, and some had even won an official start in life that way. Of the boys of Duling, eight out of ten had dreamed that dream.

"I dreamed it too," said Du De. "My brother and me, every day on the threshing floor at the edge of the village, played till we wore through a pair of soles a month. We thought one day we'd go to Chang'an and play for the Sage, and win ourselves a place in the world."

He gave a laugh, a very short one.

"Then the family was poor, and I joined the army at sixteen. My brother stayed home and farmed. He played football too, half his life, and never got through any great man's gate. As for me—Hexi, Lingzhou, here to Changshan, play here and play there, always for soldiers." He patted his own leg. "All that skill, and it showed itself once, today, outside the walls of Jiumen. By the time the world's at peace, I'll be old, and can't kick any more."

Yisi did not answer. After a while he asked: "And your brother now? Still in Duling?"

Du De did not answer at once. He bent his head and looked at his two feet, looked a long time.

"Gone," he said. "Last winter, when An Lushan came, they were raising troops in Chang'an and he went too, and followed Gao Xianzhi to hold Shanzhou. Then Gao Xianzhi fell back from Shanzhou to Tong Pass, and all along the road it was men crushing men, horses trampling men, so many died. He died on the road to Tong Pass. Not at the rebels' hands—trampled by our own side. A man from our village sent word. They never found the body."

When he had finished he raised his head and looked at the moon again.

Yisi looked at the moon too. The two of them sat on the stone a long while, and neither spoke. It was very quiet outside the city; that gleaming rolled pitch was probably white now too.

Then Du De stood up, swayed, and Yisi put out a hand to steady him.

"To score one goal like that in a lifetime," said Du De, "a man could die with no regrets."

Yisi said: "You've drunk too much. Don't talk like that."

Du De chuckled and did not argue. He turned his head and looked at Yisi, and his eyes were bright in the moonlight.

"After this, in battle," said Du De, "if you hear somebody shout *Du De*, don't take it for anybody else."

Yisi said: "I'll remember."
