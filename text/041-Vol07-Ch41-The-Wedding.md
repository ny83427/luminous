# Volume Seven　A-Lian

## Chapter 41　The Wedding

> With a craftsman's hand He made the ten thousand things, then set up the first man, and gave him besides a nature good and harmonious, and charged him to keep the world He had made.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

In the fifth month of the eleventh year of Dali, the locust trees of Chang'an came into flower.

There were many locust trees in Chang'an. Both sides of Zhuque Street were lined with them, the foot of every ward wall had them, the gates of great houses had them too. Row upon row they stood along the streets, had stood there more than a hundred years, and the people of Chang'an called them the Locust Tree Offices. When the fifth month came, the whole city smelled of locust blossom—sweet, with a little rankness in it. The blossom fell on the ground, was crushed by cart wheels, trampled by hooves, stuck to people's soles, carried into their houses, carried onto their beds.

At the gate of the Daqin monastery in the Yining Ward there was a locust tree too.

This tree was older than the monastery. The monastery had been founded in the Zhenguan years, and the tree was there already. Under the tree there was a well with a bluestone curb, worn shiny, the well rope having cut several deep grooves into the stone. It was the ward's well; the people of the ward drank its water, and the people of the monastery drank it too.

Every morning Yonan gave out bread under this tree.

He had done it eighteen years. From the first year of Qianyuan, when he came into Chang'an with the seven teachers, until now, every day at first light he came out with a bamboo basket and stood under the locust tree, and to everyone who came, gave a piece of bread. Those who came were beggars, porters, people fleeing famine from the countryside, children of the poor families of the ward. He did not ask who they were, nor what they believed. When he had finished giving he drew a bucket of water at the well and washed his hands. This was the first time of the day.

On the morning of the sixteenth of the fifth month, he gave out the bread as usual, and washed his hands as usual.

Then he went back into the monastery and put on a new robe.

That day he was to be married. He was seventy-one.

---

The Patriarch's answer had arrived at the start of spring. What the letter said, everyone at the monastery came to know afterwards. God has regard to man's weakness.

When they knew it, each took it in his own way.

Ishaq wept. He was fatter than ever now, needed two men to help him sit down, and when he wept his belly heaved; when he had finished weeping he went to the kitchen to ask how many sheep should be stewed for the wedding feast.

Babai got out the monastery's register and left a line blank under Yonan's name. He left the line and wrote nothing in it, waiting.

Gabriel said that on the banks of the Two Rivers, those who married wore crowns on their heads. There were no such crowns in Chang'an, so he went out of the city to the bank of the Wei, broke off a bundle of willow withies, and sat in the gallery weaving. The scar on his left cheek wrinkled in the sun; his hands were very deft, and when he wove one wrong he unpicked it, and having unpicked it wove again.

When Sabrisho had heard the letter, after a long while he said one word: "Allowed."

Everyone at the monastery said it was the first time in eighteen years in Chang'an that he had said that word.

Haddad said nothing. He had known long ago. From that year in Xizhou, he had known.

Narsai said nothing either. He read the scriptures as always, copied books as always. Copying needed reed pens, and reed pens wore blunt with use and had to be trimmed with a small knife. That little knife of his he had bought many years before in the market at Ctesiphon; its blade no longer than a finger, the edge thin, the handle ground from bone and gone yellow, it had trimmed pens for thirty years. That spring, people at the monastery often saw him sitting in the gallery trimming pens, sliver after sliver of reed skin falling at his feet, swept up and falling again.

---

The wedding was not held in the hall, but in the back courtyard.

Yonan himself wanted it so. Yisi asked him why, and he said: "The hall is the Lord's. The back courtyard is people's."

Yisi thought, and asked no more.

The back courtyard was not large. On the east a row of side rooms, the translating rooms, paper heaped under the windows; on the west the kitchen, with a few beds of spring onions planted outside its door. In the very middle stood a jujube tree, its blossom fine and tiny, hardly noticed by anyone. Sabrisho took a few monk-soldiers and swept the courtyard early in the morning, carried in benches and set them in rows, set them dead straight. When they were set, he stepped back a few paces to look, went up again, and moved one bench half an inch.

More people came than had been expected.

Everyone at the monastery came. Of the twenty-four monk-soldiers of those days a dozen or more were left, their hair grizzled, standing in the back rows, their backs still straight. Sargis stood at the very end, half a head taller than the rest. Xu Jing came too; he had long since left the army and minded a shop for someone in the West Market, and as soon as he came in he began to talk—about how in those days in Xizhou he had guarded these elders—and halfway through someone stuffed a flatbread in his mouth.

Some people came from the West Market too. The vinegar seller to the left of A-Lian's shop, the shoe seller to the right, the old *hu* merchant across the way who sold mutton, and a few porters who often came to eat. They stood at the courtyard gate craning their necks to look in, not quite daring to enter. Haddad went over and invited them in one by one, and found them places on the benches.

And there were some who stood under the locust tree outside the monastery gate and did not come in. They were the people who came every morning for bread. They had heard that the old *hu* monk who gave out the bread was being married today, and had come. Having come they did not enter, but stood and squatted under the tree, waiting.

Ishaq had two great basins of mutton soup carried out to them.

---

A-Lian was fetched from the West Market by Haddad.

When she walked into the back courtyard, the courtyard went quiet for a moment.

She was fifty. Her hair was more than half white, combed very neatly, coiled at the back of her head, held with a wooden pin, not dyed. She wore a blue skirt and jacket, not new, washed very clean. There was no powder on her face. She was not tall, had grown a little stout, walked very steadily, her eyes looking ahead and not to either side.

Jingjing stood behind Yonan, and saw her eyes.

Black.

He moved his gaze away.

Yonan stood under the jujube tree waiting for her. He wore that new robe, his back bolt upright, his face as it always was, clean, beardless, almost shining in the sun. He watched her come towards him step by step, his two hands hanging at his sides, his fingers moving once, and again. Jingjing knew that it meant he wanted to wash his hands.

A-Lian came up to him and stopped.

Neither of them spoke.

Afterwards it was A-Lian who spoke first. She spoke in Tang, very low; only the few standing near heard.

"That robe of yours—the seam's come open at the back."

Yonan was taken aback, and reached round to feel his back.

A-Lian had already taken a needle from her sleeve, threaded, and told him to turn round, and there under the jujube tree, before the whole courtyard, in three or five stitches sewed up the seam that had come open. When she had sewn it she bent her head and bit the thread off with her teeth.

The whole courtyard watched, and no one made a sound. Ishaq's eyes were red again.

---

Yisi led the rite.

He was now Superintendent of Chang'an; the Patriarch was far away in Baghdad, and the affairs of the monastery in Chang'an were his to direct. He wore the purple robe, the one Jingjing had taken in for him, still a little loose at the shoulders. The scar on his brow was still there after twenty years, like a centipede lying there; as a man grows old the centipede grows old too, and its colour had faded.

The rite was done after the old custom of the banks of the Two Rivers, only simplified. Yisi took up an earthenware cup with wine in it. He said the blessing in Syriac, said it very slowly, biting every word clear, as when he spoke Tang. When he had said it, he made the sign of the cross over the cup with his hand.

Yonan drank a mouthful. A-Lian drank a mouthful.

Gabriel brought up the two crowns woven of willow, set one on Yonan's head and one on A-Lian's. The withies had been broken three days before and had grown a little dry; the leaves were curling at the edges. As A-Lian's was put on, a leaf fell and landed on her shoulder. Yonan reached out and took it off for her.

Babai sat at a small table to one side, opened the register, and in the line left blank under Yonan's name wrote two names. One in Syriac, one in Tang. The Tang he wrote himself, very properly.

阿蓮. A-Lian.

---

When the rite was over, there was the feast.

Ishaq's mutton soup was brought in, basin after basin. The *hu* flatbread had been baked early that morning and sent by the boy from A-Lian's shop, stack upon stack, still too hot to hold. The people on the benches began to talk, began to laugh, and Xu Jing began on Xizhou again; this time no one stuffed bread in his mouth, and everyone listened while he turned a band of forty or fifty robbers into two hundred.

Yisi stood up and said he would say a few words.

The courtyard fell quiet.

Yisi stood under the jujube tree, still holding that earthenware cup. He looked at Yonan, looked at A-Lian, and looked at the whole courtyard.

"I can't make speeches," he said.

Someone below laughed. Everyone at the monastery knew it was the first thing he said every time he opened his mouth.

"I came to Chang'an at seventeen," said Yisi. "The elder Yonan wasn't bishop then. Later he was. He was over me. Strict. He washed his hands three times a day, and made me wash mine three times a day too."

Again someone laughed.

"I washed them half a year," said Yisi, "and stopped. He didn't say anything to me."

He paused.

"Then he went back. When he left, I didn't see him off. Then he came again. The road he came by, I know it; my son walked it."

He did not look at Jingjing. Jingjing, standing behind Yonan, did not look at him either.

"He's seventy-one this year," said Yisi. "He waited thirty years."

He raised his cup, not high, to about his chest.

"The road is open."

He drank a mouthful and sat down.

For a long time there was no sound in the courtyard. Then Sargis in the back row shouted something in Syriac, and the monk-soldiers shouted it after him together, until the whole courtyard hummed. The people from the West Market did not understand, and clapped along.

---

Then it was Jingjing.

He was thirty. He was taller than Yisi and shorter than Yonan. His face was very pale; whom he took after in this, no one said. He now had charge of the monastery's translating; of that heap of paper in the east wing, half was in his hand. There were a few scholars in Chang'an who had discussed letters with him and gone away saying: that young deacon at the *hu* monastery speaks better Tang than a Tang man.

What he was to say today, he had drafted the night before. He had written it in parallel prose, the couplets matched just so—*thirty years of wind and frost, in one morning the clouds disperse*; *the three dots of the heart at last become one intertwining bough*—a whole sheet of it. When he had written it he read it through once, and at the second line his own face went red first. He held the sheet to the lamp and burned it, and wrote again. The second draft was only seven or eight lines, and all it set down were things he had seen with his own eyes. When he had written it he did not read it again, but folded it and put it under his inkstone.

Today he did not take it out either. Those few lines, he remembered.

He stood up, first made a bow to Yonan, then made a bow to A-Lian. It was a Tang bow, the bow of a disciple to his elders, made very correctly, exactly like the bow he had made to Old Duan the year he was ten.

"The elder Yonan is my teacher," he said.

He spoke the Chang'an tongue, very clearly, not fast, not slow, and those few people from the West Market standing at the courtyard gate understood every word.

"Twenty years ago, in Xizhou, I taught seven elders to speak Tang. I wrote the characters on a wooden board, very big. Water, fire, gate, road. The other elders traced them, and when they'd learned one, asked for the next. Only the elder Yonan never asked for the next."

"He wrote only one character. He wrote it for twenty years, and writes it better than I do."

Jingjing paused.

"That character is *heart*."

The courtyard was very quiet. The jujube blossom fell, fell on the benches, fell on people's shoulders.

"The elder Yonan told me that this character was taught him many years ago by someone in Chang'an. He was slow, and learned only this one," said Jingjing. "Today, the one who taught him that character has come. From now on, he can learn the second."

He turned to A-Lian and made her another bow.

"Lady A-Lian," he said, "from now on, the elder's Tang characters I leave in your hands."

A-Lian sat there looking at him.

She looked a long time. Her hands lay on her knees; on her blue skirt there was a small smudge of flour, probably brushed on in the shop that morning before she came out. Her finger rubbed at that smudge once, and again, and did not rub it away.

"Yes," she said.

That one word.

Jingjing sat down.

Ishaq beside him was wiping his eyes. Haddad kept his head down over his soup. Yisi held that earthenware cup, looked at the little wine left in it, and did not move.

Only Yonan had his head up, looking at Jingjing, looking a long time, as if to fix his face in memory. Jingjing remembered that twenty years before, in that little room in Ctesiphon, the first time Yonan saw him, he had looked at him just like this.

---

Narsai had stood at the edge of the courtyard all the while.

He did not sit on a bench, nor drink the soup. When Yisi raised his cup, he did not raise his. When the monk-soldiers shouted, he did not shout. He stood in the gallery of the east wing, his back against a pillar, a reed pen and a small knife in his hands, trimming.

A sliver of reed skin fell, and another.

Someone brought him a bowl of soup. He nodded, and did not take it, so the man set the bowl on the step of the gallery. The soup slowly cooled, and a white skin of fat formed on it.

When Jingjing had sat down, he glanced towards the gallery.

Narsai was looking at him too. Their eyes met once. Narsai's eyes were still like two stones; nothing could be read in them. He finished trimming the pen, tried the tip with his thumb, and put the knife away in his sleeve.

He nodded once to Jingjing, turned, and left.

As he left, he passed under the jujube tree, past Yonan and A-Lian. Yonan saw him and stood up.

Narsai did not stop. He walked past.

Yonan stood there watching his back, watching him go out of the back courtyard gate. After a while Yonan sat down again. A-Lian turned her head and glanced at him, and did not ask.

---

When it was nearly dark, the guests dispersed one after another.

As the people from the West Market left they each bowed to Yonan and said a heap of lucky words, and Yonan answered them one by one, haltingly. The people under the locust tree outside the gate finished their mutton soup and dispersed too; one child, before leaving, brought the empty bowl in, licked perfectly clean.

Jingjing was helping to put away the benches in the back courtyard.

He was carrying a bench towards the west wing, and halfway there someone behind him called.

"Deacon."

It was A-Lian.

She held a bowl, steaming.

"I hear the deacon's stomach isn't strong," she said. "A whole day of cold wine—this will settle it."

Jingjing looked down at the bowl. In it was hot water, with a few slices of ginger floating on top, cut very fine.

He put down the bench and took the bowl. It was hot; he held it in both hands.

"Thank you, Lady A-Lian," he said.

A-Lian looked at him. Her eyes were black in the dusk; nothing could be read in them. She nodded, turned and went, back to the jujube tree to clear the bowls and chopsticks from the table. Yonan stood there waiting for her and helped her stack the bowls, stacked them crookedly, and she stacked them again.

Jingjing stood in the gallery and drank that bowl of ginger water.

When he had drunk it, he washed the bowl clean and put it back in the kitchen.

---

That night Yisi and Jingjing went back to their lodging together.

They lived in a small courtyard east of the monastery; as in Lingzhou in those days, the room had a low table, two mats, and the small round shield hanging on the wall. Under the shield, on its wooden peg, the short mace still hung; it had not been taken down in years.

The two of them walked along the monastery's passage, and neither spoke. The moon had not risen yet; the passage was dark, and only the small lamp at the monastery gate showed a little light far off.

At the courtyard gate, Yisi stopped.

"That bowl of water she gave you," said Yisi. "You drank it."

"I drank it," said Jingjing.

Yisi said nothing more. He pushed open the courtyard gate and went in first.

Jingjing followed, took two steps, and said one more thing.

"It was hot."
