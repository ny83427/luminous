# Volume Six　The Shuofang Army

## Chapter 32　The Order

> In the third year, there was a monk of Daqin named Jihe, who looked to the stars and turned toward our civilisation, and gazed at the sun and came to pay homage to the Most High.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

In the fifth month of the second year of Baoying, a caravan from the west reached Chang'an first and brought word that the little deacon of the Yining Ward was coming back; he had crossed the Long Mountains already, and would arrive in a few days. Yisi heard it, nodded, and went south of the city to see to his gruel kitchen as usual; the next day too, and the third. Early on the fourth day he led the bay horse out of the Kaiyuan Gate and went ten-odd *li* west, to the Linggao post station. Most people going west were seen off here; most coming back from the west were welcomed here too. At the station gate stood a few old willows, their lower branches stripped bare by people breaking off twigs, though higher up the leaves had grown thick. Yisi stood under the willows all morning, and in the afternoon a streak of yellow dust rose on the road to the west.

The camel train moved very slowly. At its head came a grey horse, and the rider sat very straight, very tall, thin, a layer of dark fuzz sprouting on his chin. Yisi watched until the horse drew near before he knew the rider was Jingjing. Jingjing had seen him too; he froze a moment on horseback, jumped down, and leading the reins came and stopped three paces in front of him. He was taller again than when he left, his face burned dark, his lips cracked and peeling, a bluish ring under his eyes, and as he walked he dragged his left foot a little—probably his shoe had rubbed his foot raw. He said: "Pa, I'm back."

The first thing Yisi asked about was his stomach. Jingjing burst out laughing, and laughing, his eyes went red; he said going out he'd drunk snow-water once below the Pamirs and squatted most of the night, that once, and never again after; coming back, afraid of crossing the mountains in winter, he'd spent a winter at Balkh keeping Elder Ahad company and left only when spring opened; Elder Ahad was still alive, only his eyes were worse. Yisi said he knew all that, put out his hand and pressed it on Jingjing's shoulder, pressed hard, and having pressed it, took his hand back.

---

Behind the camel train came three men in grey robes, and Jingjing presented them to Yisi one by one.

The first was called Azarel, a little over sixty, tall, his back very straight, a white beard hanging to his chest; he walked slowly and spoke slowly, pausing after each sentence as if each had first to be weighed in his mouth. He had been sent by the Patriarch, and after proclaiming the order was to go on round the monasteries at Luoyang, Lingzhou and the rest, and when he had seen them, write back to the Patriarchate and await its wishes; of the three he was the first. Luoyang had been plundered by the Uighurs again the winter before, and how the dozen-odd households in the Xiushan Ward had come through it this time Yisi still had no sure word; when Elder Azarel heard it, he said he would go to Luoyang first. The second was called Marya, round-faced, round-bellied, a preacher, whose voice was by nature as big as a bell; Jingjing told Yisi afterwards that Elder Marya had preached most of a year along the road, and by the end, whenever the camel-drivers saw him open his mouth, they went off to feed the camels; by the time he reached Chang'an he had preached himself hoarse. The third was called Samaan, small and thin, his beard straggly; before he spoke he first folded both hands into his sleeves, and he clasped in his arms a leather-covered account book which he carried wherever he went; when he made his bow to Yisi, Yisi saw the book was already half filled.

Elder Marcus had not come back; he had stayed in the Two Rivers. Jingjing said the elder had asked for a small room in the back courtyard of the Patriarchate, and sat at its door every day in the sun, telling his beads and speaking to no one. The three old believers had all come back, though. The old medicine-dealer who had been to Fulin climbed down from a camel's back, his legs gave way, he knelt on the ground, and then lay flat on his face and did not get up for a long while; when the others went to help him up he waved them off, said no need, he only wanted to lie a while on the ground of Chang'an.

---

That night Yisi boiled a pot of water, boiled it very hot, poured it into a wooden basin, set it on the floor before Jingjing, and told him to put his feet in. Jingjing sat on the mat and took off his shoes; the sole had worn through in a hole, and his sock was stuck to the blood blisters on his foot, and he winced as he pulled it off. Both feet were calloused all over, the heels split in several cracks, the little toe of the left foot swollen shiny. He put his feet in the water and drew in his breath at the heat. Yisi squatted by the basin looking at those two feet, reached into the basin, scooped up water and poured it over the tops of Jingjing's feet, poured once, and again. Jingjing bowed his head and looked at his father's hand: a very big hand, the back of it covered in old calluses, the water running down between the fingers and back into the basin. The pot on the stove was still steaming; the room was so quiet only the sound of the water could be heard; and only then did Jingjing speak. "Pa, Uncle's gone." Yisi's hand stopped above the water.

Jingjing told it from the beginning. He told of the board struck in the mornings at the Patriarchate, of his uncle every morning biting the knot tight with his teeth, of his uncle asking questions so finely that if you said one number wrong he'd point it out next time, of his uncle asking what had become of that basin of fire, and when he said it hadn't gone out, his uncle nodding. He told how the Patriarchate had had no peace these years, how his uncle never slept two nights running in one room. He told of that night in the room beside the storehouse, of his uncle's left hand gripping his wrist, of his uncle's last three sentences, not one word changed. Then he told of Baluma: of the lamp oil, of the bread money, of the riverbank, of telling Baluma to go, of Baluma on the third morning after he left lying at the gate of the Patriarchate in a new pair of shoes, of the stake with nothing carved on it.

When he had finished, the water in the basin was no longer hot. Yisi had stayed squatting there all the while, his hand still resting on the basin's rim, water dripping drop by drop from his fingers. He asked one thing: "Was he in pain?" Jingjing remembered how, seven years ago, when he first came to the Patriarchate, he too had wanted to ask that, thinking of his uncle's arm, and that time had not got it out. He said that when his uncle spoke, it didn't sound like pain.

Jingjing looked at him, his lips moving several times, before he got out the question he had held in all the way home: "Pa, letting Baluma go—was it wrong of me?" Yisi did not answer at once; he stood, went to the stove, ladled a dipper of hot water from the pot, came back and poured it slowly into the basin, and the water in the basin steamed again. He put the dipper down, squatted again, and said: "Your uncle gave you words to pass on, and you passed them on." And as he said it he scooped up water again and poured it over the tops of Jingjing's feet.

---

Jingjing brought out his uncle's things. One was the Patriarch's order, a roll wrapped in oilcloth, sealed with wax, the Patriarch's seal pressed into the wax; this roll was in Elder Azarel's keeping, and would be opened before everyone tomorrow. The other was only a paper, folded into a small square, unsealed: what his uncle had written to Yisi. Jingjing said that when his uncle gave it to him he said: take it with you when you go back; that day was less than a month before his uncle died.

Yisi took the paper and unfolded it. There were only a few lines on it, in the country speech of Balkh, spelled in Syriac letters, the letters leaning to the left, one by one, like men walking into the wind. When Yisi was small and learning his letters, it was Shapur who taught him; Shapur held his hand and wrote stroke by stroke. In those days Shapur's letters slanted to the right, slanted very handsomely; Yisi had studied all his life and never got them right. Now these few lines leaned left. On the paper was written: "Your boy back to you. A head taller than when he went. Stomach the same as ever. The order's attached separately; do as it says. Take care of yourself. Your brother."

Yisi folded the paper back into its small square and put it inside his coat, against his chest. Jingjing lifted his feet out of the basin; Yisi handed him a cloth, and he dried his feet. That night the lamp in Yisi's room stayed lit; Jingjing lay next door and heard his father saying scripture, in the country speech of Balkh, all night long.

---

At the hour of *si* the next day the whole monastery gathered in the hall. The seven teachers, the twenty-four guard brothers, Xu Jing, the old believers, the new believers, Tang, *hu*, filled the hall to overflowing; Official Pei came too, not in his official robe, and stood at the back. Elder Azarel stood beneath the wooden cross on the east wall, broke the wax seal before them all, unrolled the order, and read it out in Syriac sentence by sentence, pausing after each, while Jingjing, standing beside him, put it into Tang.

The order spoke first of offices. Chang'an was to have one Elder of Preaching, in charge of preaching, teaching and examination, to be held by Elder Marya; one Deacon of Charity, in charge of gruel, clothing, medicine, the gathering of the dead and the taking in of foundlings, held for the time being by Jingjing, who would be ordained to it when he came of age; one Warden of the Rule, in charge of rules, accounts, registers, rewards and punishments, to be held by Elder Samaan; Yisi was to be Superintendent of Chang'an, and the three offices above were all under the Superintendent's direction. When it came to "held for the time being by Jingjing," there was a little murmuring in the hall; Jingjing paused a moment at that point, went red, and went on translating, translating his own name as flatly as he translated anyone else's. When it came to the Warden of the Rule, Yisi glanced at Sabrisho. Sabrisho had kept the rule in the monastery these years, and kept it to the letter; under that felt-like beard of his nothing could be read. When Azarel had read this article, Sabrisho took from his coat an old book—the rules he had written down in his five years in Chang'an—went up to Samaan, and held it out in both hands; and Samaan took it in both hands.

Then came the four matters Chang'an had asked, and the order answered them one by one. The first: Chang'an would for the present have no bishop; what a bishop should decide would be decided by the Superintendent together with the Warden of the Rule and the elders in council; if anyone in the monastery did grave wrong and it was proposed to cut him off, it was to be reported to the Patriarchate, and until the Patriarchate's reply came, he was not to be cut off. At this several people in the hall glanced toward Elder Yonan; Elder Yonan kept his head bowed, his two hands folded before him, just as on the night they debated Baaluo. The second: the robe the Son of Heaven had granted might be accepted, and worn when meeting officials and on great occasions, not at ordinary worship. The third: the Tang hymns might be sung, once examined by the Elder of Preaching; those that passed might be sung. When Narsai heard this article the corners of his mouth sank; Granny He, standing at the back, when Jingjing had translated it, wiped her eyes with her sleeve. The fourth: if portraits of the sacred countenances were granted by the government again, they were to be enshrined in a separate room, and not brought into the main hall. Official Pei marked this one in his heart.

Then came the plan of conduct, five articles in all. The first: not to contend in words with the other religions; Buddhists, Daoists, Zoroastrians, Manichaeans, each had their own law; not to dispute, not to revile, not to enter their gates and trouble them. At this article the hall was so quiet the birds could be heard in the locust tree outside. Baaluo had been gone more than two years; of the nine who had been released, six were still in the monastery, and they were all standing at the back of the hall now, heads bowed. Narsai stood at the very edge, his eyes on the floor the whole time, and did not raise them. The second: to establish trust through charity. The third: all the faithful to be in companies of fifty, each company with one man as its head; matters to be reported first to the company head, and by the company head to the deacon. At this article several of the old soldiers laughed. In the year of the gruel at the Qujiang, fifty to a block, sitting to receive it—this was where it came from; they had never thought this way of doing things would travel from Chang'an to the Two Rivers, and come back written in the Patriarch's order. The fourth: every year three men were to be trained who could preach in Tang. When Ishaq heard this article he sighed and said: three! He'd studied six years and still said *mo pa*, "don't be afraid," as *mo pa*, "grind and crawl"—and the whole hall laughed. The fifth: all dealings with the government to go by one line only, through Yisi and the official surnamed Pei; no one else might form private connections. Official Pei, standing at the back, when Jingjing had translated this article, opened his mouth; he had never imagined that the Patriarch in the Two Rivers would know his surname was Pei.

When Azarel had finished reading, he rolled up the order and gave it to Yisi. Yisi took it and did not open it. The order had been fair-copied by another hand; the writing was a Patriarchate scribe's; but whose mind was in it, he knew. He said one word: "Good."

---

That evening the monastery held a feast to wash off the dust of the road, in the back courtyard under the jujube tree, several long tables pushed together, and on them mutton broth, sesame cakes, steamed buns, stewed radish, pickles, and a jar of wine. The mutton broth had been stewed by one of the monastery's old believers the way Elder Ahad used to do it, stewed most of the day, the radish falling apart at a touch of the chopsticks. The steamed buns Hou Siyuan had brought, a big basketful; he said they were from the kitchen of the Guo mansion; this was what Lord Guo ate in the mornings, and he'd taken a few extra along the way, not knowing whether the elders from the Two Rivers would take to them. Elder Azarel tried one, chewed a long time, and said something in Syriac, which Jingjing translated: the elder said these buns have no taste, but once you've eaten one you want another. Hou Siyuan laughed till he pounded the table, and said he'd take that back and tell the kitchen.

Halfway through the meal, music started up in a corner of the back courtyard: some of the monastery's believers—an old Sogdian on the *bili* pipe, a young Tang man on the *pipa*, and a Uighur woman holding a *konghou* harp, and when her fingers plucked the strings the sound flowed out like water. When the *bili* began the whole courtyard fell quiet, and a few young men stood up and sang a song. It was sung in Tang; Babai, with a few literate Tang believers, had pieced it together character by character these two years; they had learned it before the order came, and no one had examined it yet. The first two lines were: "Alaha made heaven and earth; sun and moon and stars are all lamps he lit." It did not yet run smoothly; a few characters had tones that would not fit the tune, and the singers had to bend round them to get through.

The three elders could not understand it, and sat watching the young men's mouths open and shut. Elder Azarel turned his head and asked Jingjing what they were singing, and Jingjing translated it into Syriac line by line, very slowly; by the time he finished a line the young men were already singing the next, and he had to chase after them. Azarel listened and listened, raised his hand and wiped his eyes once, and when he had wiped them, wiped them again. Elder Marya told Jingjing hoarsely that from now on, to examine these hymns, Jingjing would have to translate them for him line by line before he could examine them at all. Elder Samaan clasped his account book; the book was closed. Jingjing, translating, listened to those few characters the singers bent round, and noted them one by one in his heart.

Xu Jing drank too much, leaned up to Yisi, patted his shoulder with that hand short two fingers, and said a great string of things: that the elders were good elders, the little deacon was a good deacon, that his life was one this monastery had picked up off the ground; and talking, he leaned against the jujube tree and fell asleep. Yisi sat at one end of the long table; he did not drink; before him were two bowls, one his own, one with a chipped rim holding a pinch of earth. He listened to that song throughout, until it ended.

---

When the feast broke up, it was past the second watch. The guests had all gone, the elders all gone to rest; Yisi first tucked Du De's bowl into his coat, and then he and Jingjing cleared the bowls and dishes from the table and took them to the well; Jingjing wanted to draw water and wash them, and Yisi said: tomorrow. The two of them walked toward the front courtyard. On the stone block to the left of the monastery gate stood a small lamp, an oil dish with a paper shade over it; the monastery had lit it for many years, lit at dark, put out only at dawn, so that anyone at the end of his road in the night, seeing the lamp, would know there was a door here. The shade was of paper pasted on in several layers, smoked yellow; a wind came through the ward, the flame wavered once, and stood straight again.

Yisi stopped under the lamp, and Jingjing stopped too. The light came up from below on the dark fuzz on Jingjing's chin, on those dark eyes of his. Yisi asked him in the country speech of Balkh: "Is the road still open?"

Jingjing stood still. He had heard those words twice in that small room in Ctesiphon, and the man who said them had said them in the country speech of Balkh, the same as his father now. He thought of the snow-water stream below the Pamirs, of the boat going downstream from the ferry on the Tigris, of the room beside the storehouse, of the stake at the edge of the burial ground with nothing carved on it; and then he said: "It's open."

The two of them went into the room. It was dark inside; Yisi felt for the lamp-stand and lit the lamp, took Du De's bowl from his coat and set it on the table, then went to the cupboard and opened its door. Two years ago, on the day Jingjing left, he had put Jingjing's bowl away in this cupboard. He took that bowl out, and set it beside Du De's bowl.
