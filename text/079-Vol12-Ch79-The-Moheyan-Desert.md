# Volume Twelve　Jade Gate

## Chapter 79　The Moheyan Desert

> The Valley of the Dawn came in awe, and the Cave of the Moon gathered in full.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

Early in the spring of the second year of Dazhong something great happened in the city of Shazhou. For some days the people of the city hardly went out; Tibetan soldiers went through the streets, troop after troop, mounted, swords at their sides, back and forth through the city. Jingfu's company lived in the gallery of that small Buddhist monastery east of the city. The monastery's old monk went out once every morning, and when he came back his face was tighter every day. Asked what was happening in the city, he wouldn't say, only: "Don't go out." One night in the third month the city suddenly broke into shouting—first the north of the city, then the east, then the whole city—shouting in Tang, very loud, people beating gongs, people beating basins, until the whole city rang. Salang climbed the monastery wall to look and saw fires risen in several places in the city, people running in the streets, people holding up torches, and under the torches a banner, red, with one character on it, too far off to make out.

At daybreak the shouting in the city stopped. The old monk came back from outside, the colour of his face changed, his eyes red. He stood at the monastery gate and told the people in the gallery:

"The Tang in the city have risen. A man named Zhang, Zhang Yichao, of a great family of Shazhou, led the city's Tang and in one night killed the Tibetan commander and drove the Tibetan soldiers out of the city. The banners over the city gates have been changed—changed to the Tang house's banners." As he spoke his voice began to shake. "The Tibetans have ruled Shazhou sixty-odd years. Sixty-odd years the Tang in the city have worn their hair in Tibetan plaits, worn Tibetan robes, spoken Tibetan. This morning all the Tang in the city are cutting off their plaits."

That day Jingfu went out of the monastery gate into the city to look. The streets were full of people, men and women, old and young, standing by the road, some with scissors, some with knives, cutting off plaits one after another; the cut plaits were thrown on the ground, thrown the length of the street. Some, when they had cut theirs, felt the backs of their heads and wept; some, when they had cut theirs, knelt on the ground and knocked their heads toward the east. An old woman past seventy sat on the threshold of a shop with a handful of cut plait gripped in her hand, weeping and saying to those beside her:

"When I was small my Pa told me: we're Tang, and sooner or later we'll go back. My Pa died; my Pa didn't live to see it. I've lived to see it."

Jingfu stood at the side of the street watching. He was wearing the black robe he had brought from Chang'an; the Bureau of Receptions' list said he was a man of An, to be sent back to his own country. He had come out of Tang country, travelled more than two years, gone out of the Tang borders and into Tibetan country, and today the city he stood in had become Tang's again. The old woman on the threshold noticed him, looked at his black robe, looked at his face.

"Are you Tang too?"

"I was born in Chang'an, the Yining Ward of Chang'an," said Jingfu. "But the Bureau of Receptions' list says I'm a man of An, to be sent back to my own country."

The old woman didn't understand and asked again: "Are you Tang or aren't you, in the end?"

Jingfu thought.

"I'm a man of Chang'an," he said. "Chang'an has sent me out."

The old woman set the handful of plait she was gripping down on the threshold, reached out and felt his sleeve.

"This robe of yours is Chang'an cloth."

Jingfu stood before her until she let go, and only then turned and went back to the monastery.

---

He Qi's caravan reached Shazhou in the fifth month of that year. It came from Xizhou by way of the Moheyan desert—twenty-odd camels carrying Xizhou's cotton cloth, Khotan's jade, Karashahr's horses—to Shazhou to trade for silk and tea. On the day it arrived a ring of people gathered at the gate of Shazhou. The city's Tang had just driven out the Tibetans, and at the gate stood their own soldiers, who questioned the caravan about where it had come from.

"From Xizhou."

"Who rules Xizhou? Do they rule it well?"

"The Uighurs rule it," said the caravan's headman. "Better than the Tibetans."

The soldiers laughed and let them into the city.

Jingfu met him at a hostel in the city. That day he went to the hostel to ask about caravans going west, and asked the hostel-keeper in passing whether he knew a man of Xizhou surnamed He, called He Qi, who used to keep a silk shop in the West Market of Chang'an.

"Know him? Old He—runs the road between Xizhou and Shazhou," said the hostel-keeper. "One of his has just come in. They're unloading in the back yard."

Jingfu went to the back yard. A man was standing there with his back to him, talking to the camel-drivers—a man of fifty or so, not tall, a little stooped, an old cloth wound round his head, his beard white, his face burned dark red by the sun, its wrinkles so deep they could hold sand. The drivers called him Old He. Old He's voice was hoarse, his throat worn down by wind and sand. Jingfu stood at the back-yard gate listening to him talk, listened a few sentences, and made out the accent—the accent of the West Market of Chang'an, the Tang a Sogdian speaks. He took two steps forward, and the man turned his head.

"He Qi." Jingfu knew him first.

The man was startled; he looked at Jingfu's black robe, then at his face, and his mouth fell open.

"Director?" he said. "Director, how are you here?"

---

He Siniang saw her brother that evening. Jingfu brought He Qi back to the small Buddhist monastery east of the city. He Siniang was boiling water in the gallery, Lingbao lying on his felt, Liu'er and Ayi picking up firewood in the yard. He Qi came in at the monastery gate, saw the woman crouched by the stove in the gallery, and stopped.

"Sister."

He Siniang turned her head, and the tongs fell from her hand to the ground. She stood up and looked at him—at his white beard, at that sun-darkened face—and knew him.

"He Qi, you've grown old."

"Sister, you've grown old too."

He Siniang went to him first and reached out and felt his face, felt the lines on it one by one.

"When I saw you off outside the Kaiyuan Gate you were in your early forties, and your face was white," she said. "Now your beard's whiter than Pa's when he died."

"The sun in the west is fierce, the wind's strong; half the year I'm on the desert; a man ages fast," said He Qi. "The year I left, Sister, you said you wouldn't go. A hundred times I've wondered how you were faring in Chang'an, whether the Great Worthy was still alive, whether the monastery was still there. A hundred times, and I never thought I'd meet you in a Buddhist monastery in Shazhou."

He Siniang brought him to Lingbao. Lingbao, lying on his felt, had heard; he pushed himself up to sit, leaning his head forward.

"Is it He Qi?"

He Qi knelt before him. "Great Worthy, it's me."

Lingbao reached out, found He Qi's shoulder, found his arm.

"He Qi, you've grown thin. Your bones dig into my hand."

"The food in the west doesn't fatten a man."

"That day outside the Kaiyuan Gate I blessed your caravan, and what I recited was *The Lord shall preserve thy going out and thy coming in*," said Lingbao. "That day I couldn't see you. Today I can feel you. The Lord has preserved you."

He Qi, kneeling, took a small cloth bundle from his breast and opened it. Inside was a wooden cross, of jujube wood, two inches or so long, the four ends whittled round, threaded on a red cord—the red cord faded now to white.

"This cross Sister gave me; it was left by Sister's first husband, Kang Shouxin," he said. "I've worn it eight years, travelled eight years of dry roads. Dry roads aren't damp; the cross hasn't rotted."

He Siniang reached out and touched that faded cord, and said nothing.

"When I left I said there was no future for *hu* in Chang'an. I was right; you've all been sent out by Chang'an," said He Qi. "But I was wrong too. I carried this cross west, and Xizhou has a church. When I reached Xizhou I stood in the church north of the city. There was no priest in the church; a few old people recited the scriptures empty-handed, and when they reached the offering bread, stopped. I stood at the back and stopped with them. Every time I stopped I thought: in the monastery in Chang'an, today they're breaking the bread; the Great Worthy is breaking it."

"The monastery in Chang'an is gone," said Lingbao.

"So I say I was half right," said He Qi. "The half I was right about is Chang'an; the half I was wrong about is this cross. The cross goes with the people."

---

That night, in the gallery, He Qi told his sister about the family.

"The wife I took out of Chang'an died in Xizhou the year before last, of dysentery. I haven't married again," he said. "My two sons are both in Xizhou—the elder runs the caravans with me; he's in the back yard unloading—the younger is in the city of Xizhou learning the fur trade; my daughter married into a Sogdian household of Xizhou. Sister, when you see them, none of them will know you. The elder was only ten the year we left."

The caravan rested half a month at Shazhou, traded its goods, and made ready to go back to Xizhou.

"I'll take you all," said He Qi. "That stretch of the Moheyan desert—eight hundred *li*, no water, no grass, no people; eight or nine days' going, and one wrong step and you've walked into the sand. In the old days the Dharma Master Xuanzang, crossing this stretch, took the wrong road and for four nights and five days had not a mouthful of water to drink, and lay in the sand waiting to die, and it was his horse that smelled water and carried him to the spring's edge." He said, "This road I've travelled eight years, a dozen-odd times. Every spring, every ruined beacon tower, every heap of bones, I know. Follow me: when I say stop, stop; when I say go, go. I'll share out the water myself, and no one is to drink one mouthful extra."

In his caravan there was a young man of twenty or so, a Sogdian named Kang Zhen, a man of Xizhou, who had charge of the caravan's water skins and camel bells.

"Kang Zhen belongs to the church north of the city of Xizhou," He Qi told Jingfu. "His great-great-grandfather was once a deacon of that church. The church was the one Master Jingjing helped repair in his day when he came east with the seven elders. Since then the church's people have grown fewer generation by generation, and now there are only a few old people left. As a child Kang Zhen learned some Syriac letters in the church, not many. At the offering once in seven, whenever he's in Xizhou, he goes, stands at the back, and recites empty-handed with the old people."

Kang Zhen stood listening and looked at Jingfu.

"Director from Chang'an," he asked, "what was Master Jingjing to you?"

"My Master."

Kang Zhen's eyes widened.

"This is a saying handed down in my family generation after generation; my grandfather told it to me. Master Jingjing lived in the church north of the city that year, at eleven," he said. "While the elders repaired the church, he pulled the weeds in the courtyard, pulled them a month. When the weeds were pulled, the elders wiped the wooden cross on the east wall of the hall, and he stood on tiptoe underneath, holding it steady."

---

They went out by the Jade Gate Pass at the beginning of the sixth month of that year. The Jade Gate Pass lay north of Guazhou; the pass fort was of earth, half fallen, and no one guarded the pass; the Tibetans had gone, and Zhang Yichao's people had not yet sent troops. He Qi had the caravan rest a night below the pass, filled all the water skins, watered the camels full, and the next day before light went out through the pass.

Out of the pass, to the north-west, was the Moheyan desert. The first two days were stones, black stones stretching beyond sight, sand under the stones; the camels' hooves crunched on them, and once the sun came up the stones were hot, hot enough to feel through the soles of your shoes. He Qi had everyone rest by day and travel by night; by day the camels knelt and the people crouched in the camels' shadow, a felt rigged as an awning against the sun; as soon as it was dark they took the road. Lingbao lay in the cart, three layers of felt over the cart awning; He Siniang sat beside him wiping his face with a damp cloth, and when the cloth dried, wet it with a little water—water she saved from her own share.

On the fourth day, where they halted at dawn, there were bones all round—camel bones, horse bones, some men's too—bleached white by the sun, half buried in sand by the wind, heap after heap, on both sides of the road. Liu'er wept with fright; Ayi held her and covered her eyes with her hand.

"These are all those who took the wrong road," said He Qi. "Some the sandstorms buried; some ran out of water and fell and couldn't get up. Don't look. Follow the camels; the camels know the road."

On the night of the sixth day a wind rose. It came from the north, carrying sand, striking the face like flung stones. The camels would go no further; they knelt and buried their heads in the sand. He Qi had everyone ring the camels in a circle, and the people crouched within the ring of camels with felts over their heads, crouched half the night. There were voices in the wind—as if someone were weeping, as if someone were shouting, as if someone far off were calling names.

"That's the sound of the sand, not people," said He Qi. "No one is to answer. Answer, and you'll follow it, and walk into the sand."

Liu'er shook under the felt. Ayi sang at her ear, in Uighur; Jingfu couldn't understand the words, but he knew the tune—a tune of the Manichaean temple. He sang too; he sang *In the highest heavens, deep reverence and praise*. He sang, and He Siniang sang with him, Elijah sang with him; Salang couldn't sing and hummed along; Lingbao in the cart recited along. In the noise of the wind a few voices, thin, thin; each time the wind swept by the voices were blown apart, and when they were blown apart, they joined again.

On the eighth day the water was nearly gone. He Qi gathered the remaining water skins in one place and shared them out one by one: a small bowl a day for each person; none for the camels—the camels could hold out two more days. That day Lingbao failed. His lips were so dry they cracked open and seeped blood. He Siniang fed him her own bowl of water; he drank one mouthful and would drink no more.

"Give it to the children."

"The children have theirs," said He Siniang.

"Then give it to Salang. Salang's carried that jar of ash all night and drunk no water."

"I'm not thirsty," said Salang.

"I've heard. Your voice has gone hoarse."

That evening He Qi saw his sister pour her own bowl into Lingbao's water skin again. He went over and snatched the skin away.

"Sister, the rule is mine: one bowl each, and no one may give his up," he said. "If you give yours up, tomorrow you'll fall in the sand, and the Great Worthy still needs someone to hold him up—who'll hold him up?"

"I'm not thirsty."

"Whether you're thirsty or not, I can see. Your lips are cracked in three places."

The two of them stood in the sand, and the caravan's people all watched. He Qi poured the water from the skin back into her bowl, poured it full, and handed it to her.

"Drink. If you don't drink, I'll take back the Great Worthy's bowl too," he said. "I'll do what I say."

He Siniang held the bowl, looked at him, looked a good while, and drank.

On the ninth day, just before dawn, He Qi stood on a camel's back and gazed north-west, gazed a long while, jumped down, and told everyone: "We're there."

Ahead was a hollow, and in the hollow a few clumps of reeds, and under the reeds, water.

The camels smelled it first, rose one after another, and without waiting to be led ran down into the hollow, ran to the water's edge, lowered their heads, and drank in great gulps. The people ran behind them; Salang ran at the very front, reached the water, knelt, untied the earthenware jar from his chest and set it to one side, scooped water in both hands and splashed his face, splashed his whole face, and scooped again and drank. Liu'er and Ayi ran to the water and lay flat and buried their faces in it. Jingfu did not run. He held Lingbao's cart and walked it step by step to the water's edge.

He Qi was already at the water. He took a wooden bowl from his breast, dipped a bowlful at the spring's eye—the water in the spring's eye was clear and cold—carried that bowl of water to the side of the cart, knelt, and brought the bowl to Lingbao's lips.

"Great Worthy, drink. This is a grass pool. Past this one, two days more, and it's Yizhou."

Lingbao found the bowl, held it, drank a mouthful, and another, and when he had drunk it gave the bowl back to him.

"He Qi, you said in your day that there was no future for *hu* in Chang'an."

"Yes."

"You were right," said Lingbao. "Today this mouthful I've drunk is sweet."

He Qi crouched by the cart, dipped another bowl of water, and handed it to He Siniang.

---

Note: In the second year of Dazhong (848) Zhang Yichao of Shazhou led the people in driving out the Tibetan commander and returned the city to the Tang; see the *Xin Tangshu*, "Tibet," the Dunhuang manuscript *Transformation Text of Zhang Yichao*, and others. Shazhou had fallen to the Tibetans in the second year of Zhenyuan (786). In the Tang the Jade Gate Pass lay north of Jinchang County in Guazhou; the Moheyan desert ran more than eight hundred *li* from the Jade Gate Pass to Yizhou; Xuanzang, "four nights and five days without a drop to wet his throat," was led by his horse to a spring—see the *Biography of the Tripitaka Master of the Great Ci'en Monastery*, juan 1. To whom Xizhou belonged at this time is unclear; some say the Uighurs already held it; only in the seventh year of Xiantong (866) did Pugu Jun of the Beiting Uighurs plainly take Xizhou. Kang Zhen is invented.
