# Volume Eleven　Jade Gate

## Chapter 61　The Long Mountains

> In the ninth year of Zhenguan he arrived in Chang'an. The emperor sent his chief minister, Duke Fang Xuanling, with the full honour guard to the western outskirts, to receive him as a guest and bring him in.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

West from Fengxiang there was, properly, a high road: through Longzhou, over the Long Mountains, down to Qinzhou.

They did not take that road.

The Fengxiang Prefecture scribe said Qinzhou belonged to the Tibetans; there were troops garrisoned in the city, the passes were strictly watched, and for decades Tang envoys taking that road had had to wait months. Go north, through Jingzhou, out by the Tanzheng Gorge, to Yuanzhou, to the Xiao Pass; the Tibetans were thinly garrisoned there, and caravans often went that way.

"Sent back to their places of origin," said the scribe. "Sent to the border counts as sent back. Which way they're sent, the register doesn't say."

When he had said it, he wrote the two characters "Jingzhou" on the document of handing over.

So they went north.

Passing Linyou, there was a ruined palace by the road, its walls fallen, wormwood growing on the foundations of its halls, wormwood taller than a man. One of the porters, a local man, said this was the Palace of Nine Perfections, where the Emperor Taizong used to come to escape the summer heat.

Jingfu stood by the road and looked a while.

The stele had written of the Emperor Taizong. Written of how he had his chief minister Duke Fang go to the western outskirts to receive Alopen; of how he looked at the scriptures himself; of how he issued an edict saying this teaching "benefits creatures and profits men, and ought to be practised throughout the world." Jingfu had learned it by heart as a boy, learned it till it rolled off his tongue.

He looked at the wormwood a while, and caught up with the column.

---

The day they reached Jingzhou, it rained.

Jingzhou was a border town. The town was small, the soldiers many. Of those walking in its streets, six in ten wore army clothes. Outside the town was the River Jing, flowing in from the west, its water muddy, yellow shot with green. A few dozen *li* further west, it was no longer Tang land.

An Administrative Supervisor of the prefecture came out to take the handover. He was forty or so, his face dark, as if from years of wind on the city wall. He called the names, and when he had finished did not at once have them taken off, but stood there thinking a while.

Then he said: Jingzhou is short of households.

Border prefectures short of households was the old trouble of these decades. Every war, people fled; those who fled did not come back, and those who came back could not farm. Outside the town a great deal of land lay waste, so waste that trees grew on it. Every year the prefecture reported up, and every year it was short.

"Among you," said the Supervisor, "is there anyone who doesn't want to go?"

No one in the courtyard spoke.

"Anyone who doesn't want to go," said the Supervisor, "can enrol in Jingzhou, be granted land, and be a household under the Two-Tax law. Tax-free for the first three years."

When he had said it, he waited.

After a long while, among the *hu* households at the foot of the wall, a man stood up. He was thirty or so, with a frostbite scar on his face, and beside him a woman and two children. Where his family came from, no one on the road had asked, and they themselves did not say.

The man said: "We've farmed."

The Supervisor nodded and called a copyist over to write down the family's names. While the names were being written, the man thought a while and gave a Tang name. The copyist asked his surname, and he said Shi. The copyist wrote Shi.

The woman had not spoken at all. She held the smaller child and looked at the Supervisor, then at the brush in the copyist's hand, watching that brush write the character Shi on the paper.

Then the old man of the Muhu.

He stood up too. He stood very slowly, and once he was up, glanced toward the east.

"Here," he said in his poor Tang speech, "I'm near Chang'an."

The Supervisor looked at him once, saw his white beard, and said: "You must be seventy. You can't work granted land."

The old man said: "I don't want land."

"Then what will you do here?"

The old man thought and said: "Wait."

The Supervisor did not ask him what for. He had the copyist write down the old man's name too, after the household surnamed Shi, and what he wrote was "lodger."

The few others of the Muhu gathered round the old man and talked to him, talked a long time. The old man shook his head. They talked more, and still he shook his head. Afterwards they stopped talking, and one by one went to him and held him a moment.

That night the old priest of the Zoroastrian shrine untied his jar once, reached in, and pinched out a little, which was ash. He wrapped the pinch of ash in a cloth and gave it to the old man of the Muhu.

The old man took it and said nothing. He tucked the little cloth bundle into his coat.

---

The next day, west from Jingzhou.

They walked two days, and came to the last fort.

The fort stood beneath a mountain gap, built of rammed earth, not large, a faded flag planted on its wall. Through the gap ran a river, flowing out of the west over a jumble of rocks, rushing loud.

The fort commander came out to look at them. He was fifty or so, not tall, a little stooped, his face lined fold on fold as if carved with a knife. He looked at the document of handing over, and then at this string of people, and when he came to Joseph and Elijah he looked twice more.

"Daqin?" he asked.

Jingfu said: "Yes."

The fort commander said "Oh."

He did not let them go at once. He had someone boil a pot of water and let everyone rest at the foot of the fort wall and drink something hot. He himself squatted beside Jingfu with a bowl, drinking too.

After a while he said: "My grandad was in the Shuofang Army."

Jingfu looked at him.

"In Shuofang back in the Tianbao years," said the fort commander. "Afterwards he followed Lord Director Guo, fought at Xiangji Temple."

Jingfu said nothing.

"My grandad said," said the fort commander, "that day at Xiangji Temple there was a heavy fog. There was a *hu* monk who went east to scout the way, three times. The third time he came back with an arrow in his face, the skin of his brow flapped down over his eyes. He pulled the arrow out himself, tore that flap of skin off too, and threw it on the ground."

He drank a mouthful of water.

"My grandad said he didn't see it himself. He heard it from an old soldier. That old soldier had a scar across his face, and when he laughed only half of it moved."

Here the fort commander turned his head and looked at Jingfu.

"When I was small I didn't believe it," he said. "Every time Grandad told it, I'd say who'd tear off his own face. And Grandad would rap my head with his pipe."

Jingfu said: "That was my grand-teacher."

The fort commander was taken aback. He looked Jingfu up and down once.

"You don't look it," he said.

"I was found," said Jingfu.

The fort commander looked at him a long time, and suddenly laughed. He laughed till the water slopped out of his bowl, slopped onto his knee, and he did not wipe it.

"Found," he said. "Found's good."

---

When they had rested enough, the fort commander stood, took the document of handing over, and made his mark on it.

"Out through this gap," he said, "it's the Tibetans' country. Go on ahead, through the gorge, and north is Yuanzhou. Yuanzhou city's been empty a long while; the Tibetans keep a camp by the city, not many men. North of that is the Xiao Pass. There are Tibetan troops at the Xiao Pass; they'll take a toll."

He thought, and said: "Give them the money, don't argue with them. They don't much kill travellers."

Then he turned to the porters.

"Porters," he said, "turn back here."

The porters had known it all along. They unloaded the bundles from their shoulders and the pots from their poles one by one, set them on the ground, and gave them to their owners. One porter, a Jingzhou man, who had grown friendly with the children of the *hu* households, when he had unloaded, took two dried jujubes from his coat and gave one to each child.

The fort commander had the wages brought and paid them out one by one.

When he came to Salang, the fort commander counted out the money and handed it to him. Salang took it and tucked it in his coat.

Then he bent down and swung Joseph's and Elijah's bundles back onto his shoulder.

The fort commander looked at him.

"You're a porter," said the fort commander. "Porters turn back here."

"Mm," said Salang.

"Past this ravine," said the fort commander, "nobody will pay you."

"Mm," said Salang.

He stuck the staff through the strap of the bundles, iron tip upward, and went to the outside of the column.

The fort commander stood at the gate of the fort and looked at his back, looked a long time.

The Jingzhou porter edged over and asked in a low voice: "Commander, that big fellow—where's he from?"

The fort commander said: "On the Chang'an County register."

The porter's mouth fell open.

---

The river in the gap was called the Tanzheng Gorge water—the water of the plucked zither.

The water ran over the jumbled rocks in tier after tier, the rocks high and low, so that the sound of the water was high and low, plink and plonk, truly as if someone were plucking a zither. The few young men of the Zoroastrian shrine, hearing it for the first time, stood by the river listening a long time, and would not go.

Salang knew nothing of zithers. He only found the water noisy.

Past the gorge, they climbed into the mountains.

The Tang people called the mountains here the Long Mountains, and also the Six Coils, because the road had to wind round six bends to get up. Snow had already fallen in the mountains, not thick, a thin layer, covering the dead grass, covering the rocks; and when the wind blew it rolled up from the ground and struck the face like sand.

The old priest could walk no further.

Salang carried him on his back again. This time the old priest did not refuse. He lay on Salang's back, the jar wedged between them, and said not one word the whole way. Only once, when the wind was especially strong, Salang felt the man on his back trembling, and reached his two hands back and held him up.

That night, they camped on a flat place halfway up the mountain.

They did not dare light an open fire. Jingfu remembered what his master had said, what Uncle Xu had said: an open fire at night shows a long way off. They dug a pit and burned a small pile of sticks at its bottom, and everyone sat round the pit and held their hands down into it to warm them.

Kang Zhen sat beside Jingfu.

In over twenty years at the monastery Kang Zhen had been the one who least liked to talk. He mended shoes, mended clothes, swept the courtyard, copied scripture, did everything except talk. The monastery's people sometimes forgot he could speak.

That night, he spoke.

"I came at fifteen," he said. "With a caravan, along this very road."

Jingfu looked at him.

"It was winter then too," said Kang Zhen. "On this same mountain too, and just like this, we didn't dare light a fire. An old man in the caravan said to me: past this mountain, it's Tang."

He held his hands down into the pit, turned them over, and over again.

"Now I'm going back," he said, "and past this mountain, it isn't Tang."

Jingfu did not take him up.

"My mother," said Kang Zhen, "I can't remember her face. I only remember that by our door there was a channel, and the water in it came down from the mountains, very cold, cold even in summer. When I was small I'd put my feet in it, and after a little I'd have to take them out, they ached so with the cold."

He was quiet a while.

"Thirty-five years," he said. "That channel—I don't know if it's still there."

The fire in the pit was nearly out. Salang put in another stick, and the fire rose a little again, lighting a few faces, red in patches, black in patches.

---

On the third day, they came down the mountain to Yuanzhou.

Yuanzhou city was indeed empty. The walls were still there, the city gate half fallen, the houses in the city only walls with no roofs. Grass grew in the streets, and a fox ran through the grass, ran behind a wall, and looked back at them.

The Tibetans' camp was north of the city, a few dozen tents, horses tethered before them. A few soldiers came out and looked at them, did not stop them, waved them on.

A day further north was the Xiao Pass.

The Xiao Pass was an old pass. Its fort had been built by the Tang; the bricks of the wall were still Tang bricks, one after another, blue-grey, some still stamped with characters. Over the gate there had once been a name-board; the board was gone, and only the two iron hooks it had hung from remained, rusted red.

The pass was held by Tibetan soldiers.

They wore sheepskin robes, the left side lapped over the right, their hair plaited into many small braids that hung on their shoulders. At their waists hung knives, the scabbards bound with copper. Their leader was a man of middle age, his face ochre, a layer of red clay smeared on his cheeks.

Beside him stood an interpreter. The interpreter too wore a Tibetan robe and braided hair, but when he opened his mouth it was Tang speech, with a Guanzhong accent, purer than Salang's.

The interpreter said: to pass, fifty cash a head. Animals extra.

Jingfu counted out the money and paid it.

The Tibetan leader had his soldiers go through the bundles. They did not search closely; they put a hand in and felt, and when they felt something hard, took it out and looked at it.

When they felt inside Joseph's coat, they took out a book.

It was the Syriac scripture Joseph had brought from the Patriarchate, wrapped in three layers of cloth. The soldier peeled the cloth off layer by layer, uncovered the book, leafed through it, could not read it, and handed it to the leader. The leader could not read it either, and handed it to the interpreter.

The interpreter looked at it a while and asked: "What scripture is this?"

Joseph looked at him. He did not understand much Tang speech, but he knew the book was in another man's hands. His hand went out halfway, and drew back again, and gripped his own robe.

"Our scripture," said Jingfu.

"A Buddhist sutra?" asked the interpreter.

Jingfu said: "No."

The interpreter looked at him once. He did not at once put it to the leader. He closed the book, weighed it in his hand, and looked at Jingfu as if waiting for him to change his answer.

Jingfu did not change his answer.

The interpreter sighed, turned his head, and said a few words to the leader in Tibetan. The leader listened, frowning, looked at the book once more, said something, and waved his hand.

The interpreter gave the book back to Joseph.

Joseph took it, held it to his chest, and wrapped the cloth round it layer by layer, very slowly, his hands shaking.

"What did he say?" Jingfu asked the interpreter.

The interpreter said: "He said, if it's not a Buddhist sutra, it's worth nothing."

---

When the bundles had been gone through, the leader caught sight of Salang's staff.

He came over and stood in front of Salang, tipping his head back to look. Salang was a head and a half taller than he. The leader reached out and felt the iron tip of the staff, felt the oily marks gripping hands had worn into it, and said something.

The interpreter said: "He says it's a fine staff. Hand it over for the toll."

Salang said nothing.

Nor did he move. He just stood there, both hands gripping the staff, the staff planted on the ground, level with his shoulder. He did not look at the leader, nor at the interpreter; he looked out beyond the gate tunnel of the pass.

The leader said something again.

The interpreter said: "He says: give it to him."

Still Salang stood.

Under the fort of the pass it went quiet a while. Several Tibetan soldiers laid their hands on their knife hilts. The few from the Zoroastrian shrine stepped back. Jingfu stepped forward, to Salang's side, and took another string of cash from his coat.

The leader looked at the cash, then tipped his head back and looked at Salang.

Salang still stood just so, not moving at all, like a tree grown in the ground.

The leader looked a long time, and suddenly gave a laugh. He took the money, tossed it in his hand, said something to Salang, and turned and went.

"What did he say?" asked Jingfu.

The interpreter said: "He said, this one doesn't look Tang."

Only then did Salang take his eyes from beyond the gate tunnel, and look at the interpreter once.

The interpreter stood there and did not go. He looked at Jingfu, and then at Salang, and then glanced east. East were the Long Mountains, the snow on their tops white in the sun.

He opened his mouth as if to ask something.

The Tibetan leader shouted from inside the gate. The interpreter answered, turned, and ran in.

---

That night, they lodged in a ruined post station outside the fort of the pass.

There was nothing in the station but four walls. Everyone crowded at the foot of the walls, keeping out the wind with their bundles. The old priest was asleep, holding his jar. Joseph held his book. Elijah leaned against Joseph. Kang Zhen leaned against the wall, his eyes open.

Salang could not sleep. He got up, went to the door of the station, and stood.

Outside it was black. The mountains to the east could no longer be seen; only the stars in the sky could be seen, and where the mountains were there were none, as if a piece had been cut out by something.

He stood a while, turned back, and looked at the row of people at the foot of the wall.

No one called names any more. The clerk was gone, the Fengxiang scribe was gone, the Jingzhou Supervisor, the commander of the fort—all gone. From now on there would be no one with a list reading one by one, and saying at the end, "Correct."

So Salang counted for himself.

He counted from beside the door, one by one, to the far end of the wall. When he had finished, he counted back again from the far end of the wall.

"Twenty-five," he said.
