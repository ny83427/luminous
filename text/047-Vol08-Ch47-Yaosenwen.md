# Volume Eight　The Daqin Monastery

## Chapter 47　Yaosenwen

> The lord can act, the subject can record; we raise this great stele to praise the supreme good.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

Late in the fourth month of the first year of Jianzhong, Jingjing took the draft of the stele text to that small room in the back courtyard and asked the elders to come and hear it. The draft had been copied four times all told: the first too messy, the second too much revised, the third with a corner soaked by steam from the stove; the stack in his hands was the fourth: ten lines to a page, eighteen characters to a line, more than seventeen hundred characters. Azarel, Marya and Samaan sat at the head; Babai, Sabrisho and Ishaq on the left; Gabriel and Haddad on the right; Yisi as always sat by the door. Narsai did not come; Jingjing had gone to ask him, and he said he was now a man who pushed a cart and gathered the dead; the room where councils were held, he would not go into.

The Tang composition the elders listened to with difficulty; Jingjing read a passage, then stopped and explained its meaning in Syriac, and read on. The first passage spoke of the Lord—"Behold, the eternal, true and still, before all firsts and without beginning; profound, spiritual and void, after all lasts and wondrously existing"—and he explained that this said that before all things there is One who has no beginning, and after all things it is still this One, who has no end; heaven and earth he made; he is three and yet one; his name is Alaha. He went on to explain the making of man; how man was enticed and went the wrong road, and three hundred and sixty-five false teachings sprang up together; the birth of Meshiha—"A virgin bore the Holy One in Daqin; a luminous star announced the blessing"—and men of Persia saw the star and came with gifts; how he "hung up the luminous sun to break open the dark realm" and "rowed the boat of mercy up to the palace of light," and "at high noon ascended to the Truth"; the rules of the monastery: striking the wood, bowing to the east, keeping the beard, keeping no slaves, the seven daily praises, the offering once in seven days; then Taizong, Alopen, Lord Fang going out to the western outskirts to welcome him, the edict; then Gaozong, the two beatings of the Shengli and Xiantian years, Xuanzong, Suzong, Daizong, reign after reign down to the present emperor; then his Pa; and at the end the hymn of praise. When he had read it he laid the stack of paper on his knees and waited.

Samaan first folded his two hands into his sleeves before he spoke. He said he had noted it sentence by sentence: "boat of mercy" was Buddhist talk; "true and still" was Buddhist talk; "mysterious" and "non-action" were Daoist talk; "wondrously existing," "spiritual and void"—he didn't know whose, but in any case not ours. Last year the new Son of Heaven issued an edict telling the various *hu* each to wear their own clothes and not imitate the Tang; a *hu* in Tang clothes is to be punished; and this stele of yours, from beginning to end, our Lord wears another household's clothes; a Tang walks up to the stele, reads it through, and takes it that Chang'an has one more Buddhist temple, one more Daoist abbey; then whose is this stele, once it's set up? Marya coughed, his voice hoarse, and said he had preached seventeen years in Chang'an; preach "Alaha" and the Tang below can't understand it; preach "the Heavenly Worthy" and they understand; and when you've preached it, tell them bit by bit that this Heavenly Worthy is not that Heavenly Worthy; if you don't first take the words they know, how do you lead them where they don't know? Samaan said: leading and leading, and you lead them back where they do know.

Jingjing waited for the two of them to finish before he spoke. He said the four characters "mysterious and non-active" he had not added; they were the very words of Taizong's edict of the twelfth year of Zhenguan; a hundred and forty years ago the Son of Heaven used these four characters to speak of us; these clothes were put on from that day, put on by the Son of Heaven; the stele writes it as the edict has it, and no one can find fault. He said that on the day Alopen reached Chang'an, not one word he himself spoke could the Tang understand; it came out of his mouth and passed through four mouths before it reached Lord Fang's ear, and by the time it reached Lord Fang's ear it was already Tang; but it was still his word, and Taizong heard it and issued the edict permitting him to build a monastery in the Yining Ward. He said further: let the elders consider who would be the first to come and read this stele: the county registrar, the archivist of the Bureau of Sacrifices, men from the Censorate, the monks of the Ximing Temple, and after them, scholars passing by; these people would not come to worship, would not go into the scripture room; most of them would stand before this stele once in their lives; to those who stand there only once in a lifetime, you must speak in the words they know. The scriptures in the scripture room spoke to those who had come in at the door; he had translated them with Elder Babai sentence by sentence, in Tang characters too, but inside there Alaha was Alaha, and no one would take it for anything else. He said that not everything on the stele was borrowed either: the Lord's names, Alaha, Meshiha, he had not borrowed from another house; three characters, two characters, all our own sounds; the Tang found them awkward to say, and he had not changed them.

Samaan drew his hands out of his sleeves, laid them on his knees, and said: one more thing—what about the wood? He said: the birth of Meshiha you've written; the ascension you've written; in between, how he died, you haven't written one character. The Dharma King Baolu wrote to the Corinthians that we preach Meshiha nailed to the wood, unto the Shihu a stumblingblock and unto the Gentiles foolishness; the Dharma King Baolu wasn't afraid of the Gentiles laughing at him; you are.

The window of the small room was open; the wind from the courtyard stirred the corners of the paper, and Jingjing held them down with his hand. He said he was afraid. He said that when a Tang read this line, the first thing he would understand was that the one we worship broke the king's law and was nailed to wood by the government. In the first year of Shangyuan that censor who memorialized to abolish the monastery had in his hand only a complaint from the Ximing Temple; carve this line, and it would be a complaint carved in stone, that could not be peeled off, could not be burned; in some later year whatever censor wanted the monastery abolished need only walk up to the stele and copy it down. He said that when the Dharma King Baolu said that, he was standing in the synagogue, standing at the city gate, saying it to those who came to listen; when he'd said it, people laughed at him, and he stood there and was laughed at; people beat him, and he stood there and was beaten; and when it was over he was still there and could go on explaining; a stone cannot; once words are carved on stone, no one stands beside them to explain them. He said that was why he did not put him in the words; he would carve him above the words. On the head of the stele, between the two coiling dragons, a space would be left, and a cross carved there, carved large, above all the characters; a Tang coming to the stele would, before he read a single character, see that first; he wouldn't know it; he would ask; when he asked, someone would lead him in at the door, and once in, the scriptures of the scripture room would tell him what that wood was about. In the words too he had left one line—"He took up the seal of the cross, melting the four lights into one unbounded"—for those who knew. He said he had not hidden him; the highest place on the whole stele, he had kept for him.

Samaan asked what would be carved under the cross. Jingjing said clouds, and under the clouds a lotus. Samaan's brows knitted, and he said the lotus was Buddhist. Jingjing said that bronze cross on the east wall of his Pa's old house in Lingzhou had a lotus beneath it, and his Pa had knelt before it who knew how many years; Elder Ahad, coming to the house to eat, had made the sign of the cross before it too, and no one had ever said it was not a cross. Yisi, sitting by the door, raised his head at this and glanced at his son, and lowered his head again.

Sabrisho, stroking his beard, said he had something too. Babai, going through the monastery's old files, had found two incidents: in the Shengli years the monks of Luoyang gathered a crowd in the eastern capital to attack us, calling us a heresy; at the end of Xiantian a band of Daoists in Chang'an jeered at us in the streets and wrote placards to stick on the monastery gate. Jingjing had written both in: "In the Shengli years the sons of Shakya used their strength and raised their voices in the Eastern Zhou; at the end of Xiantian the lesser scholars laughed aloud and slandered in the Western Hao." Sabrisho said that one who kept the rule, reading this, saw two faults in it: one, there were no names—which temple's monks, which abbey's Daoists; the stele should write them plainly, so that later men would know who had bullied us; the other, it was too light: the Ximing Temple's complaint of the first year of Shangyuan, handed to a censor, the stele did not mention with a single character. Ishaq put in beside him: and that business of Baaluo—will that be written? Sabrisho glared at him; Ishaq said he only asked, and when he had said it took up a dry flatbread and ate it himself.

Jingjing said the first article of the plan of conduct in the Patriarchate's order was not to contend in words with the other religions; Elder Azarel had read it out that year in the hall, and Elder Sabrisho was there. He said those two incidents he had written, so that readers of the stele would know we had been beaten, and when it was over were still standing; names he would not write; the Ximing Temple stands in Chang'an, and we stand in Chang'an; we live in one city with them, and will live in it another hundred years; name anyone on a stone, and you've made a hundred years' enmity with him; those who can shelter us are few, and those who can hand in complaints against us are many. He said that right after those two lines he had written of the two Great Virtues Luohan and Jilie, writing that they "together shook out the dark net, and joined again the broken cord"; Elder Ahad had told him on the road that in the year he reached Chang'an the one in charge at the monastery was Jilie; and after that he wrote of the Emperor Xuanzong's having the Prince of Ning and the other four princes come in person to the house of blessing and set up an altar: "The pillar of the Law, bent awhile, was raised the higher; the stone of the Way, tilted for a time, was set upright again." He said: first write that we were beaten, then write that the Son of Heaven protected us; whoever in later times wants to lay hands on us, reading this, will first have to think whose princes these were. The time in the first year of Shangyuan, he said, need not be written: that time our own people struck first, and the one who saved us was Director Liu, and Director Liu, saving us, did not ask us to record his name.

Azarel had listened all the while with his eyes closed; now he opened them and said: as Jingjing has written it; the head of the stele carved with a cross; when the draft is copied out, let all the teachers of the monastery read it through once, and the Tang officials who keep the faith read it once too. Samaan opened his account book, wrote a line, and read it to them: "The stele text does not record the Passion; a cross to be carved on the stele's head; settled by the council. The Warden of the Rule, Samaan, held that it should be recorded; he follows the council." When he had written it, he folded his hands back into his sleeves.

Jingjing made another copy and had Lingbao take it to the small room by the side gate where Narsai lived. Three days later Lingbao brought it back; on the draft there was only one mark: under the line "The seven daily praises shelter greatly the living and the dead," a dot made with a bamboo stylus dipped in ink, a very heavy dot, with nothing written beside it. Jingjing did not go to ask him, and did not change that line either.

---

The price of the stone was learned in the fifth month: an old stonemason who quarried in the Northern Mountains came into the city with his son, came to the monastery to look at the site, measured the ground of the front courtyard, asked how high the stele was to stand, squatted on the ground reckoning with a twig for half an hour, and named a sum. For the calligraphy a famous hand had to be engaged, and the fee, at the rate Official Pei had found out, was another sum. Samaan copied these sums into his account book: the stone; the ox-carts, rollers and labourers to bring it from the Northern Mountains into the city; the ferry money across the Wei; the stonemason and his son's wages and food for forty days; the base; the dragons carved on the stele's head; the calligrapher's fee; added up entry by entry, it came to more than half again what had been reckoned in the first month when Yisi said he would pay.

That evening a few of them sat again in the small room. When Yisi came he carried a cloth money bag, the stipend he had saved these two years; coming in he set it on the table and went to sit by the door himself. Samaan, the account book spread on his knees, said the Great Virtue Yi's stipend of these two years he had recorded, not a coin short; but it was only enough for the stone, the carting, and the stonemason; for the calligraphy and the base it was not enough. The monastery's common fund had money, true—but that was a winter's rice for the gruel kitchen, that was the smoked plum and coptis in the medicine chest, that was Narsai's money for reed mats and wooden stakes; last winter Narsai gathered twenty-seven who froze to death south of the city, twenty-seven reed mats, and this winter there wouldn't be fewer; the second article of the plan of conduct in the Patriarchate's order was to establish trust through charity; the Patriarchate tells us to establish trust with gruel, and you would take the gruel money to buy a stone. Yisi said the half that was short he would make up when next year's stipend came down; if the stele was set up a year late, the stone wouldn't run away. Samaan nodded, and said that was the safe way.

Jingjing said: no waiting, and no more of Pa's money either. He said Pa's stipend paid in was Pa's heart; but this stone would carve a hundred and forty years of events, carve the monasteries of the ten circuits, carve the names sent in from Luoyang, Lingzhou and Zhouzhi; it was a matter for the whole Church; if from beginning to end it were all one man's money, when a Tang read the passage of the great benefactor and went on to inquire who paid for this stone, and found out, it would become the stele the Great Virtue Yi's family set up for itself. The half that was short should come from the common fund. Samaan looked at him and said: the common fund's business has always been decided by the Superintendent together with the Warden of the Rule and the elders in council; your standing was given in the Patriarch's letter, but no hands have yet been laid on you; it isn't for you alone to decide. Jingjing said he would not bring out the Patriarch's letter today; he would go through the accounts with the elder.

He said he had run the gruel kitchen ten-odd years; how many came on which day in the west of the city, how much more in the twelfth month than the tenth, how much more again in the hungry gap when spring opened—he knew better than the account book; this winter's gruel would not be cut by one ladle; the stele money would not come from there. The east end of the hall roof had leaked two years; the money for repairing the hall would be moved over first, and let it leak another year; the elders' yearly clothing allowance—move half over; his own share, move it all; when Luo Fifteen of Luoyang sent in the names, his letter said the Xiushan Ward was willing to give a share of money; Old Master Jiao's nephew in Lingzhou said the same; he had written back to say no; now he would write and ask for it; with each prefecture's names carved on the stele, for each prefecture to give a share of money was only right. He said when all that was moved there would still be a little short, and that little would come out of the medicine chest; he would go to the West Market and tell Shopkeeper Wang to let the smoked plum and coptis be on credit for now; Shopkeeper Wang had long ago promised the monastery credit for medicines.

Samaan asked: is this one stone worth so much? Jingjing said it was, and said: does the elder remember that time in the first year of Shangyuan, when the censor memorialized to strip Pa of his office and take back the robe, the monastery to be abolished and its people sent back to their own countries; what saved this monastery that time was the old record of Zhenguan's edict that Director Liu borrowed from the Court of State Ceremonial, the old case of the Metropolitan Prefecture about the rice at the Qujiang, and the Ministry of War's merit report of the battle of the Xiangji Temple; now Director Liu has retired home, the new director of the Bureau of Sacrifices is surnamed Wei and sees no one, and the Zhenguan files in the Bureau's archives were burned long ago; next time someone memorializes, what will we have to speak with? Chang'an has Pa, has Official Pei; Lingzhou doesn't, Luoyang doesn't, and those seven households in the gully at Zhouzhi haven't so much as one who can write; the day the county magistrate wants to pull down their houses and says they worship an evil god, what will they have to speak with? He said Official Pei had promised to hand in a petition to the Bureau of Sacrifices on the monastery's behalf asking permission to set up the stele; once it was granted it would be a stele the government permitted; once the stele was set up, rubbings would be taken, one sent to each prefecture; when a county magistrate wanted to lay hands on them, they would bring out the rubbing, and on it would be Taizong's edict, the grace of one Son of Heaven after another, the place of the Yining Ward of Chang'an. He said the Patriarchate tells us to establish trust through charity; what this stone establishes is the trust of the next hundred years.

Samaan bowed his head and wrote in his account book; what he wrote was: "The shortfall in the cost of the stele to be met from the common fund; the gruel not to be cut. Proposed by the *papash* Adam; no objection by the Superintendent Yisi; recorded by the Warden of the Rule Samaan." When he had written it, he raised his eyes toward the door; Yisi sat there, and did not say he objected. When they broke up, that cloth money bag on the table Yisi did not take away.

---

Official Pei sent back the draft at the end of the fifth month. Official Pei was past sixty that year and had retired last year, and spent every day growing flowers at home; he took the draft back and read it five days, and on the sixth had a servant boy bring it back, the paper marked in vermilion in a dozen-odd places: "this character should be such-and-such," "this allusion is from such-and-such a book," "the tones of this line are not balanced"; Jingjing corrected them all accordingly. Only in one place, the passage about Yisi, the vermilion brush had written a line beside it: This passage is too long; it reads like a biography of one man.

That night Yisi came into the scripture room holding that page. He knew few Tang characters, but the four characters "biography of one man" he knew; Official Pei always wrote an upright hand. He laid the page on the table, sat down, and spoke to his son in the country speech of Balkh; in these years, when father and son had important things to say, they said them in this; of those in the monastery who could understand it, only the two of them were left. He said: last month you brought these sheets to show me; I said too much; you said it couldn't be cut any more, and I said no more; now Official Pei says so too; Official Pei is a reader of steles, and saw it at one glance. He said: that evening you told Samaan you were afraid the Tang would find out who paid and take it for the stele the Great Virtue Yi's family set up for itself; written as this passage is, there's no need to find out; reading this far, it already is.

Jingjing said that the day he wouldn't let Pa pay alone was for the sake of this passage. He said the money came from the common fund; in the common fund was Luoyang's money, Lingzhou's money, the elders' clothing allowances moved over, the little saved at the edges of the gruel kitchen; the stele was set up by the whole monastery; when the whole monastery sets up a stele, and the stele praises one man, that is called praise; if Pa paid himself and the stele praised Pa, that would be Pa praising himself. He said if Pa still thought this passage too long, then let him look at this passage and see which thing in it Pa had not done. "He could give away his stipend and gifts, and stored nothing at home": the fifty bolts of silk the Son of Heaven granted in the first year of Qianyuan—two to Shopkeeper Wang, two to the old man south of the city, three to Wei the Third's mother, two to the Shi Six family, fifteen to repairing the hall, and not one kept in Pa's own room. "He offered the glass of a gracious audience": that glass bowl is still in the hall now; it's what we use at the Lord's Day offering. "Every year he gathered the monks of four monasteries, serving reverently with pure offerings, completing the fifty days": the four monasteries keeping the Fast together was Pa's idea—sending silk to Luoyang, sending that wooden board, inviting them to come to Chang'an next year at the Great Fast to keep it together; the first year Pa was at Heyang and didn't make it, and every year after it was Pa who arranged it. "The hungry came and were fed": that was the six thousand west of the Qujiang Pool. "The cold came and were clothed": that was the hundred and forty-odd who beat on the gate the year the Tibetans entered the city. "The sick were healed and raised up": that was Granny He's old man's leg, the medicines changed south of the city all those years. "The dead were buried and laid to rest": that was Uncle Duan, whose grave Pa and Uncle Xu dug on the south bank at Heyang. He said that without Pa, after the fifteenth year of Tianbao this monastery would have been gone; the merit report of the Xiangji Temple, the rice of the Qujiang, the placard on the gate in the Xiushan Ward, those two sacks of rice before the Tibetan soldiers—had any one of them been missing, he would not be sitting here today writing a stele.

Yisi said the deeds were there; he owned them; what he spoke of was those few lines of praise. He tapped his finger on the paper: tapped "his art as broad as ten out of ten," and said Pugu Huaien died in his hands; it was he who fed him the medicine. Tapped "among the pure and upright tarsa," and said Alopen, Jilie, the old deacon who showed him round the Street of Heaven, Ahad, Yonan—which of them was not purer and more upright than he? Tapped "the white-robed Luminous scholars," and said that one who called for his mother by the Mianman river—he struck him looking at his face. He said carve these lines, and it's a lie.

Jingjing did not argue with him over those few characters. He said lines of praise were the Tang custom in setting up a stele; in the epitaphs of the city of Chang'an every dead man was ten out of ten; when the Tang read a stele they knew which line was the deed and which the praise; if Pa insisted these lines were lies, he would not dispute it; but he would not cut them. He said the scripture records: men light a candle, and put it not under a bushel, but on a candlestick, and it giveth light unto all that are in the house; so let your good works be seen, that men may see them and glorify the Heavenly Worthy. That lamp at the gate—he went every evening to light it, and when it was lit stood a moment beneath it. The things Pa did were that lamp; to write them was to take it out from under the bushel. He said the Tang who read this passage would see a *hu* monk, and remember that the people of this monastery went hungry together with the Tang, took arrows together with the Tang, gave out gruel in the monastery, saw the road on the battlefield; that when An Lushan rebelled, when Shi Siming rebelled, when Pugu Huaien rebelled, those who scattered afterward all came back. This meaning could be written nowhere else on the stele; only by writing of Pa could it be written.

Yisi asked again: the stele writes him and Lord Guo together; Lord Guo is eighty-four this year; while Lord Guo lives, these lines protect the monastery; when Lord Guo is gone? He said that year in the hall, Luo Fengxian said before the Son of Heaven: his office was petitioned for by Lord Guo, his robe requested by Lord Guo; in the Shuofang army they're all one family, it seems; that night he went to the Qinren Ward, and Lord Guo told him: on the battlefield, to see a road clearly for me is to save me; in the court, to say a word for me is sometimes to harm me. He said: you write "he served the Duke as claws and teeth, and the army as eyes and ears," you write "trusted even in the bedchamber"; when Lord Guo is gone, these characters carved on stone are that sentence of Luo Fengxian's carved into writing.

Jingjing said those characters he had written, and he knew it. "Claws and teeth," "eyes and ears" were camp talk Hou Siyuan had told him at the gate of the Guo mansion; everyone in camp said so; he wrote it as it was. "The bedchamber" was a phrase from the patent of appointment the Guo mansion's administrative officer issued; everyone who wrote steles wrote so. But he had put "Suzong had him go with him on campaign" before those lines: Pa's following Lord Guo was by the Emperor Suzong's edict issued at Lingwu, and on the edict was written one character: approved. The stele writes first what the Son of Heaven sent, then how Lord Guo used it. While Lord Guo lives, those who read this know it's a bond of affection; when Lord Guo is gone, what those who read this read first is still the Emperor Suzong's edict. He said this stele was written reign by reign down to the present emperor; the monastery hung on the Sons of Heaven's edicts; the Guo mansion's name on the stele came after the Son of Heaven's edict. He said too that this was why the stele must be set up by next spring: Lord Guo was eighty-four; when this stele was set up, he wanted Lord Guo still to be able to see it; if he could see it, and the Guo mansion had anything to say, while Lord Guo lived it could still be said.

Yisi got up and went out of the scripture room to the monastery gate; the lamp on the stone block to the left of the gate was still burning, the one Jingjing had lit that evening; the ward drum had long finished, the ward gates were shut, there was no one in the street. He stood under the lamp. He remembered that half bowl of cold gruel he ate in the Qinren Ward, and what Lord Guo said: that he had said it wrong once in the hall, and waited a good half hour in the little room by the Guo mansion gate, holding a bowl of cold gruel, before he understood it; his son had never set foot in that hall, never drunk that bowl of gruel, and sitting in the scripture room arranging characters one by one with a brush, arranging which line went before and which after, had thought his way to what was in that bowl of gruel, and thought it sooner than he had. He remembered too his brother's paper folded into a small square, the left-hand writing leaning left, like a man walking into the wind; the paper said: the order's attached separately; do as it says. For ten-odd years he had seen the road for Lord Guo; Lord Guo was behind, he ten *li* ahead of the line; when he had seen it clearly he went back and reported the road was open, and which way to go Lord Guo decided. This month he had sat by the door listening to his son talk with Samaan, talk with Sabrisho, talk with him; what his son was looking at was the monasteries of the ten circuits, was after Lord Guo was gone, was after he himself was gone too. He stood under the lamp till the wick crusted, pinched it off with his fingers, and went back to the side courtyard. Those lines of praise—he still could not get past them in his heart; and not getting past them, he did not raise them again.

The next morning Jingjing went to the scripture room; the stack of draft on the table was squared up neatly; the few sheets about his Pa had been turned back over and slipped into their old place, not one page missing, and the line of vermilion Official Pei had written had not been rubbed out. Yisi was splitting wood in the courtyard; seeing him come out, he leaned the axe against the woodpile and asked: when the stele is set up, how many rubbings will be taken? Jingjing said one for each prefecture. Yisi said Lingzhou should have two: one for the monastery, one to hang on the east wall of his old house, under that bronze cross; the one for Zhouzhi he would take himself; those seven households couldn't read; when it was taken, someone would have to stand there and read it to them.

---

In the sixth month, Jingjing went once to the Directorate of Astronomy to ask Li Su to choose the day. Li Su was thirty-seven that year and had served in the Directorate ten-odd years; when Jingjing came in he was bent over a great table spread with star charts; when he had heard Jingjing out, he drew an almanac from under the table, took a sheet of paper, wrote a whole page of numbers, circled one, and said: next year, the seventh day of the first month; the year is *xinyou*, of old called *Zuo'e*; the first month's pitch-pipe is *Taicou*; reckoned by the seven luminaries, that day is the sun's day—just the monastery's day of the offering once in seven. Jingjing remembered that year in the west wing he had told Li Su: don't say "the sun's day" out loud; the Tang would hear it and think we worship the sun; and Li Su had laughed then and said: your days—you have your own names for them.

He went back to the monastery and added a line at the end of the draft: "Set up in the Great Tang, the second year of Jianzhong, the year being *Zuo'e*, the seventh day of the month *Taicou*, on the great *Yaosenwen* day; at the time the Lord of the Law, the monk Ningshu, had charge of the Luminous congregation of the East." Samaan took the corrected draft and read it from beginning to end, and at this line stopped, read *Yaosenwen* aloud once, not quite right, and asked what language those three characters were. Jingjing said Persian: the day of worship, *yak-shamba*; he had written its sound in three Tang characters; the sun's day of the seven luminaries he had not borrowed, nor any such-and-such day of the Buddhist scriptures; on this stele, the Lord's name, and the day we worship, both used our own sounds. Samaan read the line once more, took his hands out of his sleeves, opened his account book, and wrote a line: "Stele text settled."

That evening Jingjing went to the gate to add the oil, changed the wick, struck the light, lit the lamp, did not stand beneath it, and went back to the scripture room to copy out the fair copy.

Note: The stele's "A virgin bore the Holy One in Daqin; a luminous star announced the blessing"; "He took up the seal of the cross, melting the four lights into one unbounded"; "In the Shengli years the sons of Shakya used their strength and raised their voices in the Eastern Zhou; at the end of Xiantian the lesser scholars laughed aloud and slandered in the Western Hao"; "there were the chief of monks Luohan and the Great Virtue Jilie"; "the Emperor Xuanzong of the Perfect Way commanded the Prince of Ning and four other princes to come in person to the house of blessing and set up an altar"; "Suzong had him go with him on campaign"; "Though trusted even in the bedchamber, he did not set himself apart in the ranks. He served the Duke as claws and teeth, and the army as eyes and ears"; "his skill as high as three generations, his art as broad as ten out of ten"; "Among the pure and upright tarsa, such goodness was never heard of; among the white-robed Luminous scholars, now we see the man"; and "the great *Yaosenwen* day" (a transcription of the Middle Persian word for the day of worship) are all from the stele. That the stele text nowhere mentions the Passion, and that the stele's head is carved with a cross upborne by clouds and a lotus, are the facts of the original stele; Jingjing's stated reasons, the meeting of the stele's cost from the common fund, and what Samaan recorded are the novel's invention. The Church of the East's texts call the Jews "Shihu" and God "the Heavenly Worthy." Paul's words are from 1 Corinthians 1:23; the words about the candle and the bushel from Matthew 5:15–16.
