# Volume Nine　The Translation Hall

## Chapter 56　Mi Jifen

> Its people know no robbers; they live in happiness and ease.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

Mi Jifen was an old soldier. How old he really was, no one in the monastery could say for sure, nor could he himself; asked, he said he was born in the Kaiyuan years; which year of Kaiyuan, he couldn't remember; at any rate older than anyone here. He was of the kingdom of Mi, Maymurgh; Mi lay beside Kang, Samarkand, a small kingdom, so small many in the city of Chang'an had never heard of it; in his father's generation the king of Mi had sent them to Chang'an as hostages, held at the Son of Heaven's side, held all their lives, and never went back. He was born in Chang'an, grew up in Chang'an, and when grown joined the army, and served till his hair was white, served till he was a general in the Shence Army. He spoke Tang with a touch of *hu* in it, which you had to listen closely to hear. His family had been of the monastery for several generations; his elder son was in the army; his younger son Siyuan was a monk in the monastery, and after Narsai died took over that death-cart and the care of the orphans. Every Lord's Day Mi Jifen came; when young he came on horseback, later he could not ride and came by carriage, and later still could not sit steady in a carriage either and had his servants hold him up on either side; and when he came he did not sit at the front, but always in the last row by the door; when others knelt he could not, and laid his stick across his knees and bowed his head. When the preaching ran long he would doze, and halfway through wake, look round, and seeing no one watching him, doze again. When Jingjing preached, he never woke him.

One day in the spring of the sixteenth year of Zhenyuan, when worship was over, he did not leave, but had his servants help him across to Jingjing, stood still, gasped a few breaths, and said: that year Luo Haoxin came to find you—it was he who sent Luo Haoxin. Jingjing thought before he remembered which year: fourteen years ago. Mi Jifen said Luo Haoxin asked him who in the city wrote good Tang; he said Jingjing of the Daqin monastery; he didn't say you understood the Buddha; afterwards when that sheet of scolding came down, he heard of it, and felt bad in his heart, and meant to come and tell you; and meaning to, had meant it fourteen years. He said too that this year that Yuanzhao of the Ximing Temple had by imperial order compiled a catalogue of Buddhist scriptures, compiled it, presented it; a Buddhist officer in his camp borrowed it to read, came upon you, copied out a few lines to show him; he read them, and felt worse still. From his sleeve he took a paper, the officer's copy; Jingjing took it: on it were those few lines Luo Haoxin had copied that year, not a character different, only with a phrase in front: the Persian monk Jingjing of the Daqin monastery. Mi Jifen said: this time it's carved into a book; for a thousand years to come, those who read the Buddhist scriptures will read these lines. Jingjing folded the paper and said: Lord Mi need not feel bad; of those lines, one isn't a bad word. Mi Jifen asked which one wasn't. Jingjing said: Jingjing should spread the teaching of Mishishi. Mi Jifen was taken aback, and laughed; when he laughed there was no sound, only his shoulders shook, shook till his stick tapped and tapped on the ground, and his servants hastily held him up.

That evening Lingbao was copying scripture for Jingjing in the scripture room, and Jingjing showed him the paper. Lingbao was past thirty; his office of deacon was "for the time being"; Samaan's accounts said the laying on of hands had not been done; he had copied scripture ten-odd years, and his Tang characters were more upright than anyone's in the monastery. When he came to "though they called it translation, they had not obtained half a pearl," he asked: Brother, what does half a pearl mean? Jingjing said: half a bead. Lingbao asked: and not obtained half a pearl? Jingjing said: not even half a bead. Lingbao held in his laughter, held it till his face went red; Jingjing laughed first himself, and Lingbao laughed too; the two brothers laughed under the lamp, and when they had laughed, Jingjing pressed the paper under the inkstone, together with that small slip on which was written "Jingjing should spread the teaching of Meshiha."

---

That year many of the Shence Army came seeking baptism. The Son of Heaven relied heavily on the Shence Army in these years; its soldiers ate their fill and dressed well and drew twice the grain of the frontier troops; the young men of Chang'an who had any way in all wanted to be enrolled. Enrolled, they had to go to war: Wu Shaocheng of Huaixi had rebelled again, the court sent troops to fight him, and a part of the Shence Army was detailed to go too. Mi Jifen was an old general of the Shence Army, and a good many of the *hu* and Tang soldiers in camp knew he believed in the Daqin monastery's faith; before the fighting, men came to him and said: Lord Mi, if we're washed once in that monastery of yours, will swords and spears not touch us in battle? Mi Jifen told them there were no such words, and they did not believe it, saying: Lord Mi, you're nearly ninety, you've fought all your life, and there isn't a hole in you; you've been washed. Mi Jifen had no choice, and led them to the monastery, led a dozen-odd, who stood in the courtyard, every one young, sturdy, bright-eyed.

Jingjing came out and asked them each in turn why they wished to be washed; of a dozen-odd men, all said the same: some said to be proof against swords and spears, some said to come back alive and marry, some said a fellow from their village in camp had been washed and fought three years and not died. When Jingjing had heard them, he told them: when the monastery washes people, there's a rule: first hear the scripture, hear it a year, and after a year, if you still wish to be washed, the monastery will wash you. The soldiers looked at one another, and their leader said: Master, we march next month; a year—we'll be long dead in Huaixi. Jingjing said he knew. He said he could not deceive them: washed, the sword still cuts in; the arrow still goes in; his Pa was washed, and took three arrows at the Xiangji Temple; Lord Guo was washed, and his eight sons—whether they were kept safe, he didn't know whether it was the washing kept them; he said if you die in Huaixi unwashed, the Lord still knows you; his Pa had a friend called Old Duan, never washed in his life, who died at Xiangzhou, and his Pa prayed for him, saying: Lord, thou knowest him. He said: before you march, the monastery will give each of you a cake and a bowl of hot broth, and pray for you; when it's prayed, you go; if you come back alive and want to hear the scripture, the monastery's gate is open.

The soldiers ate the cakes, drank the broth, and when the prayer was done, left. A few of them, at the gate, turned and asked about that cross: could they each have one, to hang at the neck? Lingbao looked at Jingjing; Jingjing said: give them; wooden; whittle them each one, and tell them: this is a sign, not a charm; wearing it is to remind you that someone in Chang'an is praying for you. Lingbao whittled a dozen-odd overnight, not handsomely, and handed them out one by one the next day. Samaan entered a line in the accounts: Fourteen soldiers of the Shence Army came seeking baptism; not baptized; given cakes and broth; prayed for; each given one wooden cross.

Three days later an orderly officer came from the Shence Army camp with four soldiers, dismounted at the monastery gate, and as soon as he was in asked which was the Great Virtue Jing. He said he came by the general's order; the general was surnamed Luo, the one leading the troops to Huaixi this time; the general had heard that a dozen-odd soldiers from camp came to the monastery seeking baptism and the monastery would not baptize them; the general was not well pleased, and said: when the Son of Heaven's troops march, there must be prayers for blessing; every monastery is holding rites; the Ximing Temple has held them, the Zhangjing Temple has held them, even the Daoist abbeys south of the city have held them, and only your *hu* temple won't; the general says: before the march, the monastery is to send men to the camp and baptize the whole camp, eight hundred men in a camp, not one fewer; when they're baptized, the general will reward the monastery with a hundred bolts of silk. When he had said it he laid his hand on his sword hilt and waited for Jingjing's answer. Jingjing said the monastery could go to the camp and pray for the whole camp; prayer is to ask blessing for the Son of Heaven's troops, and the monastery was willing; baptism, no: to be baptized a man must believe himself, ask himself, have heard the scripture himself; of eight hundred men few believe; baptize them, and that is to deceive seven hundred and more, and to deceive the general too. The orderly officer's face darkened, and he said: Great Virtue Jing, think again on the general's words; your monastery's affairs—the Commissioner of Merit's side is under the general's superior. When he had said it, he left.

Samaan said: this time there's no dodging it. Jingjing said he knew. That night he went to Luo Haoxin's house; Luo Haoxin heard it and slapped his thigh and said he knew this General Luo, the Palace Commandant's adopted son, whose word was law in camp; he, a mere field commander, could not speak to him. He went then to Mi Jifen's house; Mi Jifen lay in bed, a man of ninety; he heard Jingjing out, lay with his eyes closed, and only when Jingjing thought he was asleep had his son help him up and said: first thing tomorrow, he'd go to the camp. Jingjing said: Lord Mi, at your age, if you go, General Luo may not give you face. Mi Jifen said he had served seventy years, been in camp since the Tianbao years; of the officers in camp now, half he had watched grow up; General Luo as a boy had led horses under his hand; if he went, whether General Luo gave him face was General Luo's affair; whether he went was his.

The next day Mi Jifen had two servants hold him up and rode in a carriage in at the camp gate of the Shence Army. Before General Luo's tent he got down from the carriage and could not stand; when the servants let go, he knelt down; he knelt on the sand before the tent and told General Luo: the Daqin monastery won't baptize; it's the monastery's rule, not disrespect to the General; the monastery's people are willing to come to the camp to pray for the whole camp; he, an old soldier, had come on the monastery's behalf to beg the General's pardon. General Luo came out of the tent and saw him kneeling, and his face could not hold; he hastily had men raise him, and said: Lord Mi, what is this? And said: if the monastery won't baptize, then don't; if it prays, then let it pray; he didn't want any silk either. Mi Jifen had himself helped up, two patches of sand on his knees, and said: thank you, General. As he left, General Luo said behind him: Lord Mi, that Lord you believe in won't even baptize soldiers; in battle, does he look after you? Mi Jifen did not turn his head, and said he'd fought seventy years; whether that Lord looked after him or not, he knew himself.

The day before the march, Jingjing went into the camp with Lingbao and Siyuan; on the parade ground eight hundred soldiers stood in several ranks, and he stood before them and recited a prayer in Tang, that the Lord protect those who go out: "The Lord shall preserve thy going out and thy coming in from this time forth, and even for evermore"; and when it was recited, recited it again in Syriac. The soldiers stood; some bowed their heads, some looked about, some laughed. Those fourteen who had come to the monastery stood together, wooden crosses at their necks, standing straightest of all. When Jingjing had finished, General Luo sent a bolt of silk as a gift of thanks; Jingjing took it, and back at the monastery had Lingbao cut it up into burial clothes for the several children newly added to those rows of stakes outside the Kaiyuan Gate. Among those fourteen there was a Sogdian soldier named An, whom the camp called Sesame Cake; before he left he stood a moment at the monastery gate and told Siyuan he had a child at home, five years old; if he didn't come back, would Siyuan look after it a little?

That autumn the war in Huaixi was lost; many of the Shence Army died, and fewer than half came back. Of the few who came back, two came to the monastery: one missing an ear, one lame in a leg; they stood in the courtyard, the wooden crosses still at their necks, the red cords worn white. They said they wished to hear the scripture. Jingjing said good; from today, on the Lord's Day, sit beside Lord Mi.

The others did not come back. On the Lord's Day Jingjing had Lingbao read out their names once: the ones Samaan had entered in the accounts that day; where there was a name, the name; where not, what the camp called them: Zhao the Elder, Little Wang Five, Sesame Cake.

---

In the winter of the sixteenth year of Zhenyuan, Mi Jifen came again, this time not on the day of worship but on an ordinary day, at the hour of *wei*, the sky overcast, threatening snow. He came by carriage; the carriage stopped at the monastery gate; his servants helped him down; down from the carriage, he did not walk in, but turned back to lift a child out of the carriage; he could not lift it, and a servant took it and set it on the ground. The child was five or six, in an old padded jacket that did not fit, the cuffs worn shiny, his hair a little curly, black with a touch of yellow, his face a Tang face, but the eye sockets a little sunk, like a *hu*'s. In his hand he gripped half a sesame cake and was gnawing it as he stood; when Jingjing came out he had just gnawed the last mouthful, and licked his fingers too.

Mi Jifen gasped several breaths before he could speak. The child's father was a soldier of the Shence Army, a Sogdian, surnamed An, baptized at the monastery a few years ago; Siyuan knew him. This spring he went to Huaixi, one of those fourteen, whom the camp called Sesame Cake; he died in Huaixi, and his body was not carried back. The child's mother was a Tang, daughter of a small household outside the city of Chang'an, who died of illness the year before last. The child had been knocking about the camp with a few old soldiers, eating from a hundred families' bowls, for most of a year; the camp was to march again, the old soldiers could not see to him, and came to Mi Jifen and said: Lord Mi, you believe in that faith; what's to be done with this child? Mi Jifen said he was old, and his family could not raise him either; Siyuan said the monastery would take him.

Jingjing squatted down to the child's height. The child was not afraid of him; he looked at his face, then at the stretch of leather cord showing at his neck, and reached out to touch it; Jingjing tucked the cord back into his collar, and the child's hand grasped empty air, and he was not cross, but drew it back and wiped it on his jacket. Jingjing asked his name. Mi Jifen said he had none; his father called him A-Fu, and the camp called him Sesame Cake too, the same as his father: he could eat; give him however much, and he'd eat it, and when he'd eaten hold out his hand again. Jingjing looked at the child, and the child looked at him, his eyes very bright, not shying away at all. Jingjing remembered something his Ma had told him before she died: that in the fifth year of Tianbao she carried him newborn to the Yining Ward to find a *hu* priest, and that priest asked nothing at all, only one thing: can this child live? Those words came right to his lips, and he asked Mi Jifen: can this child live? Mi Jifen said: he can; he can eat. Jingjing said: then I'll take him. When he had said it, he was taken aback first himself, and Siyuan behind him was taken aback too: Siyuan had had charge of the orphans seven years, and for seven years Jingjing had put his name to things, and never once said he would take any one as his own. Mi Jifen was not taken aback; he nodded as if he had known all along Jingjing would say so, and had his servant bring down from the carriage a small bundle: in it the child's two garments, a pair of shoes, and a little horse whittled of wood, very roughly, one leg long and one short; Mi Jifen said his father had whittled it. The child saw the little horse and snatched it and clutched it to him. Jingjing held out his hand; the child looked at the hand, shifted the little horse under his other arm, freed one hand, and let him lead him. Jingjing's hand was cold; he had sat half a day in the scripture room, the brazier long gone out, a smell of ink in his sleeve; the child, led toward the gate, went two steps, tilted up his face, sniffed hard, smelled his sleeve, and said: black. Jingjing looked down: on his cuff, sure enough, a streak of ink was rubbed.

That evening Jingjing named the child. He thought of using his Pa's name, and felt it not fitting; thought of using a Syriac name, and thought this child was born of a Tang, grew up on Tang soil, and must live among the Tang hereafter. Then he remembered a line on the stele, written by himself: the Law flowed through the ten circuits, and the state was rich in peace; monasteries filled a hundred cities, and every house abounded in Luminous blessing—*jing fu*. He said: call him Jingfu. Lingbao beside him said: Brother's taking the words off his own stele. Jingjing said: he wrote them; if he doesn't use them, who will? The child sat by the brazier holding that little wooden horse, nearly asleep; hearing someone say *fu*, he opened his eyes and looked, and shut them again.

That night the child slept in his room, on a reed mat newly spread beside the bed. In the middle of the night the child got up and went out in the dark; Jingjing thought he was going to the privy and paid no heed, waited, and heard the well rope in the courtyard, the windlass creaking round; he sat up at once, threw on his robe, went out, and saw the child standing by the well holding a wooden dipper in both hands, gulping water, winter well water, so cold it pricked the teeth. Jingjing called "Jingfu"; the child turned his head, drops of water on his lips, and said: thirsty. Jingjing meant to say cold water mustn't be drunk; the words came to his lips, and he did not say them; he went over and took the dipper and hung it back by the well, led the child back into the room, tucked him under the quilt, and himself sat by the bed waiting. He knew what came next: first the belly rumbling, then griping wave after wave, then trip after trip to the privy, running till dawn, running till the face was white; from a child he had been like that; he had left the fire in on the stove, and set a piece of ginger by the stove. The child fell asleep very quickly, with a little snore, breath after breath, even as counting. Jingjing waited a watch, and there was no stir from the child; waited another watch; the child turned over, smacked his lips, and slept on. Near dawn Jingjing's own belly rumbled; he had drunk a bowl of broth last night, and the broth had probably gone a little cool; holding his belly he went once to the privy, and when he came back his face was white; the child was still asleep, snoring louder than before. He sat back by the bed and looked at that face flushed with sleep, and thought: this child's stomach is not like his, and his stomach was not like his Pa's either. He took up that piece of ginger by the stove, cut two slices for himself, and chewed them.

That morning the character he wrote on his board came from the book of Isaiah: "Can a woman forget her sucking child, that she should not have compassion on the son of her womb? yea, they may forget, yet will I not forget thee. Behold, I have graven thee upon the palms of my hands." He wrote the character *zhang*, palm, and when it was written held out his own palm and looked at it: on the palm there was ink, and an old scar where a stone had cut it that year, mending the monastery at Xizhou.

---

Jingfu settled in at the monastery and ate a great deal; the first few days the woman who cooked ladled him one bowl, and when he'd eaten it he stood by the stove with the empty bowl, saying nothing, only standing; the woman ladled him another, and he ate it, and when the third was ladled he waved his hand and said he was full. Nothing he ate upset his stomach: cold rice, cold cake, raw melon, well water, whatever the children of the ward pressed on him, he took and put in his mouth, and once in, swallowed, and once swallowed, it was nothing. He was not afraid of strangers; when Mi Jifen came to worship he ran up and stood before the old man looking up at him, and Mi Jifen held out a finger, and he gripped it, and gripped it all through the worship. After spring opened, Jingjing taught him his characters, Tang ones first—water, fire, gate, road—written on a newly whittled board, and had him trace them with his finger in the sand tray; the old board from Xizhou Jingjing still used every morning, and did not bring it out. Jingfu traced *water*, crooked and wavering, and when he had traced it looked up and asked: can you drink it? Jingjing said: you can't drink a character; Jingfu was a little disappointed, and bent his head and traced on.

But he sang well. When the monastery chanted at morning and evening office it chanted in Syriac; Jingfu did not understand one word, but sang along, sang louder than anyone; the tune he remembered after one hearing; the words he couldn't remember, so he made them up; the sounds he made up were near the original words and yet not quite; the old believers standing beside him wanted to laugh and dared not, and could only bury their heads lower. His Pa had once laid down a rule: speak Tang at the table; that was because Jingjing grew up in Chang'an and had to live among the Tang thereafter. Jingfu needed no lessons in Tang: he was born with a Tang mouth; the country speech outside Chang'an, the rough talk of the old soldiers in camp, the curses of the ward's children—he knew them all. Jingjing thought about it, turned the rule round, and told Jingfu: at the table, speak the monastery's speech. Jingfu asked: he doesn't know one sentence; how is he to speak it? Jingjing said: then first learn one sentence. The first he taught was the grace before meals; Jingfu learned it in three days, and from then on, before every meal, he said that one sentence in his own made-up sounds, and when he had said it touched his right hand to his forehead, his breast, and both shoulders, and then snatched up his chopsticks, touching very fast, as if afraid the rice would get cold.

That winter, one evening, Jingjing opened the cupboard. In the cupboard were three bowls: one his own, a little nick knocked in its rim; one his Pa's, which had sat here upside down since his Pa died and no one had used; and one older than both, a chip in its rim, a pinch of earth in it: Du De's. He took out his Pa's bowl; it was very coarse, fired over Lingzhou way, its glaze greyish, a ring at the foot left unglazed showing the colour of yellow earth. He took it to the stove, washed it in hot water, washed it again, dried it, ladled into it a bowl of mutton broth with radish in it and meat, set it before Jingfu, and said: eat. Jingfu ate it, and when he'd eaten it pushed the bowl forward and held it out again.

---

In the first year of Yongzhen the Son of Heaven in Chang'an changed twice. In the first month Dezong died, and the crown prince took the throne; not many months later he passed it on to the crown prince's crown prince, and the people of the city said this year the Sons of Heaven changed faster than the era names. That autumn Mi Jifen died. His family reckoned it up and said he was ninety-two. On the Lord's Day before he died he still had his servants hold him up to come, sat in the last row by the door, dozed half the service, Jingfu sitting beside him gripping one of his fingers, and the two soldiers back from Huaixi sitting on his other side. The funeral was led by Siyuan; Jingjing stood to one side and watched Siyuan recite the prayers; Siyuan recited very steadily, not one word wrong, and at the end his voice did not shake; when it was recited he patted flat the earth on his father's coffin, knelt and knocked his head on the ground three times, and when he stood his knees had two patches of wet mud on them. Afterwards the Mi family had an epitaph carved for Mi Jifen, saying he was of the kingdom of Mi, what offices he had held, that he lived ninety-two years, that he had two sons; when it came to the younger son it wrote one phrase: the monk Siyuan, dwelling at the Daqin monastery. Jingjing did not go to see that epitaph; he heard that in the whole epitaph the three characters *Daqin monastery* appeared only this once. He thought: once is once.

---

Note: Mi Jifen, of the kingdom of Mi, descended from hostages, served in office in the Shence Army, and died in the first year of Yongzhen aged ninety-two; his epitaph says "his second son, the monk Siyuan, dwells at the Daqin monastery"; the epitaph was unearthed at Xi'an in 1955. Yuanzhao's *Zhenyuan xinding shijiao mulu* was completed and presented in the sixteenth year of Zhenyuan. In the fifteenth and sixteenth years of Zhenyuan Wu Shaocheng of Huaixi was campaigned against, and the government troops were repeatedly defeated. Jingfu is an invented character.
