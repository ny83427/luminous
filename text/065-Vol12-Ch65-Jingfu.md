# Volume Twelve　Baghdad

## Chapter 65　Jingfu

> He set out the cross to fix the four quarters; He stirred the primal wind and brought forth the two breaths.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

The year Jingfu was fifty-one, he rode a horse for the first time.

In Chang'an, he had gone everywhere on foot. To the West Market on foot, to the Buzheng Ward on foot, out beyond the Jinguang Gate to take in orphans on foot. The monastery had once had a horse, his grand-teacher's, a chestnut gelding, a gift from Lord Director Guo. After that horse died the monastery kept no other. The stable stood empty, and later firewood was stacked in it.

The Uighurs gave him a horse. An old mare, good-tempered, slow-going, her teeth already worn flat.

He climbed on and sat firm, the mare took three steps, and he slid off the other side.

The children by the tents laughed till they rolled on the ground. The child who had had the flux was among them, laughing loudest, and when he had finished laughing he coughed a long while.

Jingfu got up off the ground, slapped the dust from himself, and climbed on again.

This time the mare went a dozen steps before he slid off.

The child's mother came over and helped him up, said nothing, reached out and pressed his two legs in against the mare's belly, then took the reins from his hand, looped them once, and pushed them back into his hand.

Jingfu climbed on a third time.

This time he did not fall off.

---

They went north, over the Tianshan.

The Tianshan were higher than the Long Mountains, and longer; the snow on their tops did not melt even in summer. On their flanks were pine woods, and under the pines was grass, and in the grass flowers bloomed, yellow, purple, white, patch after patch, and when the horses trod through, the petals stuck to their hooves.

Over the mountains, it was another country.

The steppe north of the mountains ran on past the reach of the eye. The grass grew knee-deep, and when the wind came the grass lay down layer by layer, from this side to that, as if a very large hand were stroking it lightly. The sky was enormous, the clouds very low, so low it seemed you could touch them from horseback.

Jingfu sat on his horse and looked a long time.

He had lived fifty years in Chang'an, and every sky he had seen had been framed by ward walls. Square, a piece here and a piece there. He had not known the sky was so large.

The child rode a pony alongside him. The child was well again; there was colour in his face, and he talked more now, and all the way he pointed at this and that and talked to Jingfu in Uighur.

Jingfu could not understand. So he nodded.

The child said something, and he nodded once. The child, pleased, said something else, and he nodded again. When the child was tired of talking, he turned his head, looked at Jingfu, said something very long, and when he had said it, waited.

Jingfu nodded again.

The child burst out giggling, giggled till he nearly fell off his pony.

Only afterwards did Jingfu learn that what the child had asked was: you can't understand a single word, can you?

---

In autumn, they came to a river valley.

In the valley there was water, there was grass, there was a wood, and the wood was birch, its bark white, peeling off in curls. The old man said: we winter here.

The tents went up. Thirty-odd of them, in a ring, an open space left in the middle, where the horses were tethered and the fires lit. Jingfu lived in the tent of the child's family. The tent was of felt, round, a skylight open at its top, through which the smoke went out. Lying in it at night, you could see the stars through the skylight.

He learned to speak Uighur.

He learned very slowly. He remembered his master saying that the seven teachers of the Law in Xizhou had studied Tang speech for a year, and Elder Ishaq still said *mo pa*, "don't be afraid," so that it came out "grind and crawl." He thought he was probably the same.

He learned the most useful words first. Water, fire, horse, milk, cold, hungry, hurts. Then the names of people, how each one in the band was called. When he had learned one, he kept it in his mind, and at night, lying down, looking at the stars through the skylight, he said them over one by one.

The child was his teacher.

The child would teach him a word, he would say it once, the child would shake his head; he said it again, and the child shook his head again; at the fifth saying the child nodded. When he had nodded, the child would reach out a hand and pat him on the head.

It was the way the child's mother praised the child.

Every time Jingfu was patted, he thought of reaching out to stroke Xiaoman's head. His hand was large; it covered the whole back of Xiaoman's head. The child's hand was small; it covered only a patch on the top of his.

---

Winter came very fast.

In a single night the valley went all white. Snow fell three days and three nights, till only the upper halves of the tents showed, and people had to dig a path out through the snow to get out of their doors. The horses stood in the snow with snow piled on their backs, unmoving, breathing out white from their nostrils.

The old man went on the night the snow stopped.

He had been ill since the spring at Yizhou, coughing, holding out all the way. Crossing the Tianshan he was still on his horse, leading at the front. In the valley, when the tents were up, he lay down.

That night, people from his tent came to fetch Jingfu.

When Jingfu came, the old man could no longer speak. He lay on a sheepskin, eyes open, looking at the skylight. The skylight was black, with a few stars in it. His hand lay on his chest; a finger moved once, and moved again.

Jingfu knelt down beside him.

The old man's eyes came down from the skylight and looked at him.

Jingfu knew what he wanted.

He laid his hand on the old man's brow, and said the words. First he said them in Syriac, the few lines he had heard all his life. When he had said them, he said them once more in Tang. When he had said them, he thought, and with the few dozen Uighur words he had learned, put together one sentence.

He put it together wrong. The child, listening beside him, frowned.

The old man heard. The corner of his mouth moved upward.

Then he raised his hand and drew on his own chest. First the upright, then the crossbar. Still he drew it askew.

When he had drawn it, his hand fell.

The people in the tent began to weep. They wept very loudly, men and women all weeping, and some cut their own faces with knives, cut them in streak after streak of blood. Jingfu did not stop them. He knelt there and waited for them to finish weeping.

When they had finished, the child came and stood beside him.

The child asked him where Grandad had gone.

Jingfu thought a long time. In Uighur he had not yet learned that word.

So he raised his hand and pointed to the skylight.

---

After the start of spring, the snow melted.

The water in the valley rose and ran rushing; it was snowmelt, so clear you could see the stones of the riverbed, so cold that a hand put in it ached.

The child's mother came to find Jingfu.

She stood at the door of the tent a long while without speaking. Then she said: when Grandad died, you said the words for him. We want that too.

Jingfu looked at her.

"When we die," she said, "we want someone to say them too."

Jingfu thought and said: saying the words is one thing; there's another thing as well.

He told her. He told it very slowly, and where his Uighur gave out, he used his hands. He used his hands a long time, and she understood.

She went back. Three days later she came again, and behind her the child, and seven or eight others. Men, women, old people, children.

Jingfu had them wait outside the tent.

He went back into the tent, opened his bundle, and took out the bowl.

The bowl with the notch in its lip. In it a pinch of earth, wrapped in cloth, wrapped very tight. The earth had long since dried right through, caked into a lump, grey in colour.

When his master gave him this bowl, he had told him its story once. It had belonged to an old comrade-in-arms of his grand-teacher's, a man called Du De, of Duling in the capital district, who joined the army at sixteen, changed his grand-teacher's dressings for twenty days in Hebei, and afterwards died holding the rear in a retreat, his hand and his sword hilt glued together with blood so that they could not be prised apart, and he was buried sword and all. The bowl was his, and the earth in it was from his grave. His grand-teacher had carried the bowl from Hebei to Lingzhou, from Lingzhou to Chang'an. At meals he set it on the table, beside his own bowl.

When his grand-teacher died, his master went on setting it there. When his master died, Jingfu went on setting it there.

Jingfu carried the bowl in both hands out of the tent.

He went to the river's edge, squatted, and untied the cloth. He tipped the dried earth into his palm and crumbled it.

"Du De," he said.

He said it in Tang. The few Uighurs could not understand; they stood behind him watching.

He scattered the earth from his hand on the grass by the river. The earth was grey, the grass new green; the earth fell, and could not be seen.

Then he rinsed the bowl in the river, and dipped up a bowlful of water.

---

That day, by the river, nine were baptized.

Jingfu took them one at a time. He held the bowl in both hands and poured the water over their heads. It was snowmelt, very cold; as it poured, some shivered, some sucked in their breath, and one child cried, and his mother put her hand over his mouth, just like the woman long ago crossing the Yellow River.

Jingfu thought of his grand-teacher.

His master had told him that his grand-teacher, in Lord Director Guo's camp, had baptized someone. He had used an army bronze basin. His master said he too had only heard tell of it; his grand-teacher never spoke of it all his life, and Lord Director Guo never spoke of it all his life either.

Jingfu had no bronze basin. He had a bowl with a notch in its lip.

It was the child's turn.

The child stood before him, face tipped up, looking at him. Jingfu raised the bowl, and the child shut his eyes.

The water poured down. The child shivered once, and did not cry. He opened his eyes, wiped the water off his face, looked at Jingfu, and smiled.

Jingfu gave him a name. It was Syriac. The child could not say it right; he said it several times, and it came out some other way. The child's mother could not say it right either, and it came out yet another way. Afterwards everyone in the band called him so, and what they called him was by then a long way from what Jingfu had given.

Jingfu let them call him as they liked.

---

That summer, Jingfu began to carve crosses.

In Chang'an a wooden cross had been nailed on the east wall of the monastery. When the county came to count things, the scribe had written in the register: wooden cross, one. That cross belonged to the county now.

He cut a length of birch in the wood, split it, and whittled two small sticks, one long and one short, whittled them square and true. Then he crossed the two sticks, cut a notch in each where they crossed, and fitted them together, fitted them so close there was not a hair's gap, needing no nail and no cord.

The four ends of the cross he whittled round. Like four leaves.

That was his master's kind. His master had worn one round his neck, left by his master's Pa's father, its four ends round too. When Jingfu was small and lay against his master's chest, he would often touch those four round ends with his finger.

On the back, he carved a small mark.

It was the shape of a flame. Carved very shallow, very small; to look at, it was like a leaf, and like a flame.

He carved one and gave it to the child. The child hung it round his neck on a leather thong and ran out to show people.

Afterwards people came to ask for them. So he carved more.

In one summer he carved twenty-odd. By the end there were calluses worn on his hands and the knife was blunt; he whetted the knife on a stone by the river, and when it was whetted, carved on. Some people hung them round their necks, some hung them on the posts of their tents, and one old woman took hers and sewed it inside her grandson's sheepskin coat, against his chest.

---

The next summer, a rider came into the valley.

A Sogdian merchant, alone, driving three pack-horses, going east. He rested a night beside the tents, drank mare's milk, ate meat, and talked half the night with the men.

Jingfu sat to one side listening. He understood half of the Sogdian.

The merchant said there had been great events in the south. The Tang people of Shazhou had risen in arms; their leader was named Zhang; in the night they had opened the city gate and driven out the Tibetan *jie'er*. Guazhou had been taken too. They said there would be more fighting to the east: Suzhou, Ganzhou.

The men asked whether they would take them.

The merchant said: who knows.

Jingfu said nothing. He thought of the temple east of Shazhou city, of the abbot whose eyebrows grew down to the corners of his eyes saying in a lowered voice: I never said this. He thought of that windowless room behind the temple, rack after rack, heaped all the way to the roof.

He did not know what had become of that room. In war, rooms burn.

Early next morning the merchant left. Jingfu stood at the door of the tent and watched his three pack-horses go far off, go to three black dots, and the dots were gone.

That evening at prayers, he said one line more.

"Lord, have mercy on Shazhou."

---

That autumn, they moved again.

While the old man was alive he had said that to the east there were still their people. After the royal camp in the north was scattered, some had not gone, had stayed on the steppe, scattered along river after river, in band after band, small as sheep droppings strewn on the ground. The Kyrgyz had taken the royal camp and could not keep hold of all of it.

So they went east.

They went more than a month, crossed several rivers, and came to a larger steppe. There the grass was taller, the rivers broader, and along the rivers were woods, patch after patch. Far off, other people's tents could be seen, sending up smoke.

They pitched camp by a river.

On the evening the tents were up, Jingfu found an earthenware dish. It was the one the child's family kept butter in, with a corner chipped off. He asked the child's mother for a lump of mutton fat and put it in the dish, then pulled a thread from the hem of his own robe, rolled it between his fingers into a wick, and stuck it in the fat.

He set the dish on top of a wooden post at the door of the tent.

The child had followed at his heels and was watching.

Jingfu struck the flint; he struck three times before it caught. The flame rose, blue first, then yellow, leaping. The fat melted and gave off a rank smell of mutton.

The child looked at the little flame, looked a while, and asked something in Uighur.

This time Jingfu understood.

What the child asked was: nobody's coming at night anyway; who are you lighting it for?

Jingfu nudged the wick, nudged the flame a little higher.

It was already dark. On the steppe the wind had risen, from the west; it bent the flame aslant, and it stood up straight again. Far off, the others' tents went dark one by one. Only this one point of fire, on top of a wooden post, was lit.

"For whoever's walking at night," said Jingfu.

He said it in Uighur. He did not say it quite right; the child understood.
