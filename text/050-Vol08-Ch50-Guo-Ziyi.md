# Volume Eight　The Daqin Monastery

## Chapter 50　Guo Ziyi

> The throne returned to the royal house, the baleful vapours were banished for ever; he stilled the boiling and settled the dust, and made our China anew.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

At the beginning of the third month of the second year of Jianzhong, two months after the stele was set up, a carriage came from the Guo mansion to fetch Yisi.

The one who came was the same old servant who had come leading a donkey in the first month, driving this time a small carriage with blue curtains, a lantern of the Guo mansion hung on the shaft. He bowed at the monastery gate and said the Lord had not been well since the first month: first a cough, then his legs swelling, swelling to the knees; at night he could not lie flat but slept sitting; the mansion had changed doctors four times, and the palace had sent someone from the Palace Medical Service to look at him too; he had taken a bellyful of medicine, and the cough had not stopped nor the swelling gone down. A few days ago in the mansion someone spoke of that time at Mingsha in the first year of Yongtai, saying the Great Virtue Yi had gone to treat Pugu Huaien; the Lord heard it, and after a night, in the morning, sent him to ask. Yisi went into the house for his medicine bag; Jingjing followed him in and went over the things in the bag one by one for him: smoked plum, coptis, dried ginger, descurainia seed, a small packet of cinnamon heart made up by Shopkeeper Wang of the West Market; and he took out that small wax-sealed jar of theriac too; Yisi shook his head and told him to put it back. Jingjing asked whether he should go with him; Yisi said the Guo mansion had asked for a physician, and a physician going was enough.

The carriage went east from the Yining Ward, across the Avenue of the Vermilion Bird, into the Qinren Ward. Of the ground of the whole Qinren Ward the Guo mansion took a quarter; the mansion wall ran on and on along the street, ran until Yisi thought it must have come to its end, and turned a corner and ran on. Through the middle went a long lane, on either side of it courtyard after courtyard: eight sons, seven sons-in-law, dozens of grandsons, each living in his own courtyard; the mansion's people said there were three thousand of the household; the figure three thousand was the mansion's accountant's; no one in the mansion had ever counted them one by one. The mansion gate stood open. Not only the great gate; the second gate and the third stood open too; a few servants stood at the gates and did not stop anyone: men bringing firewood, sellers of flowers, petty officials come to seek audience, old soldiers come to ask for gifts, in and out; a young man selling sesame cakes went in at the great gate with his carrying pole and out at a side gate, hawking all the way, and no one minded him. The old servant said it was the Lord's rule: the mansion gate was never shut; when someone came from the palace there was no need to announce him; he walked straight to the rear hall; when the mansion's staff and officers had business they walked right into the inner rooms to report; once the Lady and their daughter were at their washing and dressing, and the Lord stood beside them holding the towel and the basin for them like a servant, and the man come to report stood in the room and made his report, and the Lord never put down the towel in his hands.

Yisi waited in the small room by the mansion gate. In the small room sat Hou Siyuan, his hair all white, his legs no longer nimble; the Guo mansion had given him the post of minding the gate, and every day he sat on the bench and talked with those coming in and out; seeing Yisi, he stood up, said "Master Yi," gave up his bench, and sent someone for water. He said the Lord had taken his medicine that morning and was asleep; when he woke he'd see people; Master Yi should sit a while. And the sitting lasted till the sun was sinking west. The window of the small room faced the long lane, and all day the lane was never empty: a troop of maids went from east to west carrying silver basins, silver ewers, silver spittoons, the rims of the basins chased with twining flowers, dazzling in the sun; two sheep just slaughtered were carried past from the kitchen; how many sheep the mansion slaughtered a day, Hou Siyuan said, he couldn't say himself; at any rate every day there were some; in a courtyard behind someone was playing polo, and you could hear the hooves and the polo sticks; Hou Siyuan said that was the young masters, the grandsons; the mansion had its own polo ground; a singing-girl went from one end of the lane to the other holding a *pipa*, two *bili* players behind her, said to be for the Seventh Young Master's guests that evening; when the sun had slipped further over, cymbals and chimes sounded from the front courtyard: monks the mansion had invited to pray for the Lord's blessing; in the rear courtyard there was another band, Daoists the Son of Heaven had granted, holding a rite of offering there. Hou Siyuan said: sutras in the front courtyard, offerings in the rear, all for the Lord; when the Lord was awake he had both sides recite. Yisi sat on the bench and watched a maid carry a silver tray past the window, heaped high with candied fruit, and watching her go by, thought of that account book Samaan had spread on his knees last summer.

Toward evening someone came to call him. Yisi followed the man through three gates, across the long lane, across a courtyard planted with peonies, the peonies not yet open, a courtyard full of buds. The rear hall was very large, the doors and windows pasted with new paper, charcoal burning inside, warm as the fourth month. Guo Ziyi sat on a great couch, several back-cushions behind him, a brocade quilt over his legs, seven or eight concubines around him, some holding medicine bowls, some holding fans, some kneeling by the couch pounding his legs. He was eighty-five that year, gone thin, the flesh of his face sagging; but those eyes were still the same: looking at a man as if looking at a road, going on only when he had seen it clearly. Seeing Yisi come in, he raised his hand and had a stool brought for the Great Virtue Yi and set right by the couch, and had tea served. He said: the Great Virtue has had trouble; sit. His voice had gone hoarse; he spoke slowly, and in the middle of a sentence had to stop for breath.

Yisi felt his pulse. His hand lay on Guo Ziyi's wrist; the wrist was very cold; the pulse floated just under the skin, large and empty, gone when pressed, like pressing on a layer of water. He looked at the tongue: the tongue was swollen, tooth-marks at its edges; he lifted the brocade quilt and looked at the legs: both swollen to a shine, a pit left wherever pressed, and when the hand let go the pit remained. He understood in his heart: this was age; the heart's strength was not enough; the water ran downward, ran into the legs, ran into the lungs, and so the cough. He said it plainly: the Lord's illness—this humble monk can make the swelling of the legs go down somewhat, so that at night he can lie down and sleep, and the cough can be eased; but the root of the illness is in the years, and the years this humble monk cannot cure, nor can the mansion's physicians nor the palace's. Beside him a concubine's face changed colour; but Guo Ziyi nodded and said: those four physicians before never said this; the Great Virtue Yi has said it, and now he's at ease. Yisi wrote the prescription—descurainia to drain the water, cinnamon heart to warm the yang—and told the kitchen to boil a bowl of red bean soup every day, without salt; and the Lord was not to be given pickles. At this Guo Ziyi gave a laugh and said he'd eaten pickles all his life, and at the end he's not to have them.

When the prescription was written Yisi raised his head and saw on the small stand by the couch a silver basin holding clear water, a pair of mandarin ducks chased on its rim. He remembered that year in the side hall in the Qinren Ward, on the small stand by Lord Guo's hand there had been that bronze basin, a dent in its rim, holding half a basin of clear water; he had seen it that day, and his heart had eased. He did not ask where that bronze basin had gone.

Guo Ziyi had the concubines put down the medicine bowls and stand back a little, and had the brazier moved further off: too hot. He said the stele text he had had read to him: the copy Official Pei made; he had the secretary read it from beginning to end, read it twice; the lines about him on the stele, the line about "the bedchamber," he'd had the old servant go and say his piece, and when it was said he laughed himself: that's stele-writers' talk; stele-writers all write so. He said when the secretary came to "Suzong had him go with him on campaign," he told the secretary to stop and read it again; he said: the one who wrote the stele—that's the Great Virtue Yi's son, isn't it, the little boy who counted horses under the cart? Yisi said yes. Guo Ziyi said that line was well written, and well placed too: first Suzong, then him; when he was gone this line would still stand; the Great Virtue Yi's son sees the road better than the Great Virtue Yi.

He rested, gasped a few breaths, and said again: these few years the Great Virtue Yi hadn't come to the mansion; he knew; and he hadn't called him either; last year about the stele the Great Virtue Yi came once, and he did not see him, but only had the secretary write a card, without even his seal. He said that year in the hall Luo Fengxian said that in the Shuofang army they were all one family, it seemed; he remembered those words to this day; now his eight sons and seven sons-in-law were all officials at court, the elder ones up to military governor and minister, the younger ones at least gentlemen of the bureaus; and his grandsons were growing up one by one; next year three were due for appointment by inheritance. He said: "The year the present emperor took the throne he took away all my troops and gave me the title Venerable Father. Venerable Father, Venerable Father—it sounds fine to others; I myself know: it's to make me sit at home; the higher the seat, the less I can move." He said a man standing on a high place, with hundreds of souls hanging on him below; he coughs once, and below they tremble three times; he says one word at court, and has to think three days first; a *hu* monk goes in and out of his mansion gate, and the next day the palace knows, and knowing, they'll wonder whether Guo Ziyi is one family with the *hu*, wonder whether those sons and grandsons of his are one family with the *hu*. He said he was eighty-five; people wondering about him—they wouldn't be wondering many more years; he was afraid of their wondering about his sons and grandsons, and afraid too of their wondering about the Great Virtue Yi's monastery; so he kept the mansion gate open, let people look, let them see he had nothing hidden in his house; the Great Virtue Yi, he would not call, so they wouldn't see. He looked at Yisi and said: "All these years, I've kept you in my heart. I had no choice. Great Virtue Yi, don't hold it against me."

Yisi said this humble monk did not hold it against him. He spoke the truth. He stood up and said that if the Lord wished, this humble monk would recite a passage of scripture here for the Lord, asking the Lord to look upon the Lord. Guo Ziyi looked at him, took a sip of tea, and only then pointed outside with his finger and said: in the front courtyard the monks are reciting, in the rear courtyard the Daoists are reciting, sent from the palace, the Son of Heaven's kindness, which can't be refused; if the Great Virtue Yi recites here as well, three houses reciting at once, front, back and sides, and an old man caught in the middle—he's afraid he won't hear any one of them clearly. When he had said it he laughed, and the concubines laughed with him. He said again: "That year at Fengxiang, that basin of water—I asked the Great Virtue Yi a question, and the Great Virtue Yi answered me: the scripture has no such words. A religion that won't even promise people that—honest, at least." The concubines could not understand, and took it the Lord was speaking of old army matters. He stopped there and went no further, reached out and took the medicine bowl from a concubine's hand, drank a mouthful; the medicine was bitter, and he frowned. Yisi stood by the couch and said no more about reciting scripture. Just then someone came in to report that Minister Lu had come to ask after the Lord's illness; his carriage was already at the mansion gate.

Guo Ziyi put down the medicine bowl; the smile left his face; he said: send them all out. The concubines stood stunned a moment, and those holding basins, those holding fans, those pounding his legs, withdrew one by one behind the screen, and went out through the small door behind the screen. It was the mansion's rule that when the Lord received guests the concubines never withdrew; officials of the whole court had come, people had come from the palace, and it had always been so; and this today—the maids in the room did not know what it meant. Guo Ziyi had the fruit on the table taken away too, had the brazier moved further off again, leaned back a little on his cushions, and smoothed his beard with his hand. Then he looked at Yisi and said: Great Virtue Yi, withdraw too; go out the back way; the old servant will see you out. He said it quite ordinarily, as one might say it's turned cool, put on another layer. Yisi bowed, and following the last two maids went out through that small door behind the screen. At the door he heard someone announce in front, footsteps came into the courtyard, and he heard Guo Ziyi's voice, a little louder than before, saying: Minister Lu, you've troubled yourself; this old man is ill and cannot rise; I beg the Minister's pardon.

The old servant led him out by the rear garden. Bamboo grew in the rear garden, and behind the bamboo was a small gate, and beyond the gate a narrow lane, leading to the ward's crossroads. As they walked the old servant said softly that all his life the Lord had never had the concubines withdraw for anyone; today he had them withdraw; in thirty years at the mansion, this was the first time he'd seen it.

When he came out at the ward's crossroads it was nearly dark, and the ward gates would soon close. He looked back once: the great gate of the Guo mansion still stood open, the lanterns at the gate lit, the people coming and going not yet ceased. Two days later Hou Siyuan came to the monastery on his crutch and said that after Minister Lu left that day, the young masters asked the Lord why he had all the concubines withdraw, and the Lord said: this Lu Qi—his face is green as a ghost's, and his heart is crooked too; women seeing him would be sure to laugh; he'd remember it; and the day he got his way, not one of the Guo family would be left. When Hou Siyuan had said it he looked at Yisi, and Yisi nodded: those women, the Lord feared their laughing; this *hu* monk, the Lord feared people seeing.

He went back to the monastery; Shiyiniang was waiting in the side courtyard with rice kept warm on the stove, and asked how the Lord's illness was. He said: age; it can't be cured; he could help him sleep a little better; and said too that the Lord had said many things to him: that not calling him was because he had no choice, and he wasn't to hold it against him; and he didn't. Shiyiniang pushed his bowl toward him and said: the rice is cold; eat a few more mouthfuls.

---

In the fifth month Guo Ziyi's illness grew worse. The prescription Yisi wrote, the mansion had followed more than a month; the swelling in the legs went down somewhat and at night he could lie down; but in the fifth month it swelled back again; the cough was worse than before; he could not eat; a bowl of red bean soup he drank two mouthfuls of and pushed away. The mansion changed doctors again; the palace sent someone again; the Son of Heaven sent palace commissioners to ask after him, one every three days, bringing medicine, bringing words, standing a moment before the couch, saying a few things, and going back to report. The monks in the front courtyard were increased to twenty-odd; the Daoists in the rear courtyard were increased too; from morning to night the sound of cymbals, of chimes, of bells, of chanting drifted into the rear hall wave after wave, and did not stop even at midnight.

One evening Guo Ziyi woke and heard people talking in the outer room: one of his sons and one of his grandsons, the two of them keeping their voices down, thinking he was asleep. The son said the palace commissioner had come again today and asked whether the Lord wished to have a Great Virtue invited into the mansion, a palace Great Virtue, one at the Son of Heaven's side; he said the family had already invited some; the commissioner nodded, looked at the monks in the front courtyard, and as he was leaving asked one more thing: whether in the Lord's early days there had been a *hu* monk under his command—the one written on the stele. The grandson asked how Father had answered. The son said he answered there had long been no dealings; in the third month he came once to see about the illness, a physician invited by the mansion, and left when he had seen him. The grandson said: that *hu* monk—Grandfather hasn't called him again these days either. The son said: best not to call him; the mansion is full of palace people coming and going; your uncles' offices, your own appointment by inheritance next year, all hang on this; the stele writes him and Grandfather in one place, and all Chang'an has been to see it; if a *hu* monk comes into the mansion now, it's handing people a handle. The talk in the outer room stopped, and the footsteps went out.

Guo Ziyi lay with open eyes looking at the canopy of the bed, then turned his head and looked at that silver basin by the couch. He called, and the one who came in was that grandson. He said: in the storeroom there's a chest, old things he brought back from Fengxiang—a saddle, a water-skin, an old pair of knee-guards; in the chest there's a bronze basin; go and find it. The grandson was gone half an hour before he came back; the bronze basin was found, pressed at the bottom of the chest, wrapped in an old cloth, dust on the cloth. Guo Ziyi had him undo the cloth and bring the basin over. He took it, held it in both hands: the mouth wide, the bottom shallow, a dent in the rim; the outside rubbed by hands for decades, rubbed bright; the inside gone black. Which day this basin had been put in the chest, he remembered. In the intercalary fifth month of the fourteenth year of Dali, the present emperor only a month on the throne, the edict came down removing him as Vice-Generalissimo, removing him as military governor, honouring him as Venerable Father; that day he came back from the palace and sat in this room till dark, and the next morning when the maid brought water he told her to change to the silver basin and take this basin away and put it with those old things brought back from Fengxiang. The maid asked where the Lord wanted it put; he said in that chest in the storeroom. He had not thought then whose basin this was, what it had been used for; he had thought only that in a Venerable Father's room, on the small stand, there should not stand a dented army bronze basin; people from the palace would come in and see it and ask; and when they asked he would have to speak of Fengxiang; and to speak of Fengxiang, he would have to speak of that night. He held it and looked at it, and rubbed his thumb once over that dent, and again. Then he told the grandson to take it and wipe it clean and wrap it in a new cloth. When the grandson had wrapped it he held it in his hands and stood by the couch waiting. Guo Ziyi said: take it to the Yining Ward, to the Great Virtue Yi. The grandson asked what message Grandfather wished to send. Guo Ziyi thought and said: returned to its owner. The grandson waited for him to go on; he thought again and said: tell the Great Virtue Yi that in his illness he sees no visitors; the Great Virtue Yi need not come to see him; the mansion is crowded, in confusion; it would be discourteous to the Great Virtue. When he had said it he closed his eyes.

The grandson reached the Yining Ward early the next morning, seventeen or eighteen, in a brand-new blue robe, on horseback, a servant behind him. He dismounted at the monastery gate, first looked at the characters over the gate, then at the new stele in the front courtyard, and said he wished to see the Great Virtue Yi. Yisi came out; he held out the cloth bundle and gave Grandfather's words sentence by sentence, very carefully, as if reciting a lesson. Yisi took the bundle and opened a corner: it was that bronze basin, the dent in its rim turned just toward him. The grandson looked at him, then at the basin, a little embarrassed, and asked the Great Virtue Yi: has this basin some history? The family all say Grandfather washed his face in it all his life, wouldn't use a silver basin, and only put it away a few years ago; none of them knows why. Yisi said it was an army thing; on the march they drew water in it, washed in it, drank from it; in those years in camp he had used it too. The grandson said "Ah," as if somewhat disappointed, and as if somewhat relieved too; he bowed, mounted, and rode away. Yisi stood at the monastery gate and wrapped the bundle up again, one layer, and another.

He did not find it very surprising. In these years he had seen much of it. In Suzong's years a few gentlemen of the bureaus at court came to the monastery every Lord's Day, stood at the back, listened very respectfully, and when they left made an entry in the book of merit; then Wang Jin became chief minister and the whole court followed him in fasting and chanting sutras, and those gentlemen came no more; one of them, meeting him in the West Market, raised his sleeve from far off to cover half his face and walked past him. And there was a general who came knocking at the monastery gate in the middle of the night before a campaign and wanted to be baptized, saying that once baptized, swords and spears would not touch him on the field; Yisi told him there were no such words; the general said: wash me anyway; he was washed and left, and won his battle, came back and was promoted, and built a Buddhist temple south of the city, whose bell he gave. Many came, and many went; some looked back once as they left, and some did not even turn their heads. Yisi did not hold it against them. But Lord Guo was not like them; he could not say how he was not like them. On the day Lord Guo was baptized, what he asked was whether his sons and grandsons would be kept safe; he told Lord Guo they would not; and Lord Guo said: wash me anyway. Lord Guo had never asked that swords and spears not touch him, nor asked for promotion; the one thing Lord Guo asked for, he had not promised one word of. In which year Lord Guo put that basin at the bottom of the chest, he did not know; on which day Lord Guo found it again at the bottom of the chest and returned it to him, he knew.

That night he lit a small lamp in the hall, the wick turned very low, the flame no bigger than a bean. He took the bronze basin out of its cloth and set it beside the lamp; there was no water in it. He knelt and recited a passage in Syriac, very low, very slowly: the passage his father Milis had taught him, which his father had learned from his own father. Twenty-four years ago, at Fengxiang, in a tent lit by one small lamp, he had recited it once: that night Lord Guo got down from his folding chair, braced both hands on his knees, knelt on the felt, his knees cracking; and he took up this basin and poured three times, the water running down the white hair, into the beard, dripping drop by drop onto the felt. Lord Guo opened his eyes, looked at the little wet patch on the felt, and said it was less than he had thought. Since then he had recited this passage for many people in Chang'an: for those who believed and those who did not, for the dead. This time there was only himself to hear. When he had finished he did not get up, but knelt before the lamp till the oil was nearly dry, and only then stood and blew out the lamp.

---

On the night of the fourteenth of the sixth month, Guo Ziyi died. It was said he went very quietly; the day before he had drunk a few mouthfuls of red bean soup, lay down at night, and did not wake again. The Son of Heaven suspended court for five days and ordered the hundred officials to go and mourn; on the day of the funeral the Son of Heaven went himself to the Anfu Gate and stood on the gate tower watching the hearse leave the city. The Guo family petitioned for him to be buried in attendance at the Jian tomb, and the Son of Heaven granted it, and said too that the mound was to be raised a *zhang* higher. The Jian tomb was at Liquan, northwest of Chang'an; the hearse was to go out by the Kaiyuan Gate. The Kaiyuan Gate was not far from the Yining Ward; early on the day of the funeral Yisi went out of the monastery gate and walked to the edge of the avenue inside the Kaiyuan Gate. Both sides of the street were already full of people, standing several deep: officials, soldiers, merchants, common folk, *hu*, Tang. Yisi stood among a group of old soldiers, all of them of the Shuofang army in those days, their hair all white, some lame, some missing an arm, some on crutches; who knew where they had heard it; they had come before light and stood together, saying little. Hou Siyuan was there too; he had come out with the mansion's procession, and when he got here and saw this group, he came out of the procession and stood among them. Yisi wore that old grey robe; not tall, very broad in the shoulders; standing among them, there was nothing to show how he was different.

A lame old soldier recognized him, leaned over, and asked softly: Master Yi, when the Lord was going, did he call you? Yisi said no; and the old soldier said to himself: in his illness he saw no one; that's so. Hou Siyuan, listening beside them, turned his head away.

The procession took a good hour to pass: banners, pennons, carriages, horses; in front a band of monks, behind a band of Daoists, drums and pipes playing all the way; the Guo sons and grandsons in hemp, one after another, a long line of them. When the hearse came by, the people on both sides of the street knelt in a mass; the old soldiers did not kneel; they stood straight, folded their hands, and bowed together. Yisi folded his hands too and bowed. Bowed, he looked at the ground; on the ground were the tracks of the wheels rolled past, line after line, very deep. He thought of Old Duan, of Hun Shizhi, of Du De, of Li Guangbi, of the men below the walls of Changshan whose names he could not call; Lord Guo had once told him he remembered every one of them. Now Lord Guo was gone too, and of those who remembered, there was one fewer again.

That day back at the monastery he took the bronze basin out of its cloth bundle, drew a basin of well water, washed the basin inside and out once, and again; the dent in its rim he rubbed with his finger several times. When it was washed he dried it with a dry cloth, wrapped it again in cloth, opened the cupboard, and set it at the very back, behind Du De's chipped bowl, and shut the cupboard door. Jingjing stood at the door watching him do these things, and when he had shut the cupboard door, turned and went back to the scripture room.

Note: Guo Ziyi's mansion was in the Qinren Ward and took up a quarter of the ward, with a long lane through the middle; his household numbered three thousand, and those who went in and out did not know where one another lived; his yearly official income was 240,000 strings of cash; his eight sons and seven sons-in-law were all important officials at court; his grandsons were dozens, and when they came to pay their respects he could not tell them all apart, and only nodded; the mansion gate was not barred, his staff went in and out of the inner rooms, and when they saw his wife and beloved daughter at their dressing, Ziyi held the towel and basin like a servant—all according to both Tang histories and Tang miscellanies. In the fourteenth year of Dali, when Dezong took the throne, he removed his command and honoured him as Venerable Father. In the second year of Jianzhong Lu Qi was chief minister; when Ziyi was ill and the officials came to ask after him he never sent his women away; when Lu Qi came he sent them all away, saying Lu Qi was ugly of face and crooked of heart, women seeing him would laugh, and when one day Lu Qi got his way, his clan would be wiped out—see the *Zizhi tongjian* and the *Jiu Tang shu*, "Biography of Lu Qi." Ziyi died on the fourteenth of the sixth month of the second year of Jianzhong, aged eighty-five; court was suspended five days; Dezong watched the hearse from the Anfu Gate; he was buried in attendance at the Jian tomb, the mound raised a *zhang*. Guo Ziyi's baptism, his summoning Yisi in his illness, and the return of the bronze basin are the novel's invention.
