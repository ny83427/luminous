# Volume Twelve　Jade Gate

## Chapter 78　Joseph

> His work being done, at high noon He ascended to the Truth.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

Past Jingzhou it was no longer the Tang's country. The caravan went northwest out of Jingzhou, and by the time they reached the borders of Yuanzhou no Tang beacon towers were to be seen by the road; what they saw were Tibetan earthen forts, squatting one after another on the ridges, flying flags of yak tails. The town of Yuanzhou still stood, half its wall collapsed; inside lived Tibetans, and Tang too—Tang in Tibetan robes, their hair in Tibetan braids, their Tang mixed with Tibetan when they spoke; seeing the caravan, they stood by the road and watched and did not go away. Headman Cao said these were all Tang; after An Lushan's year, Hexi and Longyou were lost; when they were lost the townspeople couldn't run, so they stayed; stayed eighty years; their grandsons' grandsons still knew they were Tang, but couldn't speak all their Tang any more.

The Tibetan barrier was outside the town of Yuanzhou. A dozen-odd soldiers stood at the barrier with a chief, who sat on a felt with a little table before him and a steelyard on the table. The caravan's loads were unloaded one by one and opened; the chief looked at each and named a figure; Headman Cao's men weighed beside him, and when it was weighed paid in cash, in silk, in tea, and when they had paid, tied the loads up again. When the chief saw the bundles of the Daqin Monastery's few, he had them opened; opened, they were scriptures, roll after roll, letters written across, characters written down; the chief picked up a roll, looked at it, and asked what it was. Jingfu said: scriptures. The chief asked: what scriptures? Jingfu said: the scriptures of their own teaching, of Daqin; the chief said: what's Daqin? Headman Cao said a few words in Tibetan beside him; the chief heard, threw the roll back into the bundle, said scriptures were worth nothing, no duty, and told them to tie it up.

He saw the clay jar bound to Salang's chest and had it taken off; Salang took it off; the chief told him to open it; Salang undid the oilcloth; the chief craned to look; it was ash; he frowned and asked what this was; Salang said: ash. The chief said: you carry ash along too? Salang said it was entrusted to him. The chief looked once more, asked nothing further, waved his hand, and told him to tie it up.

---

They crossed the Yellow River in the first month. The caravan crossed at Huizhou; outside the town of Huizhou there was a ferry, worked by boats in summer; in winter the river froze, and when the ice was thick, carts and horses went over the ice. That winter was cold and the ice froze thick; Headman Cao first sent two camel men with long poles to probe the ice step by step, probe to the far bank and probe back; they said the ice was over a foot thick and could be crossed. He had the camels spread out one after another, spread far apart; when one had crossed, the next went; the people went with the camels; the carts came last, their wheels wound with straw rope against slipping.

Lingbao's cart was the last. He Siniang would not ride in the cart; she said the cart was heavy; she'd get down and walk, take off one person's weight; she held the cart's shaft and walked step by step on the ice. Jingfu walked on the other side of the cart; Salang led the donkey that pulled it in front. In the middle of the river there was a sound under the ice—a crack, very muffled, as if someone at the bottom of the river had struck once with a hammer. The donkey stopped, its ears up, and would not go. He Siniang stood on the ice, her face white; Jingfu stopped too. Headman Cao shouted from the far bank: don't stop; keep going; the ice cracking is normal; stopping is what does the harm. Salang hauled on the donkey's halter, hauled a few times; the donkey would not move; he untied the clay jar from his chest and handed it to Jingfu, went himself in front of the donkey, his back to it, threw the halter over his shoulder, bent down, and hauled forward step by step; the donkey followed him, one step, one step, to the far bank.

On the bank, He Siniang sat on the river shingle; her legs were weak; she tried twice to stand and could not. Lingbao in the cart had seen nothing; he had only heard that sound; he asked He Siniang what that sound just now was. He Siniang said: the ice. Lingbao said when he was small he heard Pa say that long ago at the Mianman river, crossing ice, Pa looked at the ice's colour: blue is new ice, white is old ice; new ice is thin, old ice thick. He asked He Siniang what colour today's ice was. He Siniang said she hadn't looked; she'd been too busy walking.

Joseph fell ill after they crossed the Yellow River. On the ice he had slipped and fallen; he wasn't hurt, but he got wet, and ice splinters got down his collar; he said nothing; on the far bank his clothes froze stiff, and he said nothing either. That night he began to shiver, shivered all night, and the next day the fever came.

---

They reached Liangzhou in the second month. Liangzhou was a great city, bigger than Jingzhou, bigger than Yuanzhou, its walls high, Tibetan soldiers at its gates; inside, half Tibetan, half Tang, and Sogdians, and Uighurs; the speech of the streets was a dozen-odd tongues stirred together; Jingfu stood in the street listening and could make out Tibetan, Sogdian, Uighur, Tang; he thought of the West Market in Chang'an; Master used to say: in the streets of the West Market, listen with your eyes shut and you can tell who's from where.

That year Liangzhou was in turmoil. Headman Cao said the Tibetan *tsenpo* had been dead four or five years, and since he died the Tibetans had been fighting among themselves—two great generals, one called Lun Kongre, one called Shang Pipi, fighting back and forth across Hexi and Longyou four or five years; this year they fought near Liangzhou, next year near Shanzhou; caravans on the road were robbed once by this side and once by that. He said they'd have to rest a while in Liangzhou and wait for spring to open, find out about the road, and then go west.

They rested three months in Liangzhou. They lodged in a hostel in the city kept by a Sogdian surnamed An, an old acquaintance of Headman Cao's; when Proprietor An heard they were of the Daqin Monastery in Chang'an, he said Liangzhou too had once had a Daqin monastery, in the Tianbao years; when the Tibetans came, the monastery's people fled and the Tibetans tore down the monastery and took the bricks to build barracks; now in the city those who believed in Daqin numbered only a few households, all Sogdians; at the offering once in seven they gathered in the back courtyard of one household; there was no bread; someone recited scripture, in Syriac, which the reciter himself hardly understood.

When those few households learned that people of the Daqin Monastery had come from Chang'an, at the second offering once in seven they all came to the back courtyard of the hostel. A dozen-odd, old and young; one old man nearly ninety, his eyes clouded, on a stick, came into the back courtyard, saw Jingfu, saw Lingbao lying in the cart, saw Elijah, saw Joseph lying on a felt, burning red-faced with fever, and the old man stopped and asked in Syriac: from Chang'an? Elijah said: from Chang'an. The old man did not kneel; he stood on his stick and said that when he was small his Pa took him to the Daqin monastery at Liangzhou, and in the monastery was an elder from Chang'an who broke bread and gave it to him to eat; that was the last time in his life he received bread; he was seven that year; later the Tibetans came; the monastery was gone, the elder dead; eighty years, and in Liangzhou no one had broken bread since. He said: these eighty years these few households of Liangzhou gathered at the offering once in seven just the same, recited just the same, and when they came to the offering bread they stopped; the rule his Pa set then was that when they stopped, everyone held both hands cupped at the breast, held them so while one sentence was recited, and counted it as having received; he had held them so eighty years, and was used to it.

That day Lingbao broke the bread. He sat in the cart unable to rise; he had the glass bowl set on his knees; Jingfu held the bottom of the bowl; he recited the consecration very slowly, and when he had finished broke it and handed it out piece by piece to the dozen-odd of Liangzhou; when he reached the old man nearly ninety, the old man gave his stick to the one beside him and cupped both hands at his breast, cupped them as he had for eighty years; the bread was laid in his palms; he looked down at it, looked several breaths, before he remembered that this time there was something in his palms; he put the bread in his mouth, chewing, and said to the one beside him: it's made of flour.

Joseph lay on his felt, watching. That day his fever had gone down a little and he could talk. When it broke up he called Jingfu over and said to him in Syriac: you saw. Jingfu said he saw. Joseph said that long ago in Chang'an he and Elder David stopped the Tang hymns for a month, and watched the back rows empty row by row, and then he thought the Chang'an monastery had gone too far; today, seeing these dozen-odd of Liangzhou, eighty years without anyone breaking bread, reciting a Syriac they themselves didn't understand, he thought: the people haven't gone far; eighty years, and the hands still cupped; what's gone far is anyone who could put bread in those hands. When he had said this, he looked at Jingfu once, and said no more.

---

The caravan left Liangzhou in the fifth month going west; on the road they met Lun Kongre's troops once, several hundred horse, charging down from a pass of the Qilian mountains to the south; Headman Cao had all the camels kneel and the people crouch behind the camels, no running, no sound; the several hundred horse passed beside the caravan; a few soldiers reined in, looked, reached out and pulled a bolt of silk off one camel's load, threw it over a horse's back, and went. Headman Cao said that counted as good.

In the sixth month the caravan reached Ganzhou, and it happened a caravan coming from the east had just arrived at Ganzhou, bringing news of Chang'an. The caravan's people said the Son of Heaven in Chang'an had died in the third month, died of elixirs—had taken the golden elixir refined by Zhao Guizhen's band, taken it till his temper grew violent, been ill a good while, later could not even speak clearly, and in the third month was gone. The new Son of Heaven was the late emperor's uncle; he took the throne, and the first thing he did was seize Zhao Guizhen's band of Daoists and have them beaten to death at the gate of the Jingzhao prefecture—a dozen-odd killed. They said too that the new Son of Heaven sent down an edict: the two streets of Chang'an might each add monasteries; monks and nuns might be ordained again; some of the monasteries torn down in the Huichang years were allowed to be rebuilt.

That night Jingfu sat in the back courtyard of the hostel at Ganzhou till the second watch; Elijah came over and sat down beside him. Elijah asked him: Leader, what are you thinking about? Jingfu said he was thinking about Chang'an. He said: the new Son of Heaven allows the Buddhist monasteries to be rebuilt, allows monks and nuns to be ordained again—and the Daqin Monastery? The decree said *the Daqin, the Magi and the Zoroastrians*; does the new Son of Heaven's edict say anything of Daqin? Elijah said the caravan's people didn't say. Jingfu said he couldn't work it out either. He said: even if it's allowed, the monastery's people have been sent back; the Office of Receptions' roll says *sent back to their own places*; sent out, they're sent out. Elijah asked him: among the dozen-odd Daoists killed, was Lu Shouchong? Jingfu said the caravan's people didn't know the Daoists' names. He said he didn't want to know either.

Joseph's illness did not mend. Past Ganzhou the fever came again—a few days burning, a few days easing; when it eased he could walk a few steps; when it burned he lay in the cart, crowded into one cart with Lingbao. Two old men, one blind, one sick, lying together, talking in Syriac—of Merv, of Chang'an, of Elder David, of Jingjing, of the Tang hymns sung again that year. Lingbao said when he was small he heard Brother sing "In the highest of the heavens, deep reverence and praise," and when it came to "the great earth turns again to universal peace" he sang along and sang off key, and Brother didn't scold him. Joseph said the first time he heard that one was in the twelfth month of the fourth year of Yuanhe, following Elder David in at the monastery gate; the hall was singing it; he couldn't understand it, but he could tell: they were singing the Lord.

---

They reached Shazhou in the seventh month. Shazhou was the last great city of Hexi; going west was out by the Yang Pass; going north, one must first turn back to Guazhou and go out by the Jade Gate, and further north was the sand desert. The town of Shazhou too was in Tibetan hands; there were more Tang in the town than in Liangzhou, and more monasteries; southeast of the town was a hill with sand at its foot, and on the cliff were cut row upon row of caves, with Buddhas painted in the caves; the townspeople said those were the Mogao caves; men had begun cutting caves there in the time of the Former Qin, cut them for hundreds of years, cut hundreds of them.

Headman Cao said his caravan went no further than Shazhou; further west, across the Mohoyan desert, to Yizhou, to Xizhou—that stretch of desert his men wouldn't travel; they must wait for a caravan coming from the west and go with the westerners. He said this year the west was in turmoil; since the Uighurs scattered, over toward Xizhou it wasn't peaceful either; few caravans came; you'll have to wait.

They settled in Shazhou, lodging under the colonnade of a small Buddhist monastery east of the town; the old monk of the monastery, seeing a crowd of *hu*—some sick, one blind, children—sighed and let them stay.

They waited a year in Shazhou. Of caravans going west, not one came that year; the townspeople said there was fighting over toward Xizhou, Uighur against Uighur, and the road was cut. They lived under the colonnade of that small Buddhist monastery: Jingfu drew water for the monastery, Salang carried goods for the Sogdian families of the town, Elijah taught the monastery's little novices a few Syriac letters, Ayi and Xiaoman learned Tang characters from the monastery's old monk, He Siniang cooked for the monastery. At the offering once in seven they held it under the colonnade; Lingbao sat on a felt and Jingfu held the bottom of the bowl; the monastery's old monk sometimes stood behind a pillar of the colonnade watching, and when he had watched went back to his hall to chant his scriptures.

Joseph failed in the winter of the second year. He lay on a felt under the colonnade of the Buddhist monastery and burned with fever half a month, burned till he was a handful of bones, his eyes sunk in, the pupils still bright. He knew he was done for, called Jingfu over, and told him two things.

The first was the scriptures. He said: he'd watched you carry those Tang-language scriptures—the Great Worthy Jingjing's old rolls, the new rolls Qushen copied, twenty-odd rolls in all—out of Chang'an, tied on a donkey's back, all the way; crossing the Yellow River they nearly went into an ice hole; in Liangzhou the hostel's rats gnawed the edge of one roll; you don't know where you'll go after this; wherever you go there's no monastery, no scripture cabinet; rain, snow, carried on a donkey's back, lodging under other people's colonnades—scripture rolls on the road rot in three years. He said: leave one set here. He said these days lying under the colonnade he'd heard the monastery's old monk say that at the monastery at the foot of that hill southeast of town there was an old monk people called Monk Wu, the Buddhist Chief Instructor of Shazhou, who had charge of the scriptures of all the monasteries of Shazhou; his scripture store held Tang characters, Tibetan letters, Khotanese, Sogdian—every kind of scripture he took in, and once taken in, entered in a register and put in a cabinet, and not a roll thrown away. He said: leave the set Qushen copied with him; the Great Worthy Jingjing's old rolls you take with you, keep them by you; if they rot, they're yours; the set Qushen copied left here, someone will keep it; some year, someone will open that cabinet and see it, and know that on Tang soil there were once these scriptures.

Jingfu said he'd think about it. Joseph said: don't think too long; if you think too long, he won't live to see it. Jingfu went to tell Lingbao. Lingbao sat under the colonnade, heard him out, and shook his head. He said: hand the scriptures to a monk—he can't be easy about it. He said those scriptures Brother translated character by character; beside the borrowed characters are dots, and the dots mean borrowing; a monk looking at them won't know they're borrowed and will take them as the same; he'll put them in his scripture store with their Buddhist scriptures; later someone opens it and sees non-action, sees pure stillness, sees *as a mirror reflects a thing*, and takes them for Buddhist things; what Brother fought all his life was precisely to keep people from taking Aluohe for the Buddha; put the scriptures into a Buddhist monastery and you're making Brother's scriptures go as guests to the Buddha. He said he'd rather the rolls went with you and rotted on the road; rotted, it would be our own scriptures that rotted.

Jingfu said: Uncle Lingbao, the set Qushen copied Master never saw; the old rolls are Master's hand; the old rolls he'll take with him; if they rot they're his; the set Qushen copied has a dot beside every borrowed character, not one missed; later someone opens it, sees the dots, and knows these are borrowed—that whoever borrowed the characters belonged to another house—which Master said himself. He said what he thought was: the dots are for those who come after; where those who come after are, he doesn't know; on the road there are none; in a Buddhist monastery's scripture store, there may be.

Lingbao sat there, fingers tapping his knee. He said: what you're saying are Brother's words; he can't refute them. He said: go; but tell that monk plainly: these are scriptures of Daqin, not Buddhist scriptures; when he enters them in the register, he must enter them as Daqin.

Jingfu went back to Joseph and said he'd go. Joseph nodded and said the second thing was himself. He said he was a man of Merv, came out in the second year of Yuanhe, a little past twenty, took ship with Elder David, drifted nine months at sea; he thought then that when Chang'an's affairs were settled he'd go back, back to Merv, and die in Merv. He lived thirty-six years in Chang'an, and Chang'an's affairs were never settled a single day; he never went back; now he was going back, and had got to Shazhou, half the road still to go to Merv, and he couldn't make it. He said: those of you going west—Elijah, Salang, the Great Worthy Lingbao—if you reach Merv, outside the city there's a church, and behind it a burial ground; his Pa and Ma are buried there; stand a while before their graves for him and tell them: Joseph went half the way; the other half, he's asked Elijah to go for him.

When he had said it, he closed his eyes. Elijah sat beside him holding his hand and recited a psalm in Syriac—the ninetieth: *Lord, thou hast been our dwelling place in all generations*; when he came to *we spend our years as a tale that is told*, Joseph's lips moved and he recited half the line with him; the second half he did not get out.

---

He died on the third of the twelfth month, the first year of Dazhong. He was buried on the sand east of the town of Shazhou. Shazhou's ground froze in winter too, but sandy ground froze shallow; Salang dug from daybreak till the hour of *wu* and dug out a pit; at its bottom was yellow sand, dry; Salang said dry was good; dry doesn't rot. Elijah washed his body and put on him a clean black robe brought from Chang'an, which all the way he had not been willing to wear, saying he'd keep it to wear when he met the Patriarch. Lingbao had himself helped to the graveside; he could not see; he felt Joseph's face, felt it once over, and said: Joseph, you argued with Brother all those years—argued over Tang, argued over hymns, argued over characters in the scriptures; today you've gone; Brother's waiting for you over there; the two of you can argue again.

The stake was written by Elijah, in Syriac: *Joseph, of Merv, came east in the fourth year of Yuanhe, died at Shazhou in the twelfth month of the first year of Dazhong.* Jingfu wrote another line beside it in Tang characters. The next day Jingfu rode a donkey to that monastery at the foot of the hill southeast of town, reached it only at the hour of *wu*, and spent that night at the monastery. He carried a bundle on his back; in the bundle were the eleven rolls Qushen copied—the *Sutra of Profound Peace and Joy*, the *Scripture on Proclaiming the Origin*, the *Hymn of the Three Majesties*, the *Book of Honoured Ones*, and several more—all in Qushen's hand, upright regular script, a little dot beside every borrowed character, not one missed. He saw Monk Wu; Monk Wu was past sixty, thin, long-faced, slow of speech; he heard his purpose explained, opened the bundle, looked through it roll by roll, looked till the sun slanted west; when he came to that line in the *Sutra of Profound Peace and Joy*, "as a mirror reflects a thing: the thing gone, the image vanishes," he stopped and asked Jingfu: is this your scripture? Jingfu said it was. Monk Wu said: this line sounds like ours. Jingfu said: it's borrowed; the ones with dots beside them are borrowed. Monk Wu bent to look at the little dot and smiled, and said: borrowed is borrowed; borrowed and still remembering it's borrowed—that's good.

He had a little novice enter the eleven rolls in the register; the register was written in Chinese characters; Monk Wu said what he entered was: *Year of the Hare, winter: eleven rolls of scripture of the Daqin Monastery of Chang'an, deposited by Jingfu, monk of the Daqin Monastery.* He said Shazhou was governed by the Tibetans now, and the years were reckoned by the Tibetans' twelve animals; this year was the year of the Hare; whatever year it was hereafter, the register would say that animal. He said: *deposited* means you come back to fetch them later; if you don't come back, they stay here, in a cabinet of the scripture store; if he dies, his disciples will keep them.

Jingfu clasped his hands and thanked him, and went out of the monastery gate. Outside the gate was that hill, with row upon row of caves on its cliff, their mouths black; the winter sun shone on the cliff, and the cliff was yellow. He stood at the monastery gate and looked back once, looked to see which of that row of caves held scriptures, and could not tell; they were all alike. He turned and walked toward the town, walked to Joseph's grave; a layer of the sand on the grave had been blown flat by the wind; the stake still stood; he squatted down and set the stake straight.

Note: In the second year of Huichang the Tibetan *tsenpo* Langdarma was assassinated, and thereafter Lun Kongre and Shang Pipi fought each other across Hexi and Longyou for several years; see the Tibetan chapters of both *Tang Histories*. In the third month of the sixth year of Huichang Wuzong died from taking elixirs; Xuanzong took the throne and had the Daoist Zhao Guizhen and others beaten to death, and in the fifth month again allowed the two streets to add monasteries and monks and nuns to be ordained again; see the *Old Book of Tang*, annals of Xuanzong, and the *Comprehensive Mirror*, chapter 248. The cutting of the Mogao caves at Shazhou began in the second year of Jianyuan of the Former Qin; see the Zhou-period *Stele of Li Jun's Repair of the Buddha Niche of Compassion*. Monk Wu is a character of the novel. Crossing the Yellow River on the ice at the Huizhou ferry in winter was ordinary in the Tang. Psalm 90 is in common use in the burial rites of the Church of the East.
