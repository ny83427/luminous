# Volume Ten　Morning Praises

## Chapter 64　Lady An

> How broad is the Way, how fine its answering! Forced to give it a name, we set forth the Three in One.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

The letter from Luoyang came at the beginning of the first month of the third year of Dahe, brought by a young man of the Mi family who traded silk between the two capitals; on the cover was written *To the Great Worthy Lingbao, Daqin Monastery, Yining Ward, Chang'an*; opened, it was two sheets, one in Chinese characters and one in Syriac; the Chinese sheet was written by a monk named Qingsu, the Syriac by Xuanying, abbot of the Luoyang monastery, and they spoke of one matter. Qingsu's mother, the Grand Lady An, had been buried east of Luoyang in the Yuanhe years, and before her grave stood a pillar, carved with scripture, carved with a cross; now that ground had been flooded by water, and the grave must be moved, to a new burial ground bought south of the city, on a day fixed for the sixteenth of the second month; the pillar must be moved with it and set up again; at the Luoyang monastery only three old monks were left—Xuanying was eighty and his legs could not walk; the Great Worthy of the Nine Grades, Zhitong, was ill; the Great Worthy of Deportment, Xuanqing, could not conduct it alone; could Chang'an send someone to preside over the rite of reburial?

Lingbao read the letter and handed both sheets to Jingfu. That year morning devotion numbered thirty-one; since Baoli some had gone and some had died, two more old brothers had passed away, and two of the ten who had left had come back to fill their places; the monastery's register still had twenty names, and the one kept for one who is to come was still empty; the only one who could preside was Lingbao; he was the priest; without him the offering once in seven had no one to bless the bread; and that spring he was coughing again, coughing so he could not sleep at night. He said he could not go. He said: you go, and take Joseph; Joseph can chant the Syriac scriptures; the two of you together for the rite of reburial is enough; as for the offering bread—when you reach Luoyang, Xuanqing is an elder, a priest called to Chang'an to have hands laid on him in the years Brother was bishop; he can bless it; you help him.

Jingfu asked what the pillar looked like. Lingbao said he had never seen it; he knew only that in the ninth year of Yuanhe the Luoyang monastery had set up a pillar for the Grand Lady An, the year the Great Worthy Jingjing was still living; the scripture on the pillar Jingjing had translated in Chang'an long ago; the Luoyang people copied it and carved it on stone. He said: your Master's characters you've looked at in the Chang'an scripture room a dozen-odd years; go to Luoyang and look at them in stone.

---

The two of them set out on the twentieth of the first month, on the monastery's old donkey, riding by turns, east out of the Chunming Gate, through the Tong Pass, past Shanzhou, travelling eleven days, and entered Luoyang on the first of the second month. The Daqin Monastery at Luoyang was in Xiushan Ward, smaller than Chang'an's: one hall, two rows of rooms, an old locust tree in the middle of the courtyard; at the monastery gate stood a man of about fifty, tall and thin, in a blue robe washed white, who on seeing them first wished them peace in Syriac and then again in Tang, and said he was Qingsu, son of the Grand Lady An; the abbot Xuanying was his teacher; his mother's grave he had kept fifteen years.

That night Jingfu saw Xuanying. Xuanying lay in a room on the east side, a man of eighty, his eyes clouded, his hands still warm; he took Jingfu's hand and felt the back of it, and asked whose disciple he was; Jingfu said Jingjing's. Xuanying's hand stopped, and he said: Jingjing—he had seen him; in the winter of the fourth year of Dali Jingjing came to Luoyang with Bishop Yonan to see old Jacob, and lived a winter in Xiushan Ward; he was twenty then and Jingjing twenty-three; the two of them sat at night under the locust tree talking scripture, stamping their feet with cold, talking till cockcrow. He said old Jacob died that winter; Bishop Yonan chanted the scriptures by his bed, and when he had finished went alone to the well to wash his hands, washed them three times; he and Jingjing stood under the colonnade watching, and neither dared go over. Xuanying said he remembered Jingjing saying then that the scriptures must be translated into words the Tang could listen to, and then the Tang would be willing to listen; and when the Tang had listened, they'd know Aluohe was not the god of the *hu* alone. When Xuanying had got so far, he coughed several times; Jingfu brought him water; he drank a mouthful, took Jingfu's hand again, and said: your Master's scripture—it's carved in stone; go and see.

---

Early the next day Qingsu took Jingfu and Joseph out of the city, east seven or eight *li*, to the edge of a hollow. That year the rains had been heavy; the Yi and the Luo had both risen; the water in the hollow had gone down, but the ground was still wet; step on it and your sole came up with mud. In the middle of the hollow was a grave, its mound half collapsed, and before the grave stood a stone pillar.

What Jingfu saw first was the pillar's shape. Eight faces, taller than a man, a cap on top, a ring of lotus petals carved round the cap's edge; under the shaft a base, and lotus on the base too. He had seen such pillars in Chang'an: at the gate of the Jianfu Monastery, at the gate of the Great Xingshan Monastery, at the gate of that little monastery by the West Market—all Buddhist things, called scripture pillars, carved with the Usnisa Vijaya dharani; the Buddhists said that if the pillar's shadow fell on a man, his sins were wiped away. He stood before the pillar, and for a moment could not make out what was on it. Joseph, standing beside him, spoke first. Joseph said: this is a monks' pillar.

Qingsu did not answer, but stepped forward and wiped the mud off the top of the pillar with his sleeve. Under the cap, on the face toward them, was carved a cross, and under the cross a lotus; on either side of the cross was carved a figure, sashes floating up, flying upward, holding something in their hands—the flying celestials the Buddhists paint. When Qingsu had wiped one face he went round to the next and wiped it; of the eight faces, four were carved with crosses and celestials, and on the other four were characters, cut in vertical lines; the first line Jingfu read aloud: *The Scripture of the Luminous Teaching of Daqin on Proclaiming the Origin and Reaching the Root*.

Joseph looked at those four faces of crosses, and his face was very unhappy; he said to Jingfu: this isn't right; how can there be a lotus under the cross, how can there be those flying people beside the cross? Those are Buddhist things; in the monasteries of Merv, in the monasteries of Baghdad, under the cross there are steps, there's stone, there's the vine; there's no lotus, no flying people. He said that if the Patriarchate's people saw it they'd say the monasteries of Chang'an and Luoyang had been eaten up by the monks. He said it in Syriac, said it very fast; Qingsu understood, standing on the other side of the pillar with that muddy sleeve still in his hand, and stopped.

Qingsu said this pillar was set up by him and his elder brother; his brother too had passed away some years ago; in the inscription of names, he was the younger and came after his brother. In the ninth year of Yuanhe his mother died, and the two brothers wanted to set up a mark for her; the burial ground they bought that winter from a man named Cui Xingben; it lay low; they didn't know, and bought it; the stele at Chang'an was stone, too big; he couldn't raise one; he asked his teacher Xuanying, and his teacher said the people of Luoyang all set up pillars; set up a pillar, carve scripture on it; scripture is for the living to read and for the dead too; people pass, see it, read a line, and that's enough. He went to a stonemason east of the city; the stonemason could only carve pillars, could only carve lotuses, could only carve celestials; he said he wanted a cross, and the stonemason couldn't do one; he drew one himself on paper and the stonemason carved from it; the lotus the stonemason added himself, the celestials too, saying that without lotus and celestials a pillar didn't look like a pillar. He said he had thought of having the stonemason grind them off; in the end he didn't; the place where his mother knelt every morning in the monastery in her lifetime was an old Buddhist kneeling-cushion with a lotus embroidered on it; she knelt on that cushion thirty years, knelt till she died.

Joseph would not swallow it. He said he knew; the stonemason could only carve lotuses, the kneeling-cushion had a lotus embroidered on it; but the Patriarchate's people didn't know that; what the Patriarchate's people would see was a monks' pillar. He said that when Elder David was in Chang'an long ago, it was for just this that he stopped the Tang hymns for a month; that month he was in the hall too, standing behind Elder David, watching the back rows empty row by row; Elder David changed his word only after he had seen it, and said: sing them again; but singing them again didn't mean letting everything go its own way; before Elder David died he told him: he couldn't understand the Chang'an monastery; he and Elijah must watch it for him, watch that it didn't go too far; once it went too far it couldn't come back. He said he had watched it thirty years; he didn't know how far was too far; the lotus he could bear, the flying people he could bear, but the cross standing on a lotus—looking at it, it was as if something in his heart were being trodden on. Jingfu said: Brother Joseph, don't be hasty yet; let me finish looking.

---

He looked at all four faces of crosses, then went round to the faces with characters; the first face was the opening passage of the scripture; he read it aloud: *The Luminous-Pervading Dharma King, in the city of Nasaluo in the land of Daqin*. What Nasaluo was, Jingfu knew: in the Syriac scriptures it was Nazareth, the little town where the Lord grew up; when his Master translated it he thought three nights and used these three characters, saying the Tang, hearing them, would know it was a faraway place where there was a town with people in it, and that was enough. The second face told how the Dharma King opened his teaching to all beings, spoke of the beginningless, the boundless, the limitless, of the unsayable, in the words his Master loved best—a *miao*, wondrous; a *zhen*, true; a *ji*, still; Jingfu had looked at these characters a dozen-odd years in the scripture room in Chang'an; when his Master translated he ground the ink, ground the vermilion, copied, read back to his Master; his Master said this line isn't smooth, change it; said that character's too Buddhist, replace it; replaced and replaced again, and in the end what stayed was still those few characters; his Master said that in the language of the Tang there were only these few characters that could let the Tang know there was something they did not know. He read on line by line, in Tang; Joseph understood half; Qingsu, on the other side of the pillar, moved his lips with him; Qingsu could recite it by heart. On the third face was carved the pillar's record, the brothers' words: their mother's name, when the pillar was set up, that the pillar stood here so passers-by might know that the one beneath believed in the Three-in-One.

Jingfu stood before the pillar and put out his hand to touch it; the stone was cold; the carved characters had mud packed in their strokes, and he picked it out stroke by stroke with his fingernail, picked it out, came to the third face, came to that line in the record that said whomever the pillar's shadow fell on, his sins would be forgiven by the God of the Three-in-One, and his hand stopped. He had heard monks at the gate of the Jianfu Monastery say words like these, of the Vijaya pillar; Qingsu had taken them and changed the god; when he reached this place he did not know whether he should read on, and read on.

He lowered his hand and said to Joseph: when his Master translated this scripture long ago, if the Patriarchate's people had seen it they'd have said the Chang'an monastery was eaten up by the monks too; they said it for decades, said it till he died, and he didn't change it. He said: the cross Qingsu drew, the lotus and the flying people the stonemason added, the characters are his Master's; when the grave is moved, the pillar goes with the grave, and the lotus goes, and the flying people go too; not one piece ground off; whether it's gone too far, he doesn't know either; he'll remember this word for Brother Joseph; later, if Brother Joseph sees him go too far, say it again.

Joseph looked at him, looked again at the pillar, did not nod, and argued no more; he bent down and wiped with his sleeve the mud on the pillar's base, and when he came to that ring of lotus on the base he wiped it very carefully, petal by petal.

---

The reburial was on the sixteenth of the second month, before light: Jingfu, Joseph, Qingsu, and Qingsu's elder cousin Shaocheng, his maternal uncle An Shaolian, and a sworn uncle of Qingsu's, a supernumerary commander in the Left Longwu Army of the Upper Capital, who had taken leave and hurried from Chang'an—forty-odd, in an army robe, no sword at his waist—bringing two soldiers to help carry; and the household servant who kept the grave, called Chang'er, past fifty, who had put up a straw hut at the edge of that hollow and kept it fifteen years; he went down into the pit first, alone, and loosened the earth at the four corners of the coffin. Xuanqing hurried from the city, past seventy, riding a donkey; at the grave he got down, steadied himself, and took from his robe a cloth packet; in the packet was a cake of bread and a little bottle of wine, which he had prepared at the monastery the night before.

Before the coffin was raised, Joseph chanted the scripture in Syriac—that passage which says man comes from the earth and returns to the earth, yet Aluohe does not forget those in the earth. He chanted a line, and Jingfu said a line in Tang, for Qingsu, Shaocheng, An Shaolian and the rest to hear; the supernumerary commander stood at the back, both hands hanging; he was not a believer; halfway through, he bowed his head. Xuanqing blessed the bread, broke it, and gave each believer present a small piece; Chang'er climbed up out of the pit, both hands muddy; Xuanqing laid the bread in his palm; he wiped his hands on the front of his coat, took it, and ate.

The coffin came up; one corner of the wood had rotted with the water; Qingsu knelt at the pit's edge and held up that rotted corner with his hands all the way to the cart. The new burial ground was south of the city, newly found for him by others with his own robe-and-bowl money: three *mu*, high ground, against an earth slope with cypress trees planted on the slope. The cart went most of a day and reached the new burial ground; the pit had been dug a few days before; the coffin was lowered, and the earth shovelled in spade by spade—Chang'er a spade, Qingsu a spade, and Jingfu a spade too.

The pillar was set up the next day. Four men carried the shaft and the two soldiers carried the base; the base was set down first, the shaft stood on the base, the cap put on last; that ring of lotus at the cap's edge, as it went on, was missing a petal, knocked off in the carrying; Qingsu squatted on the ground, found the petal, and tucked it into his robe. The pillar stood up: the face with the cross, the lotus and the flying figures faced the grave; the face with characters faced the road they had come by.

Qingsu added a passage to the pillar's new record, its wording thought out for him by Jingfu: that on the sixteenth of the second month of the third year of Dahe the great matter of removal was carried out; where it was moved to; who was present—the abbot Xuanying, the Great Worthy of Deportment Xuanqing, the Great Worthy of the Nine Grades Zhitong; that two men had come from the Daqin Monastery at Chang'an; when he wrote this far, Qingsu asked Jingfu: your two names—shall they be carved? Jingfu thought, and said: not carved. He said this pillar was your mother's; the people carved on it are those who keep watch over her; the two of them, once the reburial was done, would go back to Chang'an; carve them on it, and passers-by reading it wouldn't know who these two were. Qingsu said: then write *two men came from Chang'an*. Jingfu said: don't write two men either.

---

The night before going back to Chang'an, Jingfu went again to Xuanying's room. Xuanying was awake, heard him come in, and asked whether the pillar was up; Jingfu said it was up. Xuanying asked whether the lotus was still there; Jingfu said it was, missing a petal, and Qingsu had kept it. Xuanying smiled, and said: that petal he'd kept too; in the ninth year of Yuanhe, when the pillar was set up, the stonemason spoiled a piece, and Qingsu picked that up too and kept it in his room, and kept it till now. He had Jingfu feel under the bed; Jingfu felt out a wooden box and opened it; inside was a piece of stone the size of a palm, carved with half a cross, carved crooked; the stonemason had carved it crooked, chiselled it off, and carved it again. Xuanying said: take it, show it to Lingbao; tell him the Luoyang monastery is still here, and this pillar is still here too; from now on, if there's anything in Chang'an, there are people in the Luoyang monastery, and there are people in the Chang'an monastery too.

Jingfu wrapped the piece of stone and tucked it into his robe. Early the next day he and Joseph went out of Luoyang and west, and on the earth slope outside the city looked back once; the cypresses at the foot of the slope were too far away to show the pillar; they saw only a layer of white frost on the treetops of the cypresses.

---

Note: The Luoyang pillar of the *Scripture of the Luminous Teaching of Daqin on Proclaiming the Origin and Reaching the Root, with Pillar Record* was excavated at Luoyang in 2006: eight-faced, carved with crosses, lotuses and flying celestials, the scripture and record cut vertically; the record states it was erected in the ninth year of Yuanhe (814) and the grave moved on the sixteenth of the second month of the third year of Dahe (829); the names appearing include the abbot Xuanying (lay surname Mi), the Great Worthy of Deportment Xuanqing (lay surname Mi), the Great Worthy of the Nine Grades Zhitong (lay surname Kang), the Luminous monk Qingsu and his mother the Grand Lady An of the land of An, his elder cousin Shaocheng, his maternal uncle An Shaolian, a sworn uncle who was a supernumerary commander in the Left Longwu Army, and the household servant Chang'er who kept the grave; the old burial ground was bought from Cui Xingben. The pillar's form is Buddhist, with a cross in place of the Buddha image—physical evidence of the localization of the Luminous Teaching in the Tang. Xuanying's meeting with Jingjing and the stone fragment are the novel's invention; scholars differ on the relation of the *Proclaiming the Origin* scripture to Jingjing, and the novel follows one view.
