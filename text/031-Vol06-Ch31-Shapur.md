# Volume Six　The Shuofang Army

## Chapter 31　Shapur

> They keep no slaves, and hold high and low as equal among men; they amass no goods, and show that they have given all away.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

Mornings at the Patriarchate began with the striking of the board.

Before light, someone in the middle of the courtyard took a wooden mallet and struck a long board hung up there. *Tok*, *tok*, *tok*—slow at first, then fast, then slow again. When it was done, the people in every room got up and went to the church to say the morning office.

Jingjing lived at the Patriarchate the better part of a year, and not once did he get up earlier than his uncle.

Every time he went out, he saw his uncle already standing at the door of his room. His uncle stood there, head bent, tying a knot in his empty right sleeve with his left hand and his teeth. First he rolled the sleeve up, up to where the elbow would be, bit one end in his teeth, brought his left hand round—a pull, a tug, another pull. It took no more than a few breaths. When it was done he pressed the knot with his left hand, raised his head, and went to the church.

The knot was the same every day, just as tight, just as neat, as if measured with a rule.

Once Jingjing went over and said: "Uncle, let me tie it for you."

His uncle gave him a look.

"I'll tie it myself," he said.

---

In that better part of a year, every few days his uncle would call Jingjing to that little room and question him.

He asked about Chang'an. Asked about the people of the monastery, the seven elders, the monk-soldiers, the giving of gruel, the tanner's son, the old fire-keeper. He asked in great detail, going over one thing again and again, asking until Jingjing himself was sometimes muddled, but his uncle never was. If Jingjing gave a number wrong, the next time it came up his uncle would say: "Last time you said thirty-seven."

When it came to the old fire-keeper, his uncle asked: "That basin of fire—what happened to it?"

"It didn't go out," said Jingjing.

His uncle nodded, and said nothing.

When he had finished questioning, he would write with his left hand.

He wrote very slowly with his left hand. The letters leaned to the left, one after another, like men walking into the wind. What he wrote was for Chang'an. What it was, Jingjing did not know; he knew only that it went on for months, written and torn up and written again. Once Jingjing saw him write until midnight, the lamp oil burned out, and he sat on in the dark waiting for day.

"This is for your pa," said his uncle. "You'll take it with you when you go back."

---

That summer, everyone at the Patriarchate was talking of one thing.

The Caliph was going to build a new city upriver. The place chosen was on the west bank of the Tigris, by a village called Baghdad, no more than a day's journey from Ctesiphon. It was said there was to be a round city wall, wall within wall, and in the very middle the Caliph's palace. Craftsmen and labourers had been conscripted from everywhere, tens of thousands of them, and on the river the boats carrying bricks came one after another, day and night without stopping.

Some said too that when the Caliph went to look at the place, he came upon a monastery there, and an old monk in it told him of the virtues of the spot. The Caliph heard him, and decided on it.

Whether this was true, no one knew. When the people of the Patriarchate spoke of it, their faces were a little glad.

Jingjing asked his uncle: is it true?

His uncle said: "When the new city is built, sooner or later the Patriarch will move there. Where people move, affairs follow."

When he said this, he did not look glad at all.

---

Only later did Jingjing slowly learn that these nine years had not been peaceful ones for his uncle.

Nine years before, the struggle for the patriarch's seat at the Patriarchate had been fierce. His uncle had stood with the Patriarch Jacob, and the other side had lost. Those who lost did not disperse. Someone hired an assassin; the assassin got into his uncle's room; his uncle took a blow on his right arm and killed the assassin with his left hand.

The right arm could not be saved.

From then on his uncle never slept two nights running in the same room. Every evening he decided for himself which room he would sleep in that night, and having decided told only the men keeping the gate that night, so that if anything happened in the night they would know where to call him. No one else knew—not even the Patriarch.

The people of the Patriarchate had lived like this with him for nine years. Going everywhere, staying nowhere long. Eating bread and beans, wearing old robes. The monk-soldiers' pay grew less every year.

Now the new city was to be built, the Patriarch was to move, and the faction that had lost all those years ago had come back.

Jingjing asked the oldest elder at the Patriarchate whether his uncle was afraid.

The elder said: "Your uncle is not afraid of dying. What he's afraid of is the work not being finished."

---

Baluma was one of the monk-soldiers who kept the gate.

The day Jingjing arrived, it was he who had stopped them at the gate. About thirty, broad-faced, big-eyed, with a loud voice and a mouthful of white teeth when he laughed. He came from a village in the marshes to the south, had come to the Patriarchate in his teens, and had followed Jingjing's uncle for nine years.

He was very good to Jingjing. When Jingjing first came his stomach was bad for a month, and Baluma stole hot water from the kitchen for him every day, and having stolen it, winked at him and said: "The little deacon from the east has guts made of silk."

He often asked Jingjing about the east. Asked how big Chang'an was, what the Tang emperor wore, how much a month the Tang monk-soldiers were paid.

Jingjing said the monk-soldiers of the monastery in Chang'an had fought alongside Guo Ziyi, and when the fighting was over Guo Ziyi had rewarded them.

When Baluma heard this he said nothing for a long while.

"I've followed the Archdeacon nine years," he said. "The Archdeacon is a good man. I'm not saying he isn't."

He looked down at his feet. His shoes were old, the soles worn through, patched with leather, and the patches worn through again.

"Nine years," he said, "and I've never had a new pair of shoes."

His mother was sick in the village, and he had sent money back several times by others' hands, all of it borrowed. At the bread shop in the city he owed the better part of a year's bread money. Once Jingjing went with him to buy bread, and the baker scolded him in front of Jingjing, and he did not mind, but laughed and said next month for sure, next month for sure.

Out of the bread shop, he said to Jingjing: "Little deacon, when you go back east, take me with you."

Jingjing thought he was joking, and laughed too.

---

It was the beginning of the eighth month.

In the evening, Jingjing was sitting by the courtyard gate writing his journal. He liked to write here; it was light, the wind came off the river, and it was not hot.

His uncle came out from inside, came to the gate, and said something to the men keeping the gate that night. There were two of them: one was Baluma, the other an old monk-soldier whose ears were not good, who had kept the gate all his life and never left it once.

What his uncle said was: "The room beside the storehouse."

Having said it, he went back in.

After a while Baluma said a few words to the old monk-soldier, picked up an empty oil jar, and went out of the gate. As he went he winked at Jingjing and said: "We're out of lamp oil. I'm off to buy some."

Jingjing nodded, bent his head, and went on writing.

It was full dark before Baluma came back.

---

In the middle of the night, Jingjing was woken by a sound.

It was a very dull sound, like something heavy falling to the floor. Then a shout. The shout was in the country speech of Balkh.

At the Patriarchate, only two people spoke the country speech of Balkh.

Jingjing ran out barefoot.

The courtyard was pitch dark; he ran past the church, past the kitchen, to the door of the room beside the storehouse. The door was open. The oil lamp inside had been knocked over, the oil splashed across the floor, and the flames licked along the floor, lighting up the room.

His uncle lay on the floor.

There was a hole in his chest, and blood welling out of it, soaking his grey robe black. Beside him lay a short knife covered in blood. The window on the river side stood open, and on the window frame hung a scrap of torn cloth.

Jingjing knelt down and held him.

Behind him people came one after another with torches and stood at the door; some were shouting, some weeping, some climbed out of the window to give chase. Jingjing heard none of it. He saw only that his uncle's eyes were open, searching for him in the firelight.

His uncle found him. His left hand came up and gripped Jingjing's wrist. Gripped it very tight.

Nine years before, he had taken the blow on his right arm. This time, he had no right arm.

---

His uncle spoke. He spoke in the country speech of Balkh, very softly, very slowly, a breath for every word.

"Don't punish him."

Jingjing bent down and put his ear to his mouth.

"Don't kill him," said his uncle.

He breathed a few times. Blood ran from the corner of his mouth.

"Everyone has been running with me so many years," he said. "Too tired."

Jingjing looked at him. The people at the door were shouting something; someone pushed in, someone knelt down. His uncle's eyes stayed on Jingjing, looked at him a long time, as if he still had something to say.

His left hand let go of Jingjing's wrist and felt aside, felt for the empty right sleeve, found the knot. His fingers came to rest on the knot.

Then he did not move.

---

After daybreak the Patriarchate was in an uproar.

The one in charge of the monk-soldiers called the two men who had kept the gate the night before into the middle of the courtyard, and had all the monk-soldiers stand round and watch. He said: only the men at the gate knew which room the Archdeacon was sleeping in. If not this one, then that one. He would send for the whip.

The old monk-soldier knelt on the ground, unable to hear any of it, only knocking his head on the ground. Baluma stood, his face grey.

Several of the old elders stopped it. They called Jingjing over.

"What did the Archdeacon say at the last?"

Jingjing stood in the middle of the courtyard with everyone looking at him.

He thought a while, and said it. He put his uncle's three sentences into Syriac one by one, and did not change a word.

The courtyard was silent a long time.

"Who was it?" asked the one in charge of the monk-soldiers.

"My uncle didn't say," said Jingjing.

That was the truth. His uncle truly had not said.

The one in charge of the monk-soldiers was going to speak again. The oldest elder waved his hand and said: "You heard the Archdeacon's words too."

The whip was not brought.

---

That afternoon they washed his uncle's body.

Jingjing went too. He held the water and stood by, watching several elders wipe the blood off his uncle bit by bit. His uncle was very thin, so thin his ribs showed one by one. Below his right shoulder there was only a short stump of arm, the scar old, long since grown smooth.

When he was clean they dressed him in a new grey robe.

The right sleeve was empty, hanging over the edge of the bed.

One elder reached out to tie the knot. Jingjing said: "Let me."

He rolled the sleeve up, up to where the elbow would be, and tied a knot with both hands. When he had tied it he looked at it; it was not tight enough, not neat enough. He untied it and tied it again. Still not tight enough.

He tied it three times. All three times it was not as good as his uncle had tied it himself.

At last he stopped. He pressed the knot with his hand, pressed it flat, and stepped back.

---

After his uncle was buried, Jingjing did something.

He went to fill the lamps. The lamps at the Patriarchate had been Baluma's charge to fill, and these few days Baluma had been shut in his room and not allowed out, so it fell to Jingjing. He took the oil jar to the storehouse to draw oil, opened the great oil vat, and found only the dregs at the bottom.

He stood by the oil vat a while.

That afternoon he went into the city to buy bread. As the baker wrapped the bread for him he said: that loud-voiced monk-soldier of yours came early the day before yesterday and cleared his account—the better part of a year's bread money, not a coin short.

"What did he pay with?" asked Jingjing.

The baker picked a silver coin out of his cash box and showed him. It was new money, the lettering at the edge still bright; you could see at a glance it was not long from the mint.

"He had several like that," said the baker. "I asked him, come into a fortune? He said a letter had come from home—his mother was better."

Jingjing paid for the bread and carried it back.

That night he wrote two lines in his journal:

"The lamp oil wasn't bought. The bread money was paid."

When he had written them he closed the journal and sat on his bed all night.

---

The next day before light, before the board had been struck, Jingjing went down to the river.

Baluma had been let out. There was no proof, and the elders would not let him be kept shut up any longer. He sat on a stone by the river looking at the water. The water was as slow as ever, *hua-la*, *hua-la*.

Jingjing went over and stood beside him.

"And the lamp oil?" he asked.

Baluma did not turn his head.

After a long while his shoulders began to shake. He put his two hands over his face, kept them there a long time, then took them away. His eyes were red, his face grey, his white teeth biting his lip.

He told it.

In the spring someone in the city had come to him. It was outside the bread shop; the man was well dressed, spoke very politely, knew he owed for bread, knew his mother was sick, knew he had followed the Archdeacon nine years. The man said there was nothing else to it—only he had a few words he wanted to say to the Archdeacon face to face, and the Archdeacon would see no one. All he needed to know was which room the Archdeacon would be sleeping in on a certain night. He would give him a purse of money—half now, the other half when it was done.

"He said it was just a few words," said Baluma. "That's what he said."

He looked at Jingjing.

"I knew it wasn't," he said. "In my heart I knew. I told myself it was. I told myself all summer, until in the end I believed it myself."

Jingjing stood there and said nothing.

"Little deacon," said Baluma, "the Archdeacon's last words—when you translated them, you didn't change a word?"

"I didn't change a word."

"He knew it was me."

"He didn't say who it was."

"He knew," said Baluma. "He knew everything."

He squatted on the stone and wept like a child.

---

It was nearly dawn. In the eastern sky a little greyish white showed.

Jingjing looked at Baluma, looked a long time. He thought of his uncle's left hand gripping his wrist, gripping it so tight. He thought of his uncle saying: everyone has been running with me so many years; too tired.

"Go," said Jingjing.

Baluma raised his head.

"Go today," said Jingjing. "Don't come back to the Patriarchate. Don't go back to the city. Don't go looking for that man."

Baluma looked at him, his lips moving.

"The other half of the money…"

Jingjing said nothing, only looked at him.

Baluma lowered his head. After a while he nodded and stood up. He made Jingjing a bow, a very deep one, then turned and went south along the dirt road by the river.

He walked very fast, and did not look back.

Behind them, the Patriarchate's board was struck. *Tok*, *tok*, *tok*.

---

When Jingjing got back, he told the old elders.

He said the man had gone. He said his uncle had said: don't punish him, don't kill him.

He did not say who the man was. The elders did not ask. In truth they had all guessed; only no one said the name aloud. The one in charge of the monk-soldiers shouted at Jingjing a long while, shouted at him that a boy come from the east had no right to make decisions for the Patriarchate. Jingjing stood there and did not answer one word.

The oldest elder waited until he had done shouting, and then said only one thing:

"He made no decision for the Patriarchate. He passed on the Archdeacon's words."

---

On the morning of the fourth day, the old monk-soldier opening the gate found a man outside the Patriarchate.

It was Baluma.

He lay on the dirt road outside the gate, his throat cut, the blood already dry. The purse on him was gone, his belt torn off.

On his feet was a pair of new shoes.

The shoes were newly bought, of cowhide, the soles hardly touched by dust yet, the stitching on the uppers close and even.

The old monk-soldier squatted beside him, looked a long time, could not hear well, and kept asking: what's happened, what's happened here.

Jingjing stood at the gate looking at those shoes.

---

When they buried Baluma, the Patriarchate argued again.

Some said he was a man who had sold his master and could not be buried in the church's burial ground. Some said he had been murdered—if they didn't bury him, were they to throw him in the river? The one in charge of the monk-soldiers said: throw him in the river.

The oldest elder said: "The Archdeacon said: don't punish him."

In the end they buried him at the edge of the burial ground, against the foot of the wall, far from the other graves.

What should be set at the head of the grave, they argued again. A cross, some would not allow. A name, some would not have. His crime—no one was willing to take up the brush for that. They argued until in the end no one could say what should be written.

Jingjing went to the storehouse and found a wooden stake, sharpened one end, and carried it on his shoulder to the grave.

He set the stake at the head of the grave and drove it down with a wooden mallet. Nothing was carved on the stake; it was bare, with only the marks of the knife that had shaped it.

He struck it many times. Once, once, once. The burial ground was very quiet, only the sound of the mallet on the stake, *tok*, *tok*, *tok*, carrying all the way down to the river.

At the last stroke he stopped, and laid the mallet on the ground beside the grave.
