# Book Two　Messiah

# Volume Seven　A-Lian

## Chapter 37　Water and Lamp

> Man's first nature, whole and simple, was open and did not swell with itself; his heart, plain and unclouded, had at first no craving.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

At the gate of the Daqin monastery in the Yining Ward of Chang'an there was a lamp. Not a big one: a bronze oil dish set on the stone block to the left of the gate, with a cover of woven bamboo strips over it, the cover pasted with several layers of paper to keep off the wind. It was lit as soon as dusk fell; when the ward drum had finished it was still burning; oil was added once at midnight, and it was put out only at dawn. Everyone in the ward knew this lamp. When the old charcoal-seller packed up late and felt his way home in the dark, he saw that point of yellow and knew he was nearly there; the ward guards on night patrol, when they got here, would stand a moment under the lamp too, stamp the dust off their feet, and go on. When children asked grown-ups why there was a lamp lit at the *hu* temple's gate, the grown-ups said: the *hu* monks' rule. The monastery's own people could not say whose rule it was; they knew only that at the end of the Tianbao years, when Yisi went to Lingzhou, the lamp was lit by the monastery's old deacon; in the year Chang'an fell the old deacon was cut down by rioting soldiers, and the lamp went out, and stayed out more than a year. In the winter of the second year of Zhide, when Yisi came back, he dug this bronze dish, green with rust, out of the side room, wiped it, set it on the steps at the monastery gate under the lintel, and lit it. In the second month of the next year, when Yonan reached Chang'an with the seven elders, on the very first evening he took the bronze dish down from the steps and moved it to the stone block to the left of the gate. Yisi asked him why he moved it; Yonan said there was less wind here. For many years before that, a coarse earthenware oil dish had stood on that stone block, long since broken, nobody knew where.

Who first lit this lamp, only two people in the monastery now knew: one was Yonan, the other Yisi.

---

In the eleventh month of the first year of Yongtai, Yisi came back from Jingyang.

He had been gone nearly two years all told. When he left it was the first month of the second year of Guangde, the snow not yet melted; when he came back, the leaves of the locust trees in the ward had all fallen, and it was winter again. The monastery's people came out to meet him and saw him leading the old bay horse, a bundle wrapped in old felt on its back, his face darker and thinner than when he left, the scar on his left brow white in the cold wind. That night Jingjing brought him a bowl of gruel; it was hot; he finished it and sat under the lamp, sat till the wick had crusted twice, and Jingjing sat across from him and told him the monastery's affairs of these two years one by one.

Jingjing told it in detail. He said that after the Tibetans left, of the hundred and forty-odd people in the monastery a hundred or so had gone home; a dozen-odd had nowhere to go and were still living in the felt tents in the back courtyard; Elder Samaan, following the order, had put them in a company of fifty with a company head, two meals of gruel a day, every entry in the accounts. He said Elder Azarel had taken him to Luoyang last spring; Old Jacob in the Xiushan Ward was still alive, only hard of hearing, you had to shout to talk to him; the Mu daughter-in-law had remarried, to another sesame-cake seller, and the shop next door had opened again. He said Lingzhou couldn't be got to: Lingzhou had fallen into Pugu Huaien's hands last year, and only this ninth month, when Huaien died at Mingsha, was the road open again; Old Master Jiao had sent word by someone that the monastery's lamp was still lit, and he wiped that bronze cross on the east wall every day. He said things were bad in Hexi: Liangzhou had been lost last year, fewer and fewer caravans were going west, and in these two years not one letter had come from the Patriarchate, nor did they know whether the letters the monastery wrote had arrived. Here Jingjing paused, and said Elder Azarel had said that after spring opened there was a great matter the monastery had to discuss; what matter, the elder had not said.

Yisi listened throughout, and when he had listened, asked whether Elder Samaan could read the Tang characters of the gruel accounts. Jingjing said he couldn't; the Tang accounts were kept by someone else, a woman who had come into the monastery with the people of the ward in the days the Tibetans were in the city; her surname was Pei; she could read and write, and her accounts had not one mistake. Yisi listened, turned the bowl in his hand half round, and said: you've run the charity two years, and run it well; you'll go on running it; and the outside business, Official Pei's side—from now on you'll go along on those errands too; he would keep only the monastery's general accounts and the letters to the Patriarchate. Jingjing was taken aback, and asked whether his Pa was tired. Yisi said tired, no; he'd been seeing the road ten years, and his eyes might as well rest a little; and having said it he sent Jingjing to bed, and sat on a while himself.

The next day Elder Samaan heard of it and came to the side courtyard to find Yisi, his leather-bound account book in his arms. Before he spoke he first folded both hands into his sleeves, and when they were folded he said: the order was perfectly clear—all dealings with the government to go by one line only, through Yisi and the official surnamed Pei; no one else might form private connections; Jingjing was Deacon of Charity, not Superintendent; for the Superintendent to hand this line to the Deacon of Charity, it must first be reported to the Patriarchate, and until the Patriarchate's reply came, it could not be handed over. Yisi said he had not handed it over; he only had Jingjing go along; the talking would still be his, the deciding still his. Samaan said: go along, go along three times, and Official Pei will know only Jingjing and not the Superintendent; that's just how rules get broken. The two of them stood in the courtyard talking a long while, all in Syriac, Samaan quick, Yisi slow to answer, and at the end Samaan opened his account book and made an entry: On such a day of such a month the Superintendent ordered the Deacon of Charity to accompany him to the government offices; the Warden of the Rule judged this improper, advised against it, was not heeded; to be reported to the Patriarchate. When he had written it, he shut the book and said: what he had to say he had said, what he had to record he had recorded; what Yisi did was Yisi's affair. Yisi looked at his account book, smiled, and said the elder had recorded it rightly. When Samaan had gone, Jingjing came out of the west wing and asked whether his Pa would do as the elder said. Yisi said: yes, and no; from now on, Official Pei's business, he'd still go himself, and Jingjing would go along and sit in the gatehouse waiting, and when they came back he'd tell him what had been said inside. Jingjing asked what he'd be going along for, then. Yisi said: going along to learn the road.

That day, past noon, Yisi went with Jingjing to the gruel kitchen at the West Market.

At the gruel kitchen a woman was keeping the accounts.

Yisi had seen her before he left, but had not taken notice. She had come in at the monastery gate in the days of the tenth month of the first year of Guangde when the Tibetans were in the city, along with the households of the ward that could not run. That day Jingjing had opened the gate and taken people in, a hundred and forty-odd all told, and she was one of them, supporting a lame old woman, her mother-in-law. She said herself that her own family's surname was Pei; she was eleventh in birth order among the girls of her family, Shiyiniang, the Eleventh Daughter; she had married into a family named Cheng in the Yankang Ward. The Cheng husband had been a militia soldier; when the militia garrisons were stopped he enlisted in the Shence Army, and in the second year of Qianyuan went with Army Supervisor Yu to attack Xiangzhou, and died below the walls of Xiangzhou; his body did not come back; only the scabbard of his straight sword was brought back by a man of his squad. The mother-in-law's legs were bad, and the two of them had kept a few old rooms, living by mending and stitching for the silk shops of the West Market. On the first night the Tibetans entered the city, half a street in the Yankang Ward burned; she carried her mother-in-law on her back and ran west all the way, and when she reached the Yining Ward she saw a lamp at the monastery gate, the gate open, and came in. When she told people about that night afterwards, she said little of the Tibetans; what she said most about was the young *hu* monk at the monastery gate. By then the inside of the gate was already packed with people; Elder Samaan stood inside waving his hands, saying the rice wasn't enough, take in more and it wouldn't last ten days; the young *hu* monk at the gate looked back once, turned round again, and let people in one by one; when he got to her and saw the mother-in-law on her back, he reached out and took the old woman from her and carried her into the hall on his own back. She followed behind, and saw him, carrying her, turn his head and tell the people behind not to push: the gate won't close, there's room for all. That young *hu* monk was Jingjing.

In those ten-odd days the monastery's gruel grew thinner day by day. Elder Samaan drank it thin himself, till his face was a size thinner; the time the Tibetan soldiers came to beat on the gate, she was hiding with the women of the ward in the felt tents in the back courtyard, and heard horses whinnying and men shouting outside, heard the old Tocharian believer at the gate jabbering in words she couldn't understand, and then it was quiet, and someone came in and said the Great Virtue Yi had given two sacks of rice and the men had gone. She did not know then who the Great Virtue Yi was, only that he was the one in charge at this monastery, a *hu*, who had fought alongside Lord Guo.

That winter the mother-in-law died in the monastery. Jingjing laid out her body for her and buried her at the edge of the ground south of the city where Yisi buried people. When she was buried, Pei Shiyiniang did not leave. She said the house in the Yankang Ward had burned, and if she went back there was nowhere to live; she could write, she could keep accounts; if the monastery had any use for her, she'd stay and work, for two meals a day. Elder Samaan was just then worrying over the gruel accounts; he could write Syriac but not Tang characters, and the registers the Tang ward headmen sent him he couldn't read a word of; hearing from Jingjing that she could read and write, he had her try. She tried: a whole register, from beginning to end, without one wrong character. From then on, the Tang accounts at the gruel kitchen were hers.

She could read and write because as a child she had learned from her father. Her father was of a distant branch of the Pei clan, had been a minor clerk in the Metropolitan Prefecture for some years; he had no son, and so taught his daughter as a son, taught her characters, taught her accounts, taught her to recite the *Classic of Filial Piety*. After she married into the Cheng family, the characters were no use to her; now, at the monastery, they were of use again. When Official Pei came to the monastery later and saw her at the gruel kitchen, he was stunned a good while before he recognized her as the daughter of a distant uncle of the clan, seen once as a child at a clan sacrifice, when she still wore her hair in two little tufts.

At first she only kept the accounts and left when she had done; when the monastery preached she did not listen, nor ask. Then once, copying a register for the ward headman for Jingjing, halfway through she asked him: those *hu* monks in your monastery, who get up every day before light and sing—what is it they sing? Jingjing said it was the psalms, songs written long ago by a king, who wrote of not being able to sleep at night, begging the Lord not to leave him. She thought a while and said that after her husband died, she too couldn't sleep at night. From then on she began listening to the preaching at the back of the hall. When Elder Marya preached, with Jingjing beside him putting it into Tang line by line, she sat in the last row by the door and listened, and when she had listened went back to the gruel kitchen to keep her accounts.

She listened more than a year. On the morning of the Day of the Resurrection in the first year of Yongtai, Elder Marya baptized her. Yisi was still at Hezhong then, and did not see it. The water for the baptismal basin Jingjing heated; he boiled it, then mixed in cold, mixed it till it no longer scalded the hand, and only then poured it into the big bronze basin. The baptismal name Elder Marya gave her was Martha; Jingjing translated for her that Martha was a woman in the scripture: the Lord went to her house, and she was busy getting the meal ready, busy running in circles, and the Lord said to her, your heart is hung with so many things, you'll never be done with being busy. When Pei Shiyiniang had heard it, she smiled and said: that name, now, is well chosen.

---

After Yisi came back, Official Pei came to the monastery twice.

The first time he only sat and drank a bowl of water, asked about Jingyang, asked after Lord Guo's health, and as he left stood a moment at the monastery gate looking toward the gruel kitchen, and said that Shiyiniang owed a great deal to the Great Virtue Yi's care these two years at the monastery. Yisi said he had not been in Chang'an; those who cared for her were Jingjing and Elder Samaan. Official Pei nodded and left.

The second time was early in the twelfth month, and he came out with it as soon as he was in the gate. He said Shiyiniang was a sister of his clan; her husband had been dead six years, her mother-in-law too was gone, and her own family had long since had no one left; a woman could not keep accounts at a monastery all her life. She would not go back to the Yankang Ward herself, nor would she marry a Tang again; he had asked her, and she said the people of the monastery treated her well, and she was willing to be at the monastery. When Official Pei got this far, he looked at Yisi and said: "The Great Virtue Yi's wife has been gone more than ten years, and your son is grown. In your religion, I hear, you're permitted to marry. I'll act as go-between. If the Great Virtue Yi finds it distasteful, take it that I never spoke."

Yisi was just then working out a rice account; when he had heard it, he put down his brush and sat a good while before he spoke. He said he was forty; he had three arrow wounds on his body that hurt on overcast days, and when he turned over at night you could hear it through a wall; he had fought ten years with Lord Guo, and if Lord Guo called him again he would still have to go, and whether he would come back he himself did not know; he was a *hu*, and the Tang looked at *hu* monks across a gap to begin with; marry a Tang widow, and what people said outside would not be pleasant. He said these things not to decline, but for fear Official Pei did not know them, and for fear Shiyiniang did not know them. He said too that he was a monk, and held office besides; marrying a Tang woman might not pass under Tang law; this was something Official Pei would have to ask about for him. He said that if she knew all this and was still willing, he would think about it.

Official Pei left, and a few days later came again, and said he had asked Director Liu of the Bureau of Sacrifices; Director Liu said you people were foreigners outside the pale, and when foreigners of one kind married among themselves it went by their own custom; Shiyiniang had entered your religion, and so counted as one of your people; the Bureau of Sacrifices was not concerned; on the Metropolitan Prefecture's side, she need only be struck off the Cheng household register, and that he would see to. Those few evenings Yisi sat under the lamp, and when Jingjing came in with the gruel he looked at him once and asked whether something was on his Pa's mind. Yisi told him what Official Pei had said, told it very flatly, as though it were someone else's affair. When Jingjing had heard it, he stood there, and after a while said: "Pa, these days since you came back, you eat alone at night, and when you've eaten you sit, and sit till the second watch. Shiyiniang keeps good accounts, and she's a good person. Ma's been gone so many years. If Pa had someone to eat with, I'd feel easier, out running about."

Yisi raised his head and looked at him. Jingjing was nineteen, half a head taller than he; as he said these words his face was a little red, but his eyes did not slide away. Yisi said he understood, and sent him to bed.

A few days later Yisi went to the gruel kitchen himself. His grey robe had a hole worn through at the elbow, worn at Hezhong. He stood by the gruel kitchen and waited until Pei Shiyiniang had finished the day's accounts and put her brush away in its bag, and only then went over and told her what he had told Official Pei that day, just as he had told it: the arrow wounds, the fighting, being a *hu*, what people would say. When he had told it, he added one more thing: that Jingjing was a child he and his former wife had taken as their own, not his own blood; in the monastery only one or two old people knew it; Jingjing himself did not know; he had never asked, and Yisi had never told him. He told her this for fear that someday someone might speak of it to her and she would think he had hidden it from her; and he begged her, if Jingjing did not ask, not to tell him.

When Pei Shiyiniang had heard it, she did not answer at once; she looked at the hole at the elbow of his robe, took a needle from the sewing pouch beside her brush bag, and told him to hold out his arm. Yisi was taken aback, and held out his arm. She stood right there by the gruel kitchen, beside a pot of gruel not yet cool, and mended that hole, and when it was mended bit off the thread, and only then raised her head and said: "These things the Great Virtue Yi has said, I've seen them all in two years at the monastery, and heard people speak of them too. You speak slowly; I can hear you clearly." She paused, and said again: "That short-lived man of mine, when he went, said nothing to me at all, not even 'wait for me to come back.' Since you're willing to tell me these things first, now I know."

---

Before the marriage, Elder Samaan spent a night going through the canons.

He was Warden of the Rule; the monastery's rules were all in his charge; Yisi was Superintendent, and for the Superintendent to take a wife he must first check for him whether it accorded with the rules. He spread out the few rolls of canons he had brought from the Patriarchate on the table one by one, went through them till midnight, and found two articles. The first had been laid down two hundred-odd years before by two great synods, saying those in holy orders were permitted to marry, and a priest who lost his wife was permitted to marry again, without losing his office; the second was laid down some decades later still, saying that from then on bishops should not marry, nor monks who had taken vows. He copied the two articles onto a sheet of paper and the next day took it to Elder Azarel; Azarel read it, nodded, and said: then go ahead.

When the matter was discussed, the seven teachers and the three elders from the Patriarchate were all there. Elder Ishaq was so pleased he slapped his belly and said he should have married long ago; the monastery's gruel all tasted the same; marry a Tang wife, and from now on the monastery would have Tang food to eat. Elder Gabriel asked which passage of scripture would be read at the wedding, and said he'd like to read the passage where Jacob met Rachel at the well. Elder Sabrisho said it was allowed, and when he had said it added: the rule says it's allowed, so it's allowed. Elder Narsai sat at the very edge and did not open his mouth from beginning to end; when they broke up, he got up, went over to Yisi, looked at him once, and said: "You never vowed." And having said it, he left. Yisi stood there and thought a while, and could not work out what he meant.

Elder Yonan was there too. He sat in a corner, his two hands folded on his knees, listening to the others, and when they had all spoken, he said he was glad for Yisi, and having said it bent his head and looked at his own hands. His hands were very clean, just washed, not a speck of dirt between the fingers.

Three days before the wedding, Pei Shiyiniang went once to the Cheng grave south of the city. There was no one in the grave; only that scabbard was buried there. Jingjing went with her and carried a basket of paper money for her; she squatted before the grave and burned it, and when it was burned, said a few words to the grave, in Tang, very low; Jingjing stood a few paces off, could not hear, and did not go nearer. When she had done, she stood up, brushed the dust off her knees, and said: let's go. On the way back she told Jingjing she had told him she was going to marry a *hu*, and that *hu* spoke slowly, and everything he said was true. Jingjing made no answer, but carried the empty basket for her. Narsai, hearing later that she had gone to burn paper money, frowned and said: a baptized person, still burning paper money. Sabrisho said the rule did not say it wasn't allowed; Yisi said it was he who had allowed it.

The wedding was held on the eighteenth of the twelfth month, very simply. Official Pei presided, and wrote the marriage document himself, and by Tang custom the rite was held at dusk. A few lamps were lit in the hall; Elder Marya officiated, and by the rule of the Patriarchate placed a crown on each of their heads, and had the two of them drink together from one cup of water, with a little earth in it brought from before the Holy Sepulchre; Elder Gabriel read the passage of Jacob at the well. Yisi wore his ordinary grey robe, not the purple; Pei Shiyiniang wore a dress of blue cloth she had sewn herself, nothing on her head, her hair put up with only a wooden pin. People came from the ward; the old soldiers from the gruel kitchen came too; Xu Jing drank two bowls of wine and stood at the door of the hall boasting to people that outside the Jade Gate he'd cut down seven bandits in one night; when he had said it, Hou Siyuan slapped him on the back of the head and said: what you cut down that night was an earthen platform. Yisi did not drink, but held a bowl of hot water and raised it to everyone who came. When the rite was done, Jingjing was the first to step forward; he made Pei Shiyiniang a bow and called her: "*Yi*."

When the Tang say *yi*, auntie, they mostly mean a mother's sister, though some use it for a father's secondary wife; when Official Pei heard it, his face moved. But Pei Shiyiniang answered to it, and answered very quickly, as though she had long since thought this child would call her so. Her eyes reddened a moment, and she reached out and brushed at a rice-water stain on his cuff; it would not brush off, and she laughed.

When Jingjing had called her so, he stepped aside and looked at his Pa. Yisi stood in the hall, still holding that bowl of hot water, saying something to Elder Marya, his face turned to the side as he spoke, the scar on his left brow twitching in the lamplight. When Jingjing was small in Lingzhou, every time his Pa came back from the camp, the first thing he did on coming in was take off his shield and hang it on the wall, then take off his mace and hang it beside the shield, and only then sit down to eat; and while they ate he spoke only Tang, and would not let him speak *hu*. In those days there were only the two of them in the house. The year his Ma died he was six; he remembered only that his Ma had washed his feet in warm water and at night stuffed his padded coat with cotton, remembered that when the apricot tree in the monastery bloomed, his Ma was gone. He watched his Pa standing side by side with the bride in the hall, and all at once he thought his Pa had grown old, and thought too that his Pa seemed to have grown a little younger, and could not say which it was.

---

That night the party broke up late. When the guests had all gone, and the lamps in the hall had been put out one by one, Yonan came out from the back courtyard carrying the oil jug, and went to the monastery gate to see to the lamp.

The lamp had long been lit that evening; he had come out to add the midnight oil. For years now he had been quick to claim the lighting of the lamp and the adding of oil; when the young deacons said, Elder, let me do it, he said: I'll do it; when Jingjing said, Elder, let me, he still said: I'll do it. Every evening he came out of the back courtyard with the oil jug in one hand and a slow-match in the other, lifted the paper cover, added oil, trimmed the wick, lit it, put the paper cover back; and when it was back he did not leave at once, but stood a while at the gate, looking east, looking south, as if waiting for someone to turn in from the ward gate. So it was this night too. When he had added the oil he stood there, and heard voices in Yisi's room in the side courtyard, speaking Tang, one voice slow, one voice soft, speaking a while, pausing a while, and then they could not be heard. Yonan stood under the lamp till his hands were cold. He remembered the fourth year of Tianbao.

Of the fourth year of Tianbao, Yonan remembered some things and Yisi remembered some things, and put together it went roughly like this. That year Yonan was forty.

---

He had come to Chang'an in the autumn of the third year of Tianbao. That year a Great Virtue had come from the kingdom of Daqin, named Jihe, leading a company eastward from the Patriarchate; the Son of Heaven summoned him, with Luohan, Pulun and others of the monastery, seventeen in all, to perform rites of merit in the Xingqing Palace. The Patriarchate sent Yonan to be bishop in Chang'an, and he came with Jihe's company, arriving with them. When he arrived, the monastery's old believers came out to meet him, and seeing his face smooth, not a single hair of beard on it, the skin drawn tight, the eyes very bright, muttered among themselves: how could the Patriarchate send a baby? Later, seeing his hands, they stopped muttering. His hands were lean, the sinews standing out, ten years older than his face.

In those days the monastery was still called the Persian monastery. In the ninth month of the next year the Son of Heaven issued an edict saying this religion came from Daqin and had been transmitted to the Middle Kingdom long ago; the name Persian monastery was not right; the Persian monasteries of the two capitals should all be called Daqin monasteries, and those in the prefectures and commanderies of the realm should be changed likewise. On the day the new plaque was hung, the monastery's people all stood at the gate to watch, and Yonan stood watching too; of the characters on the plaque he knew not one; Yisi read them out to him character by character.

Yonan was a very diligent bishop: every day he preached, baptized, kept accounts, met officials. He preached in Syriac; there was an old Sogdian believer in the monastery, thirty years in Chang'an, who put it into Tang for him; Yonan said a sentence and stopped, waiting for the old believer to translate a sentence. Meeting officials was the same: he said a sentence, the translator said a sentence, the official said a sentence, the translator said another, back and forth, and a whole morning was gone. Of Tang he knew a few dozen words—eat, drink, sit, good, not good, thank you, Lord—and could understand somewhat more, but could not speak it. Tang characters he could not write one.

At his side he had a young deacon named Yisi, from Balkh, twenty years younger than he, three years in Chang'an, whose Tang was slow but every word of it clear. Yisi had married the year before, a Sogdian girl, and the two of them lived in the side courtyard east of the monastery, as yet without children. In those years Yisi was often borrowed by the old teacher of the monastery who could heal eyes, to go with him into the palace and into the Palace Medical Service to interpret, and was not often at the monastery; sometimes, when Yonan had finished preaching and Yisi happened to be there, he would have Yisi say the important things over again for him, and when Yisi had said them the people below would nod. Yonan, watching them nod, felt a little envious, and a little ashamed.

---

One day in the second month of the fourth year of Tianbao, a porter came to the monastery looking for him; he said his child was sick, had been feverish three days, and he wanted to borrow a few coins from the bishop to buy medicine. That day Yonan was in the front hall seeing a minor clerk of the Metropolitan Prefecture about a boundary of the monastery's land, and had talked all morning; the porter could not wait, and told the gatekeeper he lived in the West Market, in the lane behind the *hu* eating-house—ask and you'll find it—and having said it left, probably afraid there was no one to watch the child. When Yonan had seen the clerk off and heard the gatekeeper's message, he went back to his room for a string of cash, and took his medicine bag too. At the Patriarchate he had studied some years with an old physician; he could treat the sick and make up a few medicines; he thought that after three days' fever, money alone would not be enough.

He went alone. The West Market was not far from the Yining Ward; he went in at the north gate of the West Market, through the silk-sellers, the medicine-sellers, the sellers of gold and silver ware, south, until he came to a narrow lane. At the mouth of the lane was an eating-house run by a *hu*, a great pot set up at its door with mutton bones boiling in it, white steam rising in clouds. Behind the eating-house was that lane. The lane was very narrow, the walls on either side close together; the sun did not get in, the ground was always wet, and rotten vegetable leaves and charcoal ash were heaped at the foot of the walls. A few doors stood half open, and in them sat women with powdered faces, who looked at him once as he came in and then did not look; one laughed and said something in *hu*, and the others laughed too.

Yonan understood the *hu* words, and did not stop. He asked three people in the lane, and none knew of any porter; he walked from one end of the lane to the other and back again, and did not find him. He stood in the middle of the lane not knowing whether to go or to wait, thought, and decided to wait a little. When he had waited about as long as it takes to drink a cup of tea, he heard someone coughing at the foot of the wall.

The cough was muffled, as if coughed into a hand, a fit and a pause and another fit. Yonan followed the sound to the very end of the lane, where the wall turned a corner, and at its foot squatted a woman. She was very thin, wrapped in a lined jacket of no colour you could name, her hair loose, her face buried in her knees, both arms clasped round herself, her shoulders shaking at every cough; on the ground was a patch of what she had coughed up, streaked with blood. Yonan squatted down and wanted to say "you're sick," but for the moment could not remember the word "sick," and reached out to feel her forehead. She shrank back at once, the back of her head knocking the wall, and raised her head to look at him. It was a very young face, twenty or so, the cheekbones whittled high by illness, the lips white, the eyes very dark, dark as a well. She said: "Don't touch. Dirty."

That word Yonan knew. He did not draw back his hand, but laid it on her forehead—very hot—and took her wrist and felt the pulse, counted a while: fast and floating. He asked whether she was cold; she looked at him, nodded, and shook her head. Yonan helped her up and helped her to the door of the eating-house at the mouth of the lane. The *hu* keeper of the eating-house knew her; he said she worked at his stove—washed bowls, split wood—for two meals a day, and rented a draughty little room in the back lane; this half month she'd been coughing badly, couldn't do the work, couldn't afford charcoal. Yonan counted out some coins from the string, laid them on the keeper's chopping board, and said a few words to him in *hu*. The keeper took the money, led her into a little room behind the kitchen, brought a brazier, and fetched a bowl of water.

She took the bowl and was going to drink; Yonan reached out and held the bowl down. It was a bowl of water just drawn from the well, cold rising from its rim. He could not think of the words, and pointed at the pot on the stove, then at the bowl. The keeper understood, poured the cold water away and ladled a bowl of hot. She held that bowl of hot water a long time before she drank it in little sips, and when she had drunk it raised her head, looked at Yonan, and said something. Yonan did not understand. What she said was that from a child she couldn't drink cold water—one drink and her stomach was upset—how did this *hu* monk know? Yonan understood those words only many days later; and even then he did not know; he only treated the sick, and those who treat the sick do not let the sick drink cold water. That day he made up a packet of medicine for her—apricot kernel, liquorice, and two kinds of root brought from the Patriarchate—and told the keeper to boil it for her once a day. When he left, the lane was already dark; he asked round the lane once more about the porter, and still no one knew.

Afterwards he went to see her a few more times. The first time was three days later: she was sitting by the brazier, coughing less, a little colour in her face; when she saw him come in she stood up, not knowing how to bow, and stood with her head down. He asked her name; she said A-Lian. He repeated "A—Lian," very awkwardly, as if he had a pebble in his mouth, and she smiled, very quickly, and at once put the smile away. The second time she had stopped coughing, and asked how much the medicine cost; he said no money; she did not believe it and kept asking, and he could not find the words, and pointed at the sky. She looked where he pointed and asked: heaven gave it? He thought, and nodded: that was more or less it. The third time he went, she was not in the little room; the keeper said she was well again and back at the stove washing bowls, soaking in cold water from morning to night, and wouldn't rest when told to. Yonan stood at the door of the eating-house and through the kitchen curtain saw her bent over a big wooden tub washing bowls, sleeves rolled to the elbow, arms red with cold. He stood a while, did not go in, and turned back to the monastery. That night he knelt a long time before the cross; he did not know what he was asking for; he only knelt there.

---

Half a month later, she came to the monastery. That day the monastery was preaching; Yonan stood at the front preaching, the old Sogdian believer beside him translating, twenty or thirty people sitting below, more *hu* than Tang. As he preached Yonan saw someone standing at the door, wrapped in that jacket of no colour, leaning on the doorpost, not coming in and not going away. He paused; the translator thought he had finished a sentence and translated it; he went on preaching. When it was over and the people had dispersed, she was still standing at the door; Yonan went over, and she said: "I came to listen." He said: "Sit." That word he could say.

From then on she came every few days, always sitting in the last row by the door, leaving when it was over, speaking to no one. When the monastery's old believers saw her, some frowned, some pretended not to see; one old woman said a few words in *hu* to Yonan, meaning: Bishop, someone out of that lane—it won't do to bring her into the monastery. Yonan heard her out and said: "She, listen." The old woman opened her mouth and did not get any words out.

The preaching at the monastery was in the afternoon, and when it was over it was nearly dark. From the ward gate to the monastery gate you had to pass through a narrow stretch of street with no shops and no lamps along it, and a corner was missing from the step at the monastery gate, crushed long ago by a cart wheel and never mended. One day she left a little late, dusk already falling, and as she went out stepped right on that broken corner and went down with a thud on her knees on the step, skinning both of them. Yonan heard the sound and came out of the hall; she was already on her feet, brushing the dust off her clothes, saying it's nothing, it's nothing, and went off limping. The next evening there was a lamp on the stone block at the monastery gate, a coarse earthenware oil dish with a paper cover over it. When the monastery's people asked who had put it there, Yonan said: the step is broken; light it. The step was later mended; the lamp was not taken away.

She listened at the monastery half a year, and in the eighth month was baptized, not long before the new plaque was hung. That morning the air had already turned cool; the monastery's baptismal basin was a big bronze basin, ordinarily filled with well water. The night before, Yonan had gone to the side courtyard and said a word to Yisi's wife, and first thing the next morning that Sogdian woman heated a pot of water on the stove, boiled it, then mixed in cold, mixed it till it no longer scalded the hand, carried it into the hall and poured it into the bronze basin. Yonan put his hand in to test it: the water was warm. A-Lian knelt before the basin, and Yisi's wife stood at its side supporting her. Yonan scooped up water in his hands and poured it on her head, poured three times, saying one Name each time, and when he had done, dipped his finger in oil and drew a cross on her forehead. The water ran down her hair, over her forehead, over her eyes, and dripped on the ground before her knees; she kept her eyes closed until Yonan had finished, and only then opened them, and her eyes were full of water, there was no telling which kind. She stood up and said softly: "It's hot." Yonan made no answer, turned away, and shook the water left on his hands dry. That day Yonan gave her a baptismal name, Susanna, a pure woman in the scripture. She could not say the name; she said it a few times, and still called herself A-Lian.

---

After her baptism she came more often still. One day when the preaching was over she stayed behind and told Yonan she wanted to learn to read, wanted to be able to write her own name. As she said it her two hands twisted together, as if she were saying something very shameful. Yonan could not write Tang characters; he thought a moment, brought out from his room a spoiled wooden writing tablet, wrote four Syriac letters on it in charcoal, right to left, and pointed to them, reading them out to her: "Alaph, Lamadh, Yudh, Nun." She looked at those four curly things a long time and asked: is this me? Yonan said: you. She put out a finger and traced the four letters once, very slowly, and when she had traced them, traced them again.

She learned very fast: in one month she knew all twenty-two letters; in two months she could spell the name of everyone in the monastery. She spelled Yisi, spelled Yonan, spelled the old Sogdian believer, crookedly, but without a mistake. Yonan found an old roll of the psalms and taught her to read it letter by letter; she could not understand the meaning, but she could sound it out. When she read, Yonan sat beside her listening, listening to her read the Syriac with a Chang'an accent, the tail of every word lifting a little, and as he listened he laughed. She stopped and asked him what he was laughing at; he said: good.

She taught him too. She said she knew few characters; she had worked some years at the eating-house stove, and at its door hung a board with the things it sold written on it; she had looked at it every day till she knew it: cake, soup, mutton, wine, noodles, money. The Tang characters she knew were all things to eat, plus one for money. One day she said: you preach in Chang'an and can't write one Tang character—how can that do? And she wrote on the back of that wooden tablet with the charcoal, wrote one, had him read it, then had him copy it. Cake. Soup. Mutton. The characters he wrote had crooked horizontals and crooked verticals, like a fence blown over by the wind, and she laughed and laughed at them, and when she had laughed, made him write them again. She taught him a great many; he learned one and forgot one; today he learned *soup*, and tomorrow remembered only the three drops of the water radical. In the end even she was at her wits' end, thought a while, and wrote the simplest one.

"*Xin*," she said. Heart. She said it was the *xin* of *dianxin*, the snacks on the board; of all the characters she knew, it was the easiest to write: one hooked curve, three dots. "Look, it's like a bowl with three beans in it." Yonan looked at it a long time, took up the charcoal and wrote one; the hook curved too wide, the three dots crowded to one side; she shook her head, and he wrote another. He wrote all afternoon, wrote till dark, and in the heart he wrote, the three beans had at last all dropped into the bowl. She said: remember this one; never mind the others; you people preach, you're always preaching about the heart. He remembered it, and never minded the others.

---

What came after, Yonan could seldom remember in order; he remembered only scattered things.

He remembered that when she ate a sesame cake she always broke it in two first, took one half herself and pushed the other half across to him; he said he'd eaten already; she paid no heed and pushed it across, and he ate it. He remembered that in winter her hands got chilblains, splitting open and oozing bloody water, and still she worked at the eating-house stove, her hands soaking in cold water washing bowls; he made up a jar of salve for her, mutton fat mixed with a few powdered medicines, and every time she came to the monastery she first washed her hands clean at the door and only then held them out for him to put on the salve. He remembered that reading the psalms she stopped at one place and asked what it meant, and he put it into Tang and could not, and gestured a long while, and at last she said she understood: it was someone keeping watch in the night, waiting for the dawn. He remembered the first time she called him by name: she called him Yonan, but a Tang mouth was not used to the sound, and it came out Yuenan, and he did not correct her.

Of that year, Yisi saw some things too. He saw Yonan come back from the West Market with mud from the back lane splashed on the hem of his robe; saw Yonan, when he had finished preaching, look toward the last row by the door; saw the lamp in Yonan's room at night, once lit, burn till the fourth watch. He was a deacon and Yonan the bishop; he asked nothing; only once, when Yonan had him take medicine to the West Market in his place, he went, and at the kitchen door of the eating-house gave the medicine to the woman washing bowls; she took it and asked whether Yuenan had sent him; he said yes. He came back to the monastery, told Yonan the medicine was delivered, and said not one word more.

Yonan remembered too the fifteenth of the first month of the fifth year of Tianbao. That year at the Lantern Festival, by the edict of the year before last, the ward gates were not shut for three nights, the fourteenth to the sixteenth of the first month; the streets were full of lanterns, people pressed against people, and the merrymaking went on till dawn. Those three nights the lamp at the monastery gate was still lit by him, and when he had lit it, he did not go back to the back courtyard. This Yonan never told anyone.

He remembered too that every night before and after, he knelt before the cross and begged the Lord to take this thing out of his heart. He begged one autumn, and begged one winter. The Lord did not take it away.

---

That spring, she came to find him. That day she did not sit in the last row; she waited till everyone had gone, came and stood before him, and told him. She said it very flatly, as though she had thought about it many days, and having thought it through, there remained only the step of saying it. She said: "You're the bishop. If people find out about this, you're finished, and your monastery's finished too. People outside say you're a *hu* religion to begin with; let a thing like this happen, and they'll say, look at those *hu* monks, having children with women from the lane behind the eating-house. I won't have you ruined. I'll go, I'll take the child and go, go far away, where no one will find us."

Yonan stood there and could not say a word. Of the few dozen Tang words he knew, not one was of any use. When she had said it, she left; the lamp at the gate was already lit, and she walked past under it, her shadow drawn out very long, all the way to the ward gate, where it turned a corner and was gone. Yonan stood in the hall till midnight.

She did not go far. Alone, no money, no home, her belly bigger day by day—how far could she go? She lived another half year in that lane in the West Market; the keeper of the eating-house took pity on her and still let her work in the kitchen, giving her two meals a day. The women of the lane said she was a fool, said she'd been taken in by a *hu* monk; she said nothing, washed bowls, split wood, and at night sat alone in that draughty little room by a little stove-fire tracing those four letters on the wooden tablet. In the ninth month of the fifth year of Tianbao the child was born, a boy, very thin, his cry very small, his eyes dark like hers.

---

On the fifth day after the child was born, she carried him to the Yining Ward; she did not go in at the monastery gate but went round to the side courtyard east of the monastery and knocked. It was Yisi who opened the door. Yisi was twenty-one that year, not tall, broad-shouldered, his hair short, his brown eyes very deep; he looked at the woman outside the door, at the child wrapped in rags in her arms, looked a while. He knew her; she had sat more than a year in the last row at the monastery; there was no one in the monastery who did not know her. A-Lian told him, told it very briefly, only that she could not raise this child, and begged him to take it as his own. Yisi asked only one thing: "Can this child live?" A-Lian said it could. Yisi said: "Then I'll take him." He turned his head and called into the house, and his wife came out; when the Sogdian woman saw the child she stood still a moment, said nothing at all, reached out and took him, carried him in, laid him by the brazier, and turned to heat water on the stove. A-Lian stood at the door watching the woman inside crouch before the stove adding wood, watched a while, did not go in, and turned and left.

That night Yisi's wife washed the child's body in warm water; she mixed the water herself, mixed it three times, tested it three times with her elbow. When the child was washed he stopped crying and lay with those dark eyes open, looking at the ceiling. She wrapped him up, held him in her arms, and sat by the brazier all night. Yisi woke in the middle of the night, saw her still sitting there, and asked whether she was tired; she said no, and said that in two years since she married him her belly had never stirred; she had begged the Lord many times at night, and the Lord had not given her one, and today one came to the door. As she said it she kept her head bowed, looking at the child's face, and said this child's eyes are dark, not like yours, not like mine either; if anyone asks from now on, say they're like his mother's side. Yisi did not answer her, but got up and put another piece of charcoal on the brazier. That Sogdian woman raised the child six years afterwards, taught him to walk, taught him to talk, taught him to make the sign of the cross before eating, stuffed his padded coat with cotton in winter, stuffed it right up to the winter she fell ill.

Three days later the child was to be baptized. Yisi carried him into the hall; Yonan was already waiting before the bronze basin, and the water in the basin was warm again. There were not many people in the hall that day; Yisi and his wife stood beside the basin, and in the last row by the door stood one more, wrapped in that jacket of no colour. Yonan saw her, and his hand paused on the rim of the basin before he took the child, scooped up water, and poured it over that small, small head, three times. The child cried once, very softly. Yonan named him Adam, saying it was the name of the first man.

When the baptism was over, Yonan went back to his own room, opened the monastery's register of baptisms, took up his reed pen, and wrote one line in Syriac: "In the year one thousand and fifty-eight of the Greeks, in the month of Teshri, baptized one male infant, named Adam. Son of A-Lian. Received by Yazdbozid and his wife." Yazdbozid was Yisi's own name; the Tang could not say it, and called him only Yisi. When he had written it he came out carrying the register, went to the rear door of the hall, stood a moment on the threshold, and only then went down. A-Lian had not yet gone; she was standing at the foot of the steps outside. Yonan handed her the register, then held out the pen; halfway, the pen stopped a moment before it reached her hand. She looked at the line of writing, could not read it, recognized only her own four letters in it; she took the pen, and below that line, with those four letters, stroke by stroke, she wrote her name. When she had written it she gave him back the pen, turned, and left.

That night Yonan went to the side courtyard. Yisi and his wife were not yet asleep; the child was sleeping in a bamboo basket by the brazier. Yonan stood at the door and did not go in, but called Yisi out, and the two of them stood under the jujube tree in the side courtyard. There was a moon that night, and Yonan's face under it was shining white. He told Yisi, in Syriac, very slowly, word by word, as if each word had to be hauled up from somewhere very deep. He said that child was his.

Yisi heard him out to the end, and when he had heard it, stood under the jujube tree and for a long while did not move. Yonan said he did not ask Yisi to hide it for him; he asked only that Yisi not tell the child; when the child grew up, when one day he himself came back, he would tell him himself. Yisi said: "Elder, I won't tell him this. But if one day he asks me, I won't lie." Yonan looked at him, nodded, and said: then let it be so.

---

The letter from the Patriarchate came that winter. It said: Bishop Yonan is recalled to the Patriarchate; set out at once. Why he was recalled the letter did not say. Afterwards some said someone in the Chang'an monastery had written to the Patriarchate; others said no one wrote, the Patriarchate had caught wind of it on its own. Yonan himself never asked.

The day he left, a light snow was falling. He did not go to the West Market, and she did not come. He walked to the monastery gate; the lamp was not yet lit, it was still light; he stood a while before the stone block, lifted the paper cover, filled the oil dish to the brim, and put the cover back. Yisi was waiting outside the gate holding the horse, and in Yisi's arms was the child, wrapped in a sheepskin, only his small face showing. Yonan looked at the child once, only once, then turned his head away, mounted, and said from the saddle: "Lamp. Keep it lit." Yisi said he would keep it lit. Yonan turned his horse's head and went west, the snow falling on his shoulders, falling on that smooth beardless face of his, and he never once looked back.

Two years later a letter came from the Patriarchate to Chang'an, sent by Yonan through someone, asking Yisi to go to the West Market and find someone. Yisi went; the people of the lane said she was gone; not many days after the child was given away she left, taking nothing with her, and no one knew where she had gone. Yonan, at the Patriarchate, received Yisi's reply; the reply was very short; he read it and burned it. From that year on Yonan washed his hands three times a day; why three times, he never told anyone.

Whose the child was, Yisi told no one for nineteen years. Jingjing from childhood called him Pa and called his former wife Ma, and never asked. In the first year of Zhide, Yonan asked of his own accord to go east, came back to the east from the Patriarchate with Jingjing's party, and all along the road watched this ten-year-old child writing under the lamp, and said to him: the way you write, you're like someone.

---

On the night of the eighteenth of the twelfth month of the first year of Yongtai, Yonan stood at the monastery gate until the second watch.

Over in the side courtyard there had long been no sound, and the ward had long been without voices; the snow on the street had not yet all melted, and the moon shone on the snow and made the ward walls white. Yonan stood till his legs were stiff before he remembered that he should go back. He bent down, lifted the paper cover, and looked at the oil in the dish; there was still plenty of oil, but the flame was leaning a little; he meant to trim the wick, and the slow-match went out in the wind; he turned his back, shielding it from the wind with his body, blew on it again, and could not get it to catch.

There were footsteps behind him. It was Jingjing, coming out from the kitchen in the back courtyard, rice water on his sleeves, an empty wooden bucket in his hand; all evening he had been clearing up what was left from the wedding for his Pa, and had put the rice to soak in the kitchen for tomorrow's gruel kitchen at the West Market, and only now come out. He saw Yonan crouched before the stone block shielding the flame, put down the bucket, came over, squatted down too, and cupped both hands round the slow-match.

"Elder," he said, "let me." Yonan looked at him. The lamplight came up from below on those dark eyes. Yonan gave him the slow-match.

Jingjing trimmed the wick straight, and put the paper cover back, put it back very straight.

Note: Jihe's coming east in the third year of Tianbao and the edict summoning Luohan, Pulun and others, seventeen in all, to perform rites of merit in the Xingqing Palace, and the edict of the ninth month of the fourth year of Tianbao changing the Persian monasteries of the two capitals to Daqin monasteries, follow the stele and *Tang huiyao* juan 49 (the edict's "prefectures and commanderies of the realm"). The ward gates left open for three nights at the Lantern Festival follow an edict of the third year of Tianbao (*Tang huiyao* juan 49 / *Cefu yuangui*; exact source to be checked). Baptism in the Church of the East by threefold pouring and the sealing of a cross in oil (the stele's "seal of the cross"); the crowning and the common cup at weddings, an old custom of the Church of the East's marriage rite. That clergy might marry, and might remarry on being widowed, follows the decisions of the Synod of Acacius (486) and the Synod of Babai (497); the rule that bishops not marry came later, around the mid-sixth century (to be checked). The Cheng family were originally militia soldiers; after the militia garrisons ceased issuing their fish tallies in the eighth year of Tianbao, the husband enlisted in the Shence Army; the Xiangzhou campaign was that of the nine military governors in the second year of Qianyuan, with Yu Chao'en as Army Supervisor. That foreigners outside the pale offending among their own kind were judged by their own custom follows the *Tang lü shuyi*, "Mingli." Pei Shiyiniang, the Cheng family, the baptismal names Martha and Susanna, the register of baptisms, and what Samaan recorded are the novel's invention.
