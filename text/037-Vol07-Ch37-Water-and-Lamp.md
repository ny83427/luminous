# Book Two　Messiah

# Volume Seven　A-Lian

## Chapter 37　Water and Lamp

> Man's first nature, whole and simple, was open and did not swell with itself; his heart, plain and unclouded, had at first no craving.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

The gruel was still warm, as promised. Jingjing had set the pot on the brazier in the side courtyard and kept it simmering all along, and when he ladled it out, white steam still rose from the bowl's rim. Yisi sat under the lamp, holding the bowl in both hands, drinking a mouthful at a time, very slowly, as if he meant to melt all the cold of the road out of his belly. Jingjing sat across from him and waited until he had finished, then went and ladled half a bowl more, set it at his elbow, and only then began to tell him, one thing after another, what had happened at the monastery these two years.

"After the Tibetans left, of the hundred and forty-odd people who had sheltered in the monastery, about a hundred went home. Ten or so were left whose houses had been burned and who had nowhere to go, and Elder Samaan had them stay for the time being in the felt tents in the back courtyard," Jingjing said. "The Elder ran it by the letter of the patriarch's instructions: teams of fifty, each with a team leader, gruel twice a day, and who had drawn how many bowls is all in the ledger, not one entry out of place. Early last spring Elder Azarel took me to Luoyang. Old Jacob in the Xiushan Ward is still there, only he's gone deaf; you have to shout at him. I shouted three times before he understood whose child I was, and when he understood, he took my hand and wept. The Mu family's widow has remarried, another man who sells sesame cakes, and the shop next door has opened again. She says a sesame-cake oven mustn't go cold; cold is bad luck."

At this the corner of Yisi's mouth moved. He asked how Lingzhou was.

"We couldn't get to Lingzhou. Last year it fell into Pugu Huai'en's hands and the road was cut for a whole year; only this ninth month, when Huai'en died at Mingsha, did it open again. Master Jiao sent a letter by someone. He says he goes to sweep the Lingzhou monastery every other day, and the bronze cross on the east wall he polishes every day, polishes till you can see your face in it, and Pa is not to worry." Jingjing paused, and his voice dropped. "Things are bad in Hexi. Liangzhou was lost last year. Fewer caravans go west every year; these two years not a single letter has come from the Patriarchate, and the letters the monastery sends, we don't know whether they arrive. And one more thing. Elder Azarel says that when spring comes there is a great matter the monastery must discuss. I asked what it was, and the Elder wouldn't say; he said only that it would wait until Pa came back."

Yisi turned the empty bowl in his hands. After a while what he asked about was something else: "The accounts of the gruel—can Elder Samaan read them? As I remember he doesn't know Tang characters. The register the ward headman sent over, he always used to bring to me to read aloud."

"The Elder doesn't keep the accounts in Tang characters," said Jingjing. "In the days when the Tibetans were in the city, a woman came into the monastery with the people from the Yankang Ward, surnamed Pei. She can read and write and reckon. The Elder saw her fine hand and put her in charge of the accounts in Tang characters. Two years now; I've checked them against the stores several times, and she hasn't been wrong by a single character."

Yisi heard him out, set the bowl down on the table, and said, "The charity you've managed for two years, and managed well. From now on you go on managing it. And from now on you come along with me on business with Official Pei. I've thought it over. From now on I'll keep only the monastery's general accounts and the letters to the Patriarchate; everything else, little by little, I'll hand over to you all."

Jingjing was taken aback, and asked whether Pa was tired—whether he had been hurt, this time away.

"I wasn't hurt, and I'm not so very tired either." Yisi smiled. "Ten years I've watched the road for Lord Guo; my eyes have earned a rest. You've gruel to give out at the West Market tomorrow. Go to bed. I'll blow out the lamp."

---

Early the next morning Elder Samaan came to the side courtyard with his leather-bound ledger in his arms. What Jingjing had been told the night before had somehow already reached his ears.

He stood in the middle of the courtyard, first tucked both hands into his sleeves, and when they were tucked in, began. He spoke in Syriac, fast and dense, as if he had lined up the words in his belly all the way here: "The Superintendent is back, and everyone in the monastery is glad, and I am glad too. But there is one thing I must say first. The patriarch's instructions are plain: all dealings with the government go through one line only, the Superintendent and the official surnamed Pei, and no one else may form private connections. That clause the patriarch added in his own hand, and why, the Superintendent knows better than I do: the monastery in Chang'an cannot bear several people each saying something different. Jingjing is Deacon of Charity. If the Superintendent wishes to hand this line to him, he must first write to report it to the Patriarchate, and when the Patriarchate replies, then he may hand it over. Until the reply, not one step."

Yisi said, "I understand everything the Elder says. I haven't handed it to him. I'm only having him come along. I still do the talking, I still make the decisions; he sits by and listens and learns something."

"He comes along; the first time he sits by and listens; the second time he passes on a word for the Superintendent; the third time, Official Pei knows only Jingjing and not the Superintendent." Samaan shook his head. "Superintendent, I kept the rules at the Patriarchate for twenty years. A rule has never once broken all at once. They all loosen like this, a little at a time."

The two of them talked in the courtyard for the better part of an hour, Samaan fast, Yisi slow, and neither moved the other. In the end Samaan sighed, opened the ledger, took a reed pen from his sleeve, and at the stone table in the courtyard made an entry: On such a day of such a month the Superintendent ordered the Deacon of Charity to accompany him to the government offices; the Warden of the Rule judged this improper, advised against it, was not heeded; pending report to the Patriarchate. When he had written it he blew the ink dry, closed the ledger, and said, "What I ought to say I have said, and what I ought to record I have recorded. What the Superintendent does is the Superintendent's affair. If the Patriarchate asks, I will answer by this entry."

Yisi looked at the ledger and actually laughed. "The Elder is right to make that entry. If one day the Patriarchate punishes someone, let it punish me, not him."

Samaan went off with the ledger in his arms. Jingjing had been listening behind the door of the west wing all this while; only now did he come out and ask whether Pa would do as the Elder said.

"Half as he says and half not," said Yisi. "With Official Pei I'll still go myself. You come along and sit and wait in the gatehouse; don't come in. When we get back I'll tell you, sentence by sentence, what was said inside and what Official Pei's face looked like. What Elder Samaan says has its reason too. We can't let people say the monastery in Chang'an has no rules."

Jingjing thought about it and was a little rebellious. If he came along and sat in the gatehouse, he said, what was he supposed to do there?

Yisi said, "Learn the way. Which street you go in by, which gate you come out of, what the gatekeeper's surname is, whom you bow to first—these things you can't learn indoors. When I first followed Lord Guo, the whole first year I couldn't get a word in either; I just followed along."

---

That day, after noon, Yisi went with Jingjing to the gruel shed in the West Market.

A woman was keeping the accounts at the shed. She sat on an upturned rice measure with a register open across her knees, and each time someone passed in front of the pot she looked up, put a stroke in the register, and looked down again, her hand never still. An old man who had got his gruel didn't move on, and told her today's gruel was thinner than yesterday's. Without looking up she said yesterday the rice shop had sent new rice and today old rice, and old rice didn't cook thick; let the old gentleman come a little earlier tomorrow, the bottom of the pot was thick. The old man went off grumbling. Yisi had seen her before he left, and had paid her no attention then; this time he stood to one side and watched a good while.

When everyone had been served, Jingjing led him over. The woman stood, closed the register, and said, "Master Yi."

Yisi cupped his hands to her and said, "Jingjing has told me that these two years the accounts in Tang characters at the monastery have all been kept by you, ma'am. While I was away, the monastery was held up by everyone in it, and you are one of them. I must thank you to your face."

The woman was a little embarrassed and waved her hands. "Please don't say so, Master Yi. I've eaten the monastery's gruel two years, and my mother-in-law's funeral was seen to by the monastery. A few entries in a ledger don't even pay for the gruel."

Her own family was surnamed Pei, and she was the eleventh of her generation; she had married into a family named Cheng in the Yankang Ward. The Cheng husband had been a militia soldier, and after the militia garrisons were disbanded he enlisted in the Shence Army; in the second year of Qianyuan he went with Army Supervisor Yu against Xiangzhou, and died below the walls of Xiangzhou. His body never came back; the men of his squad brought back only the scabbard of a cross-sword. After that she and her lame mother-in-law kept a few old rooms and lived by sewing and mending for the silk dealers of the West Market. In the tenth month of the first year of Guangde, when the Tibetans entered the city, half a street of the Yankang Ward burned the first night. She carried her mother-in-law on her back and ran west all the way to the Yining Ward, saw a lamp at the monastery gate and the gate open, and pushed in with the crowd. Inside the gate was already packed. Elder Samaan stood just within, waving his hands, saying there wasn't enough rice; take in more and they wouldn't last ten days. The young *hu* monk at the gate looked back at the Elder, turned round again, reached out and took the old woman from her back, and carried her toward the hall, calling back to those behind as he went: "Don't push, don't push—the gate won't close, there's room for everyone!" That young *hu* monk was Jingjing. When she spoke of that night afterward, she said little about the Tibetans; what she spoke of most was that one sentence.

That winter her mother-in-law died in the monastery. Jingjing laid out the body and buried it at the edge of the ground south of the city where Yisi buried people. When the burial was done she did not leave. She told Elder Samaan that the house in the Yankang Ward had burned and there was nowhere to live if she went back; she could write and reckon, and if the monastery had use for her, two meals a day would do. Samaan was just then at his wits' end over the gruel accounts, and told her to try. She tried, copied a whole register from beginning to end, and made not one wrong character. She had learned her letters as a girl from her father, a distant branch of the Pei clan who had served some years as a minor clerk in the Metropolitan Prefecture; there were no sons in the house, so he taught his daughter as if she were a son. Later, when Official Pei came to the monastery and saw her at the gruel shed, it took him a good while to recognize her: she was the daughter of a distant uncle of the clan, whom he had seen once as a child at a clan sacrifice, when she still wore her hair in two little tufts.

At first she only kept the accounts and left when she had finished. When the monastery preached, she didn't listen or ask. Once, copying out for Jingjing a register for the ward headman, she stopped halfway with her brush lifted and asked him, "Master Jingjing, let me ask you something, and don't laugh at me. Those *hu* monks of yours get up every day before light and sing. It sounds lovely. I've been listening from the back courtyard a good many days. What is it they sing?"

Jingjing said, "Those are the psalms. Long ago there was a king named David. At night he couldn't sleep, his heart was bitter, so he wrote these songs, begging the Lord not to leave him. We get up before light and sing, and what we sing is the words he wrote in the night."

She was silent a good while after hearing this, then said, "So even a king can lie awake at night. After that man of mine died, I lay awake too, eyes open, waiting for the dawn."

From then on she listened to the preaching at the back of the hall. Elder Marya preached, Jingjing beside him rendering it sentence by sentence into Tang speech, and she sat in the last row by the door, and when it was over, went back to the gruel shed to keep her accounts. She listened more than a year, and on the morning of Easter in the first year of Yongtai Elder Marya baptized her; Yisi was still at Hezhong. The water for the baptism Jingjing heated. Marya gave her the baptismal name Martha. Jingjing rendered it for her: Martha was a woman in the scriptures; the Lord went to her house, and she was busy getting the meal ready, running round in circles, and the Lord said to her, Martha, Martha, you have so many things on your heart, you could be busy and never be done.

When Pei Shiyiniang heard it she burst out laughing. "Now that's a name well chosen. How did the Elder know I'm someone who can't sit still?"

---

After Yisi came back, Official Pei came to the monastery twice. The first time he only sat and drank a bowl of water, asked about Jingyang, asked after Lord Guo's health, and as he left stood a moment at the monastery gate looking over toward the gruel shed, and said that these two years Shiyiniang had been at the monastery, much obliged to Master Yi's care. Yisi said he had not been in Chang'an; those who had looked after her were Jingjing and Elder Samaan. Official Pei said "Mm," said nothing more, mounted, and left.

The second time was early in the twelfth month. He came in and sat down in the little room where business was discussed. Yisi had tea brought; he didn't drink it, and opened by putting the matter plainly.

"Master Yi, we've known each other all these years, and I won't beat about the bush. I've come today as a go-between," said Official Pei. "Shiyiniang is my clan sister. Her husband has been dead six years, her mother-in-law is gone too, there's no one left of her own family. A woman alone, all by herself—she can't keep accounts in your monastery for the rest of her life. I've asked her. She won't go back to the Yankang Ward, and she won't marry another Tang man either. When I asked her why, she said the people at the monastery treat her well, and she wants to stay at the monastery." Here he looked at Yisi. "Master Yi, your wife has been gone more than ten years, and your son is grown. Your religion, I've heard, allows marriage. I'm making this match; if you think it beneath you, we'll say I never came today, and go on drinking tea as before."

Yisi had been working on an account of rice. When he had heard it out he laid his brush beside the inkstone and for a long while said nothing. Official Pei didn't press him, picked up his tea, and drank it slowly.

"I thank you first for your kindness, Official Pei," Yisi began, speaking very slowly. "Only there are a few things I must say first, so as not to do her wrong later. I am forty this year. I carry three arrow wounds; they ache when it's overcast or raining, and at night they hurt so I turn over, and you can hear it through the wall. I followed Lord Guo to war ten years. If Lord Guo calls me from now on I must still go, and whether I come back from going, I can't say myself. I am a *hu*. The Tang look at *hu* monks through a layer of something to begin with; if I marry a Tang widow, what people say outside will not be pleasant, I'm afraid—she'll go out to buy a handful of greens and be pointed at. And one more thing. I am a monk, and I also carry an office from the court. Whether the Tang law allows this, I truly don't know; that point, I must ask you to look into for me." He stopped, thought, and went on: "I don't say these things to put you off. I'm afraid you don't know them, and more afraid she doesn't know them. If she knows all of it and is still willing at heart, I will think it over properly."

Official Pei stroked his beard and laughed. "Master Yi talks the way he always does. In other families' matches, people set out their good points first; you set out your shortcomings, a whole table full of them. Well then, I've noted what you said. The question of the law, I'll ask about for you."

A few days later he came again and said as he came in that it was settled. He had asked Director Liu of the Bureau of Sacrifices, and Director Liu had turned the commentaries on the code for a good while and turned up a clause: you people are foreigners outside the pale, and when foreigners of one kind marry among themselves it goes by their own custom; Shiyiniang, having entered your religion, counts as one of your people, and the Bureau of Sacrifices is not concerned; the Metropolitan Prefecture need only strike her off the Cheng household register, and that he would see to. "Master Yi," he said, "the door in the law I've opened for you. The rest depends on you."

Those few nights Yisi sat under the lamp until the second watch. Jingjing came in with gruel and saw he was neither looking at the accounts nor writing letters, just sitting, and asked whether Pa had something on his mind. Yisi thought, and told him what Official Pei had said, very evenly, as if it were some other family's business.

Jingjing heard it out and stood there a while before he said, "Pa, there are some things I've wanted to say for a long time and never dared. These days since you came back you eat alone at night, and when you've eaten you just sit, sit until the second watch; I can see the lamp in your window from the west wing. Shiyiniang keeps good accounts, and she has a good heart; I've seen it these two years. Ma has been gone so many years. If Pa had someone to eat with, someone to talk to at night, I'd feel easier too, running about outside."

He was nineteen, half a head taller than his father, and as he said these few sentences his face went a little red, but his eyes stayed on his father the whole time and did not dodge. Yisi said he understood, told him to put the gruel down, and go to bed.

---

A few days later Yisi went to the gruel shed himself.

He stood at the side of the shed and waited until Pei Shiyiniang had finished the day's accounts and put her brush away in its case, and only then went over. His grey robe had worn through at the elbow, a hole rubbed at Hezhong, which he himself had never noticed.

"Shiyiniang," he said, "I expect you've heard what Official Pei had to say. I have a few things to say too that I think should be said to your face, not passed along by someone else."

He said again exactly what he had told Official Pei that day—the arrow wounds, the wars, being *hu*, the talk outside—and left out not one thing. When he had finished he didn't go, but lowered his voice and went on: "There's one more thing. Only one or two of the old people in the monastery know it, and I think you should be told too. Jingjing is a child my first wife and I took as our own; he is not my own son. He himself doesn't know. He has never asked, and I have never said. I tell you because I'm afraid someone may bring it up to you one day, and you'll think I hid it from you on purpose. And I ask one thing of you: so long as he doesn't ask, don't tell him."

Pei Shiyiniang did not answer him at once. She looked at the hole in the elbow of his robe, looked a while, took a needle from the sewing kit beside her brush case, threaded it in the sunlight, and said, "Master Yi, give me your arm first. Your robe's worn through like this and you didn't even know?"

Yisi was taken aback, and held out his arm.

Right there at the side of the shed, in front of a pot of gruel not yet cool, she mended the hole stitch by stitch. When she had finished, she bent and bit off the thread, put the needle back in the kit, and only then raised her face.

"What you've told me, Master Yi—in two years at the monastery I've actually seen all of it, and heard people speak of it too. You talk slowly, but each sentence is a sentence; I can hear it clearly," she said. "When that short-lived man of mine went, he told me nothing at all, not even 'wait for me to come back.' I waited three years for him, and what came back was a scabbard. You were willing to tell me these things one by one first, not even hiding the child's affair from me. Now I know where I stand. Go back and tell my brother Pei that I know how to answer you."

---

Before the wedding, Elder Samaan spent a whole night turning the canons. Whether it was proper for the Superintendent to marry, he had to establish first. He spread the several rolls of canons he had brought from the Patriarchate on the table, turned them till midnight, and turned up two clauses. The first was fixed by two great synods more than two hundred years ago: those in holy orders may marry, a priest widowed may marry again, and is not deprived of his office. The second came some decades later: from now on bishops shall not marry, and those who have taken monastic vows shall not marry. Yisi had never been a bishop, nor taken monastic vows. Samaan copied the two clauses out fair on a sheet of paper and the next day brought it to Elder Azarel, who read it and said, then let it be done.

When the matter was discussed, the seven teachers of the law and the three elders from the Patriarchate were all present. Elder Ishaq was the first to slap his belly and laugh. "He should have married long ago! The monastery's gruel tastes the same every day; it's drunk my tongue numb. Marry a Tang wife, and from now on we'll have Tang dishes too. I'll say it now—I want to eat her noodle soup." Elder Gabriel asked which passage of scripture would be read at the wedding. He wanted to read the one where Jacob met Rachel at the well. "Jacob kept another man's sheep seven years for Rachel, and the seven years seemed to him but a few days. The years Yisi has lived alone are longer than seven." Elder Sabrisho said that was fine, thought, and added: "If the rules say yes, then it's yes." Elder Narsai sat at the very end and said not a word from beginning to end. When they broke up he rose, went over to Yisi, and said one sentence: "You never took the vow." Then he left. Yisi stood where he was and thought about it a long time, and could not work out what he meant.

Elder Yonan was there too, sitting in a corner with his hands folded on his knees, listening to the others laugh and talk. When everyone had finished he said he was glad for Yisi, truly glad, and having said it, lowered his head and looked at his hands. His hands were very clean, just washed; there was not a speck of grime between his fingers.

Three days before the wedding, Pei Shiyiniang went to the Cheng grave south of the city. There was no one in the grave; only the scabbard was buried there. Jingjing went with her and carried a basket of paper money for her. She squatted before the grave and burned it, saying a few words under her breath as it burned, and Jingjing stood a few steps off and did not come closer. On the way back she told Jingjing herself: "I told him I'm getting married, to a *hu*, a *hu* who talks slowly and says only true things. I said, don't blame me down there; if you blame anyone, blame yourself for leaving too soon." Jingjing didn't know how to answer that, and carried the empty basket for her all the way back to the Yining Ward.

When Narsai heard later that she had gone to burn paper money, he frowned and said how could someone baptized still burn paper money. Sabrisho said the canons didn't say she mustn't. Yisi said, "I told her to go. She lived her life with him a while; before leaving, she ought at least to go and tell him."

---

The wedding was fixed for the eighteenth of the twelfth month, the rite at dusk as the Tang custom was. Official Pei presided, and wrote the marriage document in his own hand. A few lamps were lit in the hall. Elder Marya officiated and, by the custom of the Patriarchate, set a crown on each of the two, and had them drink from one cup of water, a little earth from before the Holy Sepulchre mixed into it. Elder Gabriel read the passage of Jacob at the well, read it with rise and fall, and his own eyes reddened first. Yisi wore his everyday grey robe, not the purple *kasaya*. Pei Shiyiniang wore a blue cotton dress she had sewn herself, nothing on her head, her hair caught up with a single wooden pin.

Some people came from the ward, and the old soldiers from the gruel shed came too. Xu Jing drank two bowls of wine and stood at the hall door boasting to people that once, outside Jade Gate Pass, he had cut down seven robbers in a night, cut till his blade curled. Hou Siyuan smacked him on the back of the head and said, "What you cut that night was earth mounds. Seven strokes, and not one mound fell; what you did do was scare off your own horse." The whole courtyard laughed. Yisi didn't drink wine. He held a bowl of hot water and raised it to everyone who had come, and thanked each of them.

When the rite was done, Jingjing was the first to step forward. He made Pei Shiyiniang a proper, formal bow and called her: "Auntie."

When the Tang say *yi*, auntie, they usually mean a mother's sister; some use it for a father's secondary wife. Official Pei's face moved at it, and he was about to speak, but Pei Shiyiniang had already answered, answered very quickly, as if she had long since thought the boy would call her so. Her eyes reddened. She reached out to brush at a mark of rice water on his cuff, brushed twice and couldn't get it off, and laughed first herself. "This boy—on the day of the wedding, and still gruel on his sleeve. Take it off tomorrow; Auntie will wash it for you."

Jingjing stepped back to one side and looked at his father. Yisi, holding his bowl of hot water, had turned his face to talk with Elder Marya, and the scar over his left eyebrow jumped and jumped in the lamplight. Jingjing remembered how in Lingzhou when he was small, each time Pa came back from the camp he would come in, first take off his shield and hang it on the wall, then take off his mace and hang it beside the shield, and only then sit down to eat, and at table only Tang speech was allowed. In those days there were just the two of them in the house. Ma had died the year he was six; all he remembered was that Ma had washed his feet in warm water and in the winter nights quilted his padded jacket, and that when the apricot tree in the monastery flowered, Ma was gone. He looked at Pa standing side by side with the bride in the hall and thought Pa had grown old, and thought too that Pa seemed a little younger, and couldn't say which.

---

They broke up late that night. When the guests had all gone and the lamps in the hall had gone out one by one, Yonan came out of the back courtyard with the oil pot and went to the monastery gate to add the midnight oil.

The lighting of the lamp and the adding of oil at the monastery, he had for years always been first to take on. The young deacons said, Elder, you rest, I'll do it; he said, I'll do it. Jingjing said, Elder, it's cold, I'll do it; he said again, I'll do it. Every evening he came out of the back courtyard with the oil pot in one hand and the slow-match in the other, lifted the paper cover, added oil, trimmed the wick, lit it, put the paper cover back, and when it was on he did not leave at once but stood a while at the gate, looked east, looked south, as if waiting for someone to turn in from the ward gate. The monastery's people took this for the Elder's old habit, and no one had ever asked. This bronze oil dish Yisi had dug out of the side room in the winter of the second year of Zhide, and at first it was set on the steps; the next year, when Yonan came back to Chang'an, on the very first evening he moved it to this stone block to the left of the gate, and said there was less wind here.

So too this night. When he had added the oil he heard voices in Yisi's room over in the side courtyard, speaking Tang, one voice slow, one voice soft, talking a while, pausing a while, and then he could no longer hear them. Yonan stood under the lamp until his hands were cold. Who had first lit this lamp, only two people in the monastery now knew: he, and Yisi. He thought of the fourth year of Tianbao.

---

He had come to Chang'an in the autumn of the third year of Tianbao. That year a great virtue of Daqin named Jihe had come east from the Patriarchate with a company, and the Son of Heaven summoned him, with Luohan, Pulun and others of the monastery, seventeen in all, into the Xingqing Palace to perform rites of merit. The Patriarchate sent Yonan to be bishop of Chang'an, and he came in Jihe's company. The monastery's old believers came out to meet him, saw his smooth face without a single hair of beard, the skin drawn tight, the eyes very bright, and muttered among themselves: how had the Patriarchate sent a child? Later they saw his hands and stopped muttering. His hands were thin, the sinews standing out, ten years older than his face. He was forty that year.

The monastery was still called the Persian Monastery then. In the ninth month of the next year the Son of Heaven issued an edict saying this religion came originally from Daqin and had long been spread in the Middle Kingdom, and to call the monastery Persian was wrong; the Persian monasteries of both capitals were all to be called Daqin monasteries, and those in the prefectures and commanderies of the realm likewise. The day the new plaque went up, the monastery's people all stood at the gate with their heads tilted back to look, and Yonan looked too. He knew not one character on the plaque; Yisi read them out to him character by character.

Yonan was a diligent bishop: preaching, baptizing, keeping the accounts, seeing officials every day, he would let none of it slip. Only one thing he could not do: he could not speak Tang. He preached in Syriac, and an old Sogdian believer who had lived thirty years in Chang'an interpreted for him; he spoke a sentence, stopped, and waited while the old man rendered it. Seeing officials was the same: he said a sentence, the interpreter said a sentence, the official said a sentence, the interpreter said a sentence again, back and forth, and a morning was gone with the business not half discussed. He had a few dozen Tang words—eat, drink, sit, good, not good, thank, Lord—and understood rather more, but could not speak them. Tang characters he could not write at all.

At his side was a young deacon, Yisi, from Balkh, twenty years younger than he, three years in Chang'an, whose Tang was slow but clear in every syllable. Yisi had married the year before, a Sogdian woman, and the couple lived in the side courtyard east of the monastery and had no children yet. In those years Yisi was often borrowed by an old teacher of the law at the monastery who could heal eyes, going with him into the palace and the Imperial Pharmacy to interpret, and was not often at the monastery. When Yonan finished preaching, if Yisi happened to be there, he would have him say the important things again, and when Yisi said them, the people below nodded. Yonan watched them nod and felt a little envy, and a little shame.

---

One day in the second month of the fourth year of Tianbao, a porter came to the monastery looking for the bishop. His child had had a fever three days, he said, and he wanted to borrow a few coins for medicine. Yonan was just then seeing a minor clerk from the Metropolitan Prefecture in the front hall about a boundary of the monastery's land; they had talked all morning and were not done. The porter couldn't wait. He told the gatekeeper he lived in the lane behind the *hu* eating house in the West Market, anyone would know, and left. When Yonan had seen the clerk off and heard from the gatekeeper, he went to his room and took a string of cash, and his medicine bag too. He had studied some years with an old physician at the Patriarchate; he could treat the sick and make up a few kinds of medicine. A child with a fever of three days, he thought, needed more than money.

He went alone. He went in by the north gate of the West Market, walked south through the rows of silk sellers, medicine sellers, gold and silver smiths, and came to a *hu* eating house. A great pot stood at its door with mutton bones boiling in it, the white steam rising in clouds. Behind the eating house was the lane: narrow, the walls on either side close together, the sun never reaching in, the ground always wet, rotten leaves and charcoal ash heaped along the foot of the walls. A few doors stood half open, and inside them sat women with powdered faces. One of them laughed as he came in and said something in a *hu* tongue, and the others laughed after her. Yonan understood the words, and did not stop. He asked three people in the lane, and all said they knew of no porter. He walked from one end to the other and back again, stood in the middle of the lane, not knowing whether to go or to wait, and thought, and decided to wait a little.

After about as long as it takes to drink a cup of tea, someone at the foot of the wall coughed, a muffled cough, as if into a hand. Yonan followed the sound to the innermost corner of the lane, where a woman was crouching, very thin, wrapped in a padded jacket of no colour you could name, her hair loose, her face buried in her knees. Each time she coughed her shoulders shook; by her feet was a patch of what she had coughed up, threaded with blood. Yonan crouched down and wanted to say "you are sick," but couldn't think of the word for sick, and reached out to feel her forehead. She shrank back so fast that the back of her head struck the wall, and raised her face. It was a very young face, twenty or so, the cheekbones sharpened by illness, the lips white, the eyes very dark.

"Don't touch me," she said. "I'm dirty. You're a master from the monastery, aren't you? Don't dirty your hand."

Yonan understood only "don't touch" and "dirty." He did not draw back. He laid his palm on her forehead: it was burning. He took her wrist and felt the pulse: quick and floating. He helped her up and half carried her to the front of the eating house at the mouth of the lane. The keeper knew her. She worked in his kitchen, he said, washing bowls and splitting firewood for two meals a day, and rented a drafty little room in the back lane; this half month she had been coughing so she couldn't work, and couldn't afford charcoal either. Yonan counted some coins from the string onto the chopping board and said a few words to the keeper in a *hu* tongue. The keeper took the money, led her into a little room behind the kitchen, brought a brazier, and brought a bowl of water too.

She took the bowl and was about to drink. Yonan put out a hand and held the bowl down. It was water just drawn from the well; the cold was still on the bowl's rim. He couldn't think of the words, so he pointed at the pot on the stove, then at the bowl. The keeper understood, threw out the cold water, and ladled a bowl of hot. She held the hot water and drank it down in small sips, and raised her head and said to Yonan, "You're a strange one for a *hu* monk. How did you know I can't drink cold water? I never could, since I was little; one drink of it and my stomach goes wrong. When my mother was alive she boiled it for me, winter and summer. Since my mother died, no one has ever minded about that again."

Yonan understood not a word. It was many days before he learned what she had said that day. That day he knew only that whoever treats the sick does not let the sick drink cold water. He made her up a packet of medicine—apricot kernel, liquorice, and two kinds of root he had brought from the Patriarchate—and told the keeper to brew it for her once a day. When he left, the lane was already dark. He went from door to door once more asking after the porter, and still no one knew.

---

Three days later he went again. She was sitting by the brazier, coughing less, a little colour in her face. When she saw him come in she got up in a flurry, didn't know how to greet him, and stood with her head bowed. He asked her name, asked twice before she understood.

"I'm called A-Lian," she said. "*Lian* as in lotus. The year my mother bore me, the lotuses in the pond by our door bloomed well, so she gave me this name."

He repeated after her: "A—Lian." He said it very stiffly, as if he had a pebble in his mouth. She laughed once, very quickly, and at once held it in, as if afraid she had laughed wrong.

The second time he went, she had stopped coughing, and kept asking him how much the medicine cost: she could work now, she said, and would earn it and pay him back coin by coin. He said no money. She didn't believe it and asked again. He couldn't find the words, so he raised a hand and pointed at the sky.

"No money?" She tipped her face up to look where his finger pointed. "Where in the world is there medicine that costs no money? Heaven gives it? Your *hu* heaven is more generous than ours."

He thought, and nodded. That was about what it meant.

The third time he went, there was no one in the little room. The keeper said she was well, and had gone back to the stove washing bowls, soaking in cold water from morning to night; tell her to rest and she wouldn't. Yonan stood at the eating-house door and, through the kitchen's door curtain, saw her bent over a big wooden tub washing bowls, sleeves rolled to the elbow, her arms red with cold. He stood a while, did not go in, and turned back to the monastery. That night he knelt before the cross till midnight, not knowing what he was praying for, just kneeling there.

Half a month later she came to the monastery. Preaching was going on that day: Yonan stood in front preaching, the old Sogdian believer interpreting beside him, twenty or thirty people sitting below, more *hu* than Tang. In the middle of it Yonan saw someone leaning in the doorway, wrapped in that padded jacket of no colour you could name, not coming in and not leaving. He stopped a moment. The interpreter thought he had finished a sentence and rendered it, and he went on. When the people had gone she was still standing at the door. Yonan went over, and she said, red in the face, "I… I came to listen. I won't come in—I'll just stand at the door and listen, I won't be in your way. Is that all right?"

Yonan said, "Sit." That word he could say. When he had said it he also reached out and pointed to the last row, for fear she wouldn't understand.

From then on she came every few days, always sat in the last row by the door, left when it was over, and spoke to no one. Of the monastery's old believers who saw her, some frowned and some pretended not to see. An old woman pulled Yonan aside and said a long string of things in a *hu* tongue. The gist was: Bishop, people who come out of that lane are not to be brought into the monastery. Bring them in, and after this, what woman of a decent family will be willing to come?

Yonan heard her out and said one sentence in Tang: "She, listen." He thought, and added in the *hu* tongue that the Lord in the scriptures had also eaten with tax collectors and with women like that. The old woman opened her mouth and said nothing.

The preaching at the monastery was in the afternoon, and when it ended it was nearly dark. From the ward gate to the monastery gate you went through a narrow stretch of street with no shops and no lamps, and one of the steps at the monastery gate had a corner broken off, crushed years before by a cartwheel, never mended. One day she left late, at dusk, stepped right on the broken corner, and went down with a thump on the steps, skinning both knees. Yonan heard the noise and came out of the hall. She was already up, brushing the dust off her clothes, saying it's nothing, it's nothing, my skin is thick, I don't break, and limped off. The next evening there was a lamp on the stone block at the monastery gate, a coarse earthenware oil dish with a paper cover over it. When the monastery's people asked who had put it there, Yonan said the step was broken; light it up. The step was mended later. The lamp was not taken away.

---

She listened to the preaching at the monastery half a year, and in the eighth month of that year she was baptized, a little before the new plaque went up. The monastery's font was a big copper basin, ordinarily filled with well water. The weather had turned cool by then. The night before, Yonan went to the side courtyard and said one sentence to Yisi's wife; early next morning the Sogdian woman boiled a pot of water on the stove, and when it had boiled, mixed in cold, mixed it until it no longer scalded the hand, carried it into the hall, and poured it into the copper basin. Yonan put his hand in to try it: it was warm. A-Lian knelt before the basin, Yisi's wife standing beside her to steady her. Yonan scooped the water up in his hands and poured it over her head, three times, naming a name each time, and when he had finished, dipped his finger in oil and drew a cross on her forehead. The water ran down through her hair, over her brows and eyes, and dripped onto the floor before her knees. She kept her eyes closed until Yonan finished, then opened them, and they were full of water, and you could not tell which kind of water. She stood up and said softly, "It's warm."

Yonan made no answer, turned away, and shook the water from his hands. The baptismal name he gave her that day was Susanna, a pure woman in the scriptures. She couldn't say the name; she tried it a few times and still called herself A-Lian.

After her baptism she came even more often. One day when the preaching was done she stayed behind, twisting her hands together as if about to say something very shameful, twisted them a long while, and then said, "Master, I want to learn to read. I've lived twenty years and can't even write my own name. When people sign a contract they write their names; I can only press a thumbprint. I want to be able to write my own name."

Yonan could not write Tang characters. He thought, and fetched from his room a spoiled wooden writing tablet, and with a stick of charcoal wrote four Syriac letters on it, right to left, and pointed at them one by one and said them for her: "Alaph, Lamadh, Yodh, Nun."

She stared at those four curling things, stared a long while, and asked him, "This is me? These few twisty-turny things—this is A-Lian?"

Yonan said, "You."

She put out a finger and traced the four letters, very slowly, and when she had traced them, traced them again. She learned fast: in a month she knew all twenty-two letters; in two months she could spell the name of every person in the monastery—Yisi, Yonan, the old Sogdian believer—crookedly, but none of them wrong. Yonan found an old roll of the psalms and taught her to read it letter by letter. She didn't understand the meaning, but she could sound it out, and read the Syriac in a Chang'an accent, the tail of every word tipping up. Yonan, listening beside her, began to smile. She stopped, a little cross, and asked what he was smiling at—did she read it badly? He shook his head and said, "Good."

She taught him too. The Tang characters she knew were few, all from the water board at the eating-house door: cake, soup, mutton, wine, noodles, money; she saw them every day and knew them by heart. "You preach in Chang'an and can't write a single Tang character—how can that do? Some official hands you a notice and you can't read it. People could sell you off and you'd help them count the money." So she wrote on the back of the tablet, wrote one, had him say it, then had him copy it. The characters he wrote had crooked horizontals and bent verticals, like a fence blown askew by the wind. She laughed at them, and when she had laughed, made him write again. One day he learned "soup," and the next day he remembered only the three drops of the water radical. By the end of it she was at her wits' end, thought, and wrote the simplest one.

"*Xin*, heart," she said. "The heart in 'dim sum,' *dianxin*, on the water board. Of all the characters I know, it's the easiest to write: one hooked curve, three dots. Look—isn't it like a bowl with three beans in it?"

Yonan took the charcoal and wrote one; the hooked curve was too big, and the three dots were crowded to one side. She shook her head, and he wrote again. He wrote most of the day, wrote until dark, and in the heart he wrote then, the three beans had at last all fallen into the bowl.

She clapped her hands and said, "Remember this one, and never mind the rest. You people preach and always talk about the heart. If you can write this one, that's enough."

He remembered it, and never minded the rest.

---

What happened after that, Yonan could not well remember in order; he remembered only scattered things. He remembered that when she ate a sesame cake she always broke it in two first, took one half herself, and pushed the other half over to him; he said he had eaten, she paid no attention and pushed it over anyway, and he ate it. He remembered that in winter she had chilblains on her hands, cracked open and oozing blood and water, and still soaked in cold water at the eating-house stove washing bowls; he made her a jar of salve of mutton fat mixed with powdered medicine, and every time she came to the monastery she first washed her hands clean at the door before holding them out for him to put on the salve. He remembered that once, reading the psalms, she stopped at a place and asked what it meant, and he couldn't render it into Tang and gestured a long while, and at last she said she understood: it was someone keeping watch in the night, waiting for the dawn. He remembered the first time she called him by his name: a Tang mouth couldn't manage the sound, and she said Yonan as *Yue-nan*, and he did not correct her.

Of that year, Yisi saw something too. He saw Yonan come back from the West Market with the hem of his robe spattered with the mud of the back lane; saw Yonan, when he had finished preaching, always looking toward the last row by the door; saw the lamp in Yonan's room, once lit, burn until the fourth watch. He was a deacon and Yonan was the bishop, and he asked nothing. Once Yonan had him go to the West Market to deliver medicine in his place. He went, delivered it to the kitchen door of the eating house, and gave the medicine to the woman washing bowls. She took it, wiped her hands on her apron, asked whether *Yue-nan* had sent him, and asked whether *Yue-nan* had been ill these two days, that he hadn't come himself. Yisi said the Elder was busy; the medicine was made up by the Elder's own hand; she was to put it on three times a day. When he got back to the monastery he told Yonan the medicine was delivered, and of anything else said not one word.

At the Lantern Festival of the fifth year of Tianbao, by the edict of the year before, the ward gates were not closed for the three nights from the fourteenth to the sixteenth of the first month. The streets were full of lamps, people jostling people, the noise going on until dawn. On those three nights it was still Yonan who lit the lamp at the monastery gate, and when he had lit it, he did not go back to the back courtyard. That, he never told anyone.

Every night before that and every night after, he knelt before the cross and begged the Lord to take this thing out of his heart. He begged through an autumn, and through a winter, and the Lord did not take it out.

---

That spring she came to find him. That day when the preaching was over she did not sit in the last row. She waited until everyone had gone, walked up and stood before him, and said what she had to say. She said it very evenly, as if she had thought about it for many days, and when the thinking was done, only this step was left, of saying it.

"You're the bishop. If people find out about this, you're finished, and your monastery's finished too. People outside already call you a *hu* religion; if a thing like this comes out, they'll say: look at those *hu* monks, having children with women from the lane behind the eating house. I won't have you ruined. I'll go, take the child and go, go far away where no one can find me."

Yonan stood there. Of the few dozen Tang words he had, not one was any use. When she had said it she left. The lamp at the gate was already lit; she walked out beneath it, her shadow trailing very long, all the way toward the ward gate, turned a corner, and was gone. Yonan stood in the hall until midnight.

She did not go far. One person, no money, no home, her belly bigger every day—how far could she go? She lived another half year in that lane in the West Market. The eating-house keeper pitied her and let her go on helping in the kitchen for two meals a day. The women of the lane said she was a fool, that she had been taken in by a *hu* monk; she said nothing, washed bowls, split firewood, and at night sat alone in that drafty little room by a little fire, tracing those four letters on the wooden tablet. In the ninth month of the fifth year of Tianbao the child was born, a boy, very thin, with a very small cry, his eyes as black as hers.

---

On the fifth day after the child was born she carried him to the Yining Ward. She did not go in by the monastery gate, but went round to the side courtyard east of the monastery and knocked at the door.

It was Yisi who opened it. He was twenty-one that year, not tall, broad in the shoulders, his hair short. He looked at the woman outside the door and the child wrapped in rags in her arms, and for a moment said nothing. He knew her. She had sat a year and more in the last row of the monastery; there was no one at the monastery who did not know her.

"Deacon Yi," said A-Lian, "I know I shouldn't have come. I've thought five days and can't think of another way. I'm on my own; I can't keep him. The room in the lane lets the wind in on every side, and in another month it'll be cold; he'll freeze to death. I beg you to take him as your own—say he was found at your monastery gate. About him, I'll tell no one, and after this I won't come looking for him."

Yisi looked down at the child. The child's face was no bigger than a palm, wrinkled, eyes closed. He asked her, "Can this child take the breast? Crying so small as that—can he be kept alive?"

"He can take it," said A-Lian. "He'll live. He was just born thin. He doesn't like to cry. He isn't sick."

Yisi said, "Then give him to me. Don't worry. My wife and I will raise him as our own."

He turned his head and called once into the house. His wife came out. The Sogdian woman saw the child, stopped short a moment, asked nothing, reached out and took him, carried him in, laid him by the brazier, and turned to the stove to heat water. A-Lian stood at the door, watched the woman inside crouch before the stove feeding the fire, did not go in, and turned and left.

That night Yisi's wife washed the child in warm water. She mixed the water herself, mixed it three times, tried it three times with her elbow. When he was washed the child stopped crying and lay with his black eyes open, looking at the roof. She wrapped him up, held him in her arms, and sat by the brazier all night. Yisi woke in the middle of the night, saw her still sitting there, asked whether she was tired, and told her to put the child down and rest.

"I'm not tired," she said, head bowed, looking at the child's face. "Two years I've been married to you and nothing has stirred in my belly. At night I've begged the Lord so many times, and the Lord didn't give. And today, look—one came to the door." She drew the child closer, and said, "Look at this child's eyes, so black. Not like you, not like me. If anyone asks, after this, just say he takes after his mother's people."

Yisi didn't answer her. He got up and put another lump of charcoal in the brazier. That Sogdian woman raised the child six years after that, taught him to walk, taught him to talk, taught him to sign the cross before eating, quilted his padded jacket every winter, right up to the winter she herself fell ill.

Three days later the child was baptized. Yisi carried him into the hall; Yonan was already waiting before the copper basin, and the water in the basin was warm again. There were not many people in the hall that day. Yisi and his wife stood by the basin, and in the last row by the door stood one more person, wrapped in that padded jacket of no colour you could name. Yonan saw her. His hand paused on the rim of the basin before he took the child, scooped up the water, and poured it over that small, small head, three times. The child cried once, very softly. Yonan named him Adam, and said it was the name of the first man.

When the baptism was done, Yonan went back to his room, opened the monastery's register of baptisms, took up a reed pen, and wrote one line in Syriac: "In the year of the Greeks one thousand and fifty-eight, in the month of Tishri, baptized one male infant, named Adam. Son of A-Lian. Received by Yazdbozid and his wife." Yazdbozid was Yisi's own name; the Tang could not say it, and called him only Yisi. Yonan carried the register to the back door of the hall, stood a moment on the threshold, and only then went down the steps. A-Lian had not left yet; she was standing at the foot of the steps. Yonan handed her the register, then held out the pen; halfway, his hand stopped, and only then went on into hers. The line of writing she could not read; she recognized only her own four letters in it. She took the pen and beneath that line, with those four letters, wrote her own name stroke by stroke, and when she had written it, gave back the pen, turned, and left.

---

There was a moon that night. Yonan went to the side courtyard and did not go in, but called Yisi out, and the two of them stood under the jujube tree in the courtyard. The child was asleep inside in a bamboo basket by the brazier. Yonan spoke in Syriac, very slowly, each word as if it had to be hauled up from somewhere very deep.

"Yisi, I am your elder. I ought to be in the hall blessing you both, not standing here saying such things to you. But this thing, I cannot keep you in the dark about it, with you raising this child for me. That child is mine."

Yisi stood under the jujube tree and did not move. Yonan told him all of it, starting from that lane in the West Market, down to those three nights of the Lantern Festival, down to what she had come to say to him that spring, and hid nothing of it on his own behalf. When he had finished, only then did he say what he asked.

"I don't ask you to hide it for me from the monastery, or from the Patriarchate. What must come will come, and I will bear it myself. I ask you only one thing: don't tell the child. He's small and knows nothing; let him be your son first, and grow up well. When he's grown, when one day I come back, I will tell him myself. That is for me to say, not for you."

It was a while before Yisi spoke, and he still called him Elder. "Elder, what you entrust to me, I take on. I won't tell him. Only there is one thing I must make clear to the Elder first: if one day he grows up and comes himself to ask me, I won't lie."

Yonan looked at him and for a long while said nothing; then he said, "Then let it be so. That's good too."

The letter from the Patriarchate came that winter, recalling Bishop Yonan to the Patriarchate, to set out at once. Why he was recalled, the letter did not say. Some said later that someone at the Chang'an monastery had written to the Patriarchate; some said no one had written, that the Patriarchate had heard the rumour itself. Yonan himself never asked.

The day he left, a light snow was falling. He did not go to the West Market, and she did not come. He walked to the monastery gate. It was still light; the lamp was not yet lit. He stood a moment before the stone block, lifted the paper cover, filled the oil dish to the brim, and put the cover back. Yisi was waiting outside the gate leading the horse, the child in his arms, wrapped in a sheepskin jacket with only his small face showing. Yonan looked at the child once, only once, turned his head away, and mounted. In two years in Chang'an he had learned little Tang; as he left, from the saddle, he said one sentence in Tang, said it with great effort:

"Lamp—keep lit."

Yisi, holding the child, said, "Don't worry, Elder. It'll be lit."

Yonan turned his horse and went west. The snow fell on his shoulders, fell on that smooth, beardless face of his, and he never looked back.

Two years later a letter came by way of the Patriarchate, sent on Yonan's behalf, asking Yisi to go to the West Market and find someone. Yisi went. The people in the lane said she had gone, gone a few days after the child was given away, taken nothing, and no one knew where. At the Patriarchate Yonan received Yisi's reply, read it, and burned it. From that year on Yonan washed his hands three times a day. Why three times, he never told anyone.

Whose the child was, Yisi for nineteen years never spoke of to anyone. Jingjing from childhood called him Pa, called his first wife Ma, and never once asked. In the first year of Zhide Yonan asked of his own accord to travel east, and came back east from the Patriarchate with Jingjing's party. All along the road he watched this ten-year-old child writing under the lamp, and said to him: the way you write looks like someone.

---

On the night of the eighteenth of the twelfth month of the first year of Yongtai, Yonan stood at the monastery gate until the second watch.

The side courtyard had long since gone quiet, and the ward too; the snow in the streets had not yet all melted, and the moon shone on the snow and made the ward walls white. Yonan stood until his legs were stiff before he remembered he ought to go back. He bent, lifted the paper cover, and looked at the oil dish. There was still plenty of oil, but the flame was leaning a little. He wanted to trim the wick, and the wind blew out the slow-match in his hand. He turned his back and shielded it from the wind with his body and blew once; it didn't catch. He blew again; still it didn't catch.

There were footsteps behind him. It was Jingjing, come out from the kitchen in the back courtyard, rice water on his sleeve, an empty wooden bucket in his hand. All evening he had been clearing up what was left from Pa's wedding, and had soaked in the kitchen the rice for tomorrow's gruel shed at the West Market, and only now come out. He saw Yonan crouched before the stone block guarding the flame, set down the bucket, came over, crouched too, cupped both hands round the slow-match, and said, "Elder, the wind's strong at night, and your hands are frozen stiff; you won't get it to catch. You shield the wind for me, and I'll do it."

The lamplight shone up from below on those black eyes. Yonan handed him the slow-match. Jingjing blew it alight, trimmed the wick straight, put the paper cover back, and set it on very straight.
