# Volume Ten　Huichang

## Chapter 58　Burying the Stele

> The beam of the Law, bent for a while, rose higher; the stone of the Way, tilted for a time, stood upright again.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

Early the next morning, An Yan said it.

He said it in the kitchen. In the kitchen sat Jingfu, Salang, Lü Shao, and the two old Persian monks. Xiaoman was feeding the stove, his whole face grey with ash.

"Let's bury it," said An Yan.

No one asked bury what.

The two old Persian monks looked at each other and said nothing. The elder said something in Syriac, very fast, and the younger nodded.

Salang said: "Bury it where?"

"Right beside it," said An Yan. "Dig a pit, and let it lie down."

"How heavy is it?"

An Yan thought. He had never thought about that. The year the stele was raised he had been ten, squatting on top of the wall, and had seen only dozens of men hauling on ropes, chanting as they hauled, raising the stele upright a little at a time. He had not counted how many.

"Heavy," he said.

Salang nodded, and asked no more. He reckoned in his head, came to a number, and did not say it.

The one who had not yet spoken was Jingfu.

He sat on the bench with his two hands on his knees, looking at the fire in the stove. Xiaoman put another stick into the stove, and the fire rose with a *whoosh*, reddening half his face.

"In the county's register," said Jingfu, "it says: stele, one."

The kitchen went still.

"Let it say so," said An Yan.

"When the county comes again to take things in," said Jingfu, "they'll count this stele."

"Then we say it fell."

Jingfu said nothing.

An Yan looked at him, looked a while, and understood. He opened his mouth, and shut it again.

Jingfu thought of his master. His master had told it once: when he was ten, in Lingzhou, he had broken wind at table and said it was shameful, and his Pa had said, *what shames a man is a lie, not wind.* When his master had finished telling it, he had said one thing more: in all his life Pa did everything—under Lord Director Guo's command, in Chang'an, at court—except tell a lie.

Jingfu lifted his hands from his knees, and put them down again.

"We bury it," he said. "When the county comes to ask, we say we buried it."

An Yan looked at him. "Then why bury it at all?"

Jingfu thought and said: "Buried, it won't have to shore up a canal bank."

Salang stood up.

"I'll go and find a spade," he said.

---

Before the pit was dug, Lü Shao wanted to take a rubbing.

He said that of the characters his grandfather had written, the family had kept not a single one. Once the stele was buried, they would never be seen again.

That day he did not go to the Imperial Library; he sent word that he was ill. He brought from home a big roll of paper, an ink jar, and two rubbing pads. The pads were cotton wadding bound up in cloth, one large and one small; gripped in his hands, they were like two fists.

First he drew a bucket of well water and brushed the face of the stele wet. Then he laid the paper on, sheet by sheet, smoothed it flat, and brushed it with a palm-fibre brush from the middle outward, brushing it till the paper clung to the stone, clung without a single bubble. When the paper was half-dry, he took up a pad, dipped it in ink, and patted it lightly on the paper.

Pat, pat, pat.

Pat after pat, as if patting a child to sleep.

Slowly the paper darkened. What went black was the flat of the stone; the sunken characters stayed white on the paper. Character by character they appeared, the horizontals horizontal, the verticals vertical, the dots dots.

Lü Shao patted very slowly. When he came to a character, he would stop a moment, look at it, and go on patting.

An Yan stood behind him watching. After a long while An Yan said: "You're patting too slow."

"Mm," said Lü Shao.

"Patting like that," said An Yan, "you won't be done by dark."

"Mm," said Lü Shao.

He was still as slow.

That day he rubbed the top half of the face. The second day, the bottom half. The third day, the Syriac on the two sides. The Syriac he could not read; while he rubbed, he asked An Yan what this line was. An Yan said: a name. He asked again, and that line? An Yan said: a name too. When he asked again, An Yan said: they're all names, just rub.

On the evening of the third day, the rubbing was done.

Lü Shao peeled the rubbed sheets off one by one, stacked them in order, rolled them up, and tied them with a thin cord. Rolled up, it was only as thick as an arm.

An Yan watched him tie the cord, and suddenly said: "Any paper left?"

"Two sheets."

"Take one more, a small one," said An Yan.

He went to the side of the stele, bent down, reached out a finger, and searched among the lines of Syriac letters. After a while his finger stopped on one line.

"This line," he said.

Lü Shao asked: "Who is this line?"

"The Great Virtue Yi," said An Yan. "The Master's grand-teacher."

Lü Shao cut the remaining sheet in half, brushed on water, and pasted it on that line. This time he patted still more slowly. When he had finished he peeled it off; on the paper was a line of white letters, curling, running top to bottom, like a thin river.

An Yan took it and held it up to the sky's light a while. He could not read these letters. Jingjing had taken his hand and run it over them once; he remembered only which line, not how to read it.

He folded the paper twice, into a small square, and gave it to Jingfu.

"You keep it," he said.

Jingfu took it and tucked it into his coat.

The two old Persian monks came over too, and searched on the other side. They found two names, and read them aloud in Syriac, very softly. When the elder had read them, he read them again; then he shook his head and said one thing to Lü Shao in Tang speech.

"No need to rub," he said. "Remember."

Lü Shao stood before the stele a while with the roll of paper in his arms. Then he put the roll under his arm and said to Salang: "Dig."

---

The pit was dug at night.

By day, the ward would hear it, would see it. At night the ward gate was shut, there was no one in the street; only the warden on his round, twice a night.

Besides a few of the monastery's young monks, the diggers were two men Salang had found.

Salang had come to know those two at the entrance of the West Market. They had been monks in a temple south of the city, returned to lay life in the fifth month, the green stubble on their heads only half an inch long. They had no village to go back to, and waited at the entrance of the West Market to be hired; they had waited three months, and few had hired them. When Salang carried goods in the West Market, he had carried for them a few times.

Salang told them what was to be done. They listened, and did not ask why; they asked only one thing: how many nights?

Salang said three.

They said: all right.

Salang asked how much money they wanted. The elder of the two shook his head and said: "Our temple's stele—nobody buried it."

That night, they came.

---

The pit was dug on the east side of the stele.

First Salang took a cord and measured from the foot of the stele to its top, found its length, and paced out a rectangle on the ground, a stretch longer than the stele, a stretch wider. He scored the lines in the ground with the tip of the spade, and when he had scored them, spat in his palm and dug the first spadeful.

The earth was hard. The ground of the front courtyard had been trodden by people for more than sixty years, trodden like stone; one spade-thrust took up only a thin skin. Salang changed to a mattock, and first broke, then shovelled.

Break, shovel, break, shovel.

The earth they dug out went into baskets and was carried, basket by basket, to the back courtyard, and tipped onto the vegetable beds and spread flat. Xiaoman's bed of ginger got a layer of new earth over it, until it could not be seen. Xiaoman was grieved, and squatted at the edge of the bed wanting to dig the ginger out. Jingfu said: leave it buried, ginger doesn't mind being buried.

The first night, they dug down two feet.

The second night, they dug as deep as a man. When they were a man deep, they dug out a broken tile, blue-grey, with half a lotus pattern on it. An Yan took it and looked and said: this is old, older than the stele, probably from the temple that was here before. He laid the shard at the edge of the pit, and did not throw it away.

On his round the warden passed outside the monastery wall both times. On the second night, he stopped a while before the monastery gate. Inside the wall, the sound of digging stopped. After a while, outside the wall, the lantern moved on. Inside the wall, the digging began again.

On the third night, the pit was finished. Deeper than a man by half again, its floor shovelled flat, its four walls shovelled straight. Salang jumped down, lay down in the bottom a moment, measured, climbed up, and said: "It's enough."

---

The stele was laid down in the second half of the third night.

First they wrapped it. From the wooden chest for giving clothes An Yan brought out several bolts of hemp cloth, which the monastery had meant for making winter clothes for the poor and had not yet had time to cut. He and Lü Shao unrolled them one bolt after another and wound them round from the top of the stele downwards. First the face, then the two sides, three layers. By the end, even the cross at the top of the stele had been wrapped in; all that could be seen was a long white shape, standing under the moon.

Then the ropes.

Salang had found every rope in the monastery—the well rope, the washing line, the ropes for tying firewood, old halters from the stable—and joined them together; he chose the two thickest, tied them at the top of the stele, wound them three times round, and knotted them fast. The other ends were drawn to the west side of the stele, and a dozen or more people spread out along them, gripping.

Next, at the foot of the stele, on its east side, the side toward the pit, they hollowed out the ground a little at a time. The stele was set in its base, and the base was buried in the earth. Salang jumped into the pit with the mattock and dug inward along the base. At each stroke, the people above called out whether the stele had moved.

At the seventh stroke, the stele moved.

Very slightly, very slowly, it leaned a little to the east.

"Hold it!" Salang shouted.

The dozen-odd people on the west side leaned back together, the ropes went taut and creaked. The stele stopped, slanting, neither falling further nor standing back up.

Salang climbed up out of the pit, went to the very front of the rope, wound it twice round his arm, and gripped it.

"Ease off a little," he said. "Ease off slow."

Bit by bit they let it out.

Bit by bit the stele went down toward the east.

It went down very slowly. The moon shone on it; the white hemp cloth glimmered in the moonlight, like a man slowly lying down. When the foot of the stele came out of its base there was a *crack*, like a bone slipping its joint. Everyone stopped, not daring to breathe.

Salang said: "Keep easing."

They kept easing.

The lower the stele went, the faster it went, until at the end the ropes could not hold it, and shot out of the dozen hands, scorching their palms. Salang did not let go. The rope dragged him forward several steps, his heels scoring two furrows in the ground, dragged him right to the edge of the pit before he stood fast.

The stele fell into the pit.

The sound was not loud: a dull thud. The ground shivered underfoot, and those standing at the pit's edge felt the soles of their feet go numb.

Then there was no sound.

Salang let go, and looked down at his arm. The rope had cut two purple weals into it; one had broken the skin and was seeping blood. He pulled his sleeve down over it.

---

They gathered round the edge of the pit and looked down.

The stele lay at the bottom of the pit, face up, wrapped in white hemp, unmoving. The pit was too deep; the moonlight did not reach the bottom, only halfway. Looking down, all they could see was a long white shape, lying flat.

No one spoke.

The two old Persian monks knelt and made the sign of the cross at the edge of the pit. The monastery's young monks knelt too. The two monks who had returned to lay life stood behind and looked, and they too put their palms together and bowed their heads.

An Yan did not kneel. From his belt he drew the cloth he wiped the stele with, gripped it in his hand, gripped it a while, and threw it into the pit. The cloth drifted down, light as anything, landed on the stele, and could not be seen.

Then he fetched the wooden board from the gallery. He did not take the mallet; with his finger he knocked on the board three times.

There was no sound.

Jingfu stood at the edge of the pit. He wanted to say a few words, thought a long time, and could think of nothing. He thought of his master, of his master's Pa, of that old man with the scar on his brow whom he had never seen, standing before this stele, looking at it a long time, and saying not a word.

He said nothing either.

He bent down, scooped up a double handful of earth, and scattered it in.

---

Filling was faster than digging.

The earth on the vegetable beds of the back courtyard was carried back basket by basket and tipped into the pit. The first few baskets landed on the hemp with a rustling patter. Later, when the earth was thick, it could not be heard.

Xiaoman helped too. He was small and could not lift a basket, so he scooped with his two hands and scattered handful after handful into the pit. He scattered a handful, ran back, scooped another.

When it was half filled, the stele could no longer be seen.

When it was nearly full, Salang took the rammer and rammed it layer by layer. He rammed very lightly, for fear of the noise. Ram a layer, fill a layer, ram another.

Near dawn, the pit was filled level.

In the middle of the front courtyard, where the stele had stood, there was only a stretch of new earth, darker than the ground beside it, damper. The base was still where it had been, buried in the earth, only an edge of it showing.

Xiaoman, squatting at the edge of the new earth, suddenly called out: "Master, here!"

Jingfu went over to look.

At the edge of the new earth a corner of hemp cloth showed. It was the outermost layer from the wrapping; a length had been left over at the corner, and it had not been pressed down in the filling, and had worked its way out through a crack in the earth. It was only as big as a palm, white, smeared with earth.

Xiaoman reached out to push it back into the soil.

"Leave it," said Jingfu.

Xiaoman looked up at him.

Jingfu did not explain. Xiaoman looked at him a while, and drew his hand back.

The sky in the east was already pale. In the ward the cocks crowed for the first time. The two monks who had returned to lay life went out by the back door with their spades on their shoulders, and took leave of no one. The monastery's young monks went back to their cells. Salang sat down against a gallery pillar and shut his eyes.

An Yan stood at the edge of the new earth, stood a long time.

Later he went and took down the wooden board from the gallery, took up the mallet, and struck it.

*Bang*, *bang*, *bang*.

It was day. The dew came down and wetted that corner of hemp; where it was wet, its colour went a shade darker.
