# Volume Seven　A-Lian

## Chapter 44　Hnanisho

> To be upright and great yet humble, single-minded and still yet forgiving, to save the many from suffering with a wide compassion, to give freely so that all living things are covered—this is the great plan of our practice, the ladder by which we draw others up.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

The name Ningshu was the Chang'an monastery's translation. His own name was Syriac; said aloud it sounded like "Hnanisho," and meant "the grace of Jesus." In the ninth year of Dali, when the Patriarchate sent word of a death—Patriarch Jacob had died, and the newly made patriarch was called by this name—Jingjing took the letter in the west wing and read the name over several times, wanting to match it with two Tang characters; matching and rematching, he took the sound of its latter part and wrote it *Ningshu*. He took it by sound, but the characters were Tang characters: *ning shu*, rather forgive. Babai said these two characters were good; Azarel asked what they meant, Jingjing translated, and Azarel said if the Patriarch knew, he would probably say good too.

This, Hnanisho himself never knew. In all his life he never learned one Tang character; Tang speech he had only seen a few phrases of in letters from Chang'an, which Yisi had spelled out by sound in the midst of the Syriac, like a few grains of sand in a cake.

---

In the spring of the twelfth year of Dali, the letters from Chang'an reached Baghdad.

Baghdad was a new city, built by the Caliph al-Mansur, built in a circle, the walls round, one inside another, and in the centre of the city the Caliph's palace and the great house of prayer of the Tazi; four gates on four sides, four great streets running from the gates straight to the centre, straight as stretched cords. Outside the city, along the Tigris, markets, workshops, landing-stages and dwellings had grown up patch after patch, larger than the city itself. Al-Mansur had been dead more than a year, and sitting in the palace now was his son al-Mahdi. The Patriarchate's old courtyard was still at Ctesiphon, but the Patriarch lived more than half the year in Baghdad, in a borrowed courtyard on the east bank of the river; the courtyard was not large, the walls were new, the well was newly dug too, and its water had a taste of earth.

Patriarch Hnanisho was past seventy that year, his eyes bad and his legs bad too. When he first came to live here, he once went out alone to the river, and walking and walking came to the gate of a house of prayer of the Tazi, and was courteously led back. After that he hardly went out.

The letters from Chang'an Isaac brought back. Isaac had reached Chang'an in the spring of last year and stayed there half a year, and in the eighth month of the eleventh year of Dali went out of the Kaiyuan Gate with a company of Sogdian merchants going north, the two letters from Chang'an in his coat. He went by the Uighur road, past Beiting, past Suyab, over the mountains, past Balkh, past Merv, travelled more than seven months, and when he reached Baghdad he was a size thinner, his face sunburned the colour of a Chang'an sesame cake; he had learned a few dozen phrases of Tang, and learned to eat vinegared celery too, at Yisi's house. As soon as he came into the courtyard he went to see the Patriarch and handed over the oilcloth packet, and when he had handed it over the Patriarch asked him how Chang'an was; he opened his mouth, and the first thing that came out was Tang: he said *mo pa*, "grind and crawl," and when he had said it stood stunned himself; Elder Ishaq had taught him that, saying it meant "don't be afraid"; he had practised it all the way, and when he got to Baghdad it was still "grind and crawl." He wiped his mouth and said: Priest Yisi charged him, on seeing the Patriarch, to have the letters read first, and when they were read, then he was to speak.

---

The letters were read to him by the secretary at the Patriarch's side. Letters from Chang'an went as a rule first to the secretary, who opened them, copied a file copy, and then read them to the Patriarch. This secretary was twenty or so, wrote Syriac well and read letters clearly, but had never travelled far; where Chang'an was, he had seen only on a parchment map, a dot of ink at the far east. Isaac sat beside him, and where he could not read something, read it for him.

The first letter was Yisi's. The letters big and clumsy, slanting right, only a few words to a line, scraped in several places, the parchment scraped thin and letting through the light. The secretary read very slowly, and at some words had to hold the letter to the window to make them out. Hnanisho sat in an old wooden chair and listened.

The letter said Yonan had married, in the back courtyard of the Chang'an monastery, on the twenty-sixth of the sixth month; the Patriarch's letter had arrived in the second month, and four months after it arrived they married; Yonan had said he wanted to wait for the locust flowers. Hnanisho nodded. The letter said that on the third day after the wedding Yonan died, under the locust tree at the monastery gate, by the well; he had washed his hands, and reached out to take a bamboo stylus, and his foot slipped, and Narsai reached out to catch him, and Narsai had a pen-knife in his hand. Hnanisho's head did not nod again. The secretary stopped and looked at him; Hnanisho raised his hand and had him read on.

The letter wrote of the county, wrote "those who offend among their own kind shall each be judged by their own custom," wrote of accident, wrote of redemption copper; those few Tang phrases Yisi had spelled out by sound, and the secretary read them stumbling, as if with stones in his mouth; Isaac, listening beside him, set the sounds right for him and translated them one by one: own custom—each house's rules; accident—not on purpose; redemption copper—to pay with copper in place of it. The letter wrote that Yonan's woman said she would not accept it, wrote what she said—he owes me one cake; when Isaac had translated it, the secretary raised his head and asked what owing a cake meant; Isaac said it meant owing one cake. The secretary said: then why not accept the copper? Isaac said he didn't know either; in Chang'an he had eaten the cakes that woman baked; they were good cakes. The letter wrote of how Chang'an had deliberated, wrote of suspension, of not cutting off, of gathering the dead, of sending orphans, of living; wrote that Narsai had gathered twenty-three dead in a month, and there were Tang characters on every stake. The last line was: "What the Patriarch granted was not wrong. The wrong is in Chang'an. Chang'an is in my charge. How it is to be dealt with, I will abide by the Patriarch."

When the secretary had finished reading he put the letter down. Outside the window was the river, boats on the river, boatmen calling, one long, one short.

Hnanisho asked what Yonan said before he died—did the letter say? The secretary took up the letter again, searched a while, found it, and said it gave two sayings: one in Syriac, which he said to Narsai: it was you who caught me. Hnanisho heard it, and after a while said: read it again. The secretary read it again: it was you who caught me. Hnanisho said: and the other? The secretary said there was another; Priest Yisi had spelled it by the sound of Tang; he could read it out, but did not know what it meant: nine sounds, broken in two, two and then seven.

Hnanisho said: read it. So the secretary read it, letter by letter, and what came out was a string of sounds no one could understand, read very awkwardly, very slowly. Hnanisho listened with his eyes closed, and when it was done opened his eyes and looked at Isaac. Isaac's face reddened, and he said this one he understood; he had heard people say it in Chang'an: the first two were *mian duo*, lot of dough—*mian* was the dough for making cakes, *duo* was much; the last seven were *she can't knead it all by herself*: *she*, that woman; *knead*, kneading dough.

Hnanisho sat there; the boatmen on the river called twice before he said: "I thought that dying, he would say a prayer."

Isaac said he had thought so too. Hnanisho shook his head and said: this is harder to say than a prayer; he had lived seventy-odd years and never heard a dying man say a thing like that: speaking of the dough at home, afraid she couldn't knead it all by herself. He said that when Yonan was young in the monastery he was like this: when he spoke to people he said the important thing first, and when the important thing was said he said no more. As he said these things his hands rested on his knees, the backs of them covered in spots, the fingers tapping his knee little by little.

When the letter was read, Hnanisho had Isaac sit down and tell it slowly. So Isaac sat at the Patriarch's feet and told it from the beginning. He said Chang'an was vast: over a hundred wards, a single ward bigger than a stretch of villages outside Baghdad; the wards had ward walls, and at dark a drum was beaten, and when the drum was done the ward gates shut and no one was allowed in the streets; the first night he didn't know, and was walking in the street, and the men of the patrol post at the street corner held him, and early next morning it was Jingjing who went to the post and fetched him back. He said the Chang'an monastery was in a ward on the west side of the city; the hall was newly built, five bays; at the monastery gate there was a very old tree, and under the tree a well; on a stone block to the left of the monastery gate an oil lamp was lit, lit every day; in the half year he was there it was Yonan who went to light it every evening, and when it was lit stood under the lamp looking toward the ward gate, looked till the ward drum was done before he went in; every day at first light Yonan gave out cakes to the poor under that tree, and when they were given out washed his hands at the well, washed them very slowly; he had asked Yonan why, and Yonan said it was habit. He said that morning he heard the child shout in the back courtyard and ran out with Elder Ishaq; Ishaq couldn't run, so he held him up running; when they reached the well the man was already past help; he ran back for the medicine box, and when the box came it was no longer needed. He said Priest Yisi was fifty, short, broad-shouldered, with a scar over his left brow; every day he split firewood in the back courtyard, and when the wood was split made up medicines at the medicine chest, and when the medicines were made taught that little son of his his letters; the little son was nine, his Tang name Lingbao, his Syriac name Adam too, the same name as his brother. He said Priest Yisi's wife was a Tang, who kept the accounts of the gruel kitchen at the West Market, and had a very sharp tongue; Elder Ishaq was afraid of her.

Hnanisho listened and did not interrupt once; when it came to the well his fingers paused on his knee; when it came to the little son being called Adam too, he asked why the same name as his brother; Isaac said his brother gave it him, saying: the same as me. Hnanisho nodded. When Isaac had finished, Hnanisho asked him whether the people of Chang'an believed. Isaac thought and said he couldn't really say; of those who came to the gruel kitchen for gruel, some believed and some didn't; believing or not they took the gruel, and when they'd taken it said thank you; some said thanks to Alaha, some said thanks to the *hu* master, some said thanks to the Bodhisattva, and the deacon didn't correct any of them; he said whomever they thanked, it was thanks. When Hnanisho heard this he smiled and said that when he was young in the monastery he had thought so too, and later dared not think so; the people of Chang'an dared.

---

The second letter was from the elders.

The secretary read the beginning: the canons, the order, Chang'an nearly thirty years without a bishop, deacons who could not be ordained, priests with no one to ordain them, those in Chang'an who could break the bread countable on one hand. Hnanisho listened, nodding; these things he had heard before; in the old days when Shapur was alive the letters from Chang'an had said them, and every time Shapur said: when the road is open, then we'll see; and then Shapur died, and the road had never opened. When the secretary came to the latter half, to "This year in Chang'an a man died; he slipped; the one who caught him had a knife in his hand," Hnanisho raised his hand, had him stop, and said: give me the letter.

The secretary passed it over. Hnanisho's eyes could no longer see writing well; he held the sheet to the window, held it right up to his nose, very close, and looked at it line by line; what he looked at was the shape of the writing; the words he could not make out, but the shape he could. When he had looked, he sent the secretary to the cupboard to find the Chang'an letter of the year before last, the one in which Yonan asked to be released from his vow. The secretary searched and found it, and Hnanisho had him turn to the middle passage, the passage that year at whose "not to give thought to me" he had put the letter down and walked once round the courtyard. He held the two sheets side by side to the window, looking once at one and once at the other.

He said they were written by one man. The secretary leaned over to look, looked a while, and said they were somewhat alike: the strokes all turned inward at their ends rather than flicking out. Hnanisho said: it's one man; that year all of Yonan's letter was copied by this man; the pen is the same pen; that year the middle passage, the line "not to give thought," was this man's own words; and this year's letter, the word "slipped," the line "the one who caught him had a knife in his hand"—the words too are the same kind of words; when he comes to the important place, the pen has to pause a moment, and at that pause there's a little more ink. He said he had said that year: I know his words; and this year he had written again, written of Chang'an's affairs; but when he came to write that this year a man had died in Chang'an, what he wrote was: he slipped; the one who caught him had a knife in his hand; he did not write what the dead man was to him.

The secretary turned to the end of the letter and read the signatures: Azarel, Marya, Samaan, Ishaq, Babai, Gabriel, Sabrisho, Haddad, Yazdbozid, and last, written by Adam, Deacon of Charity. Isaac beside him said Adam was Jingjing; the Tang called him Jingjing; in the Chang'an monastery the translating, the charity, the accounts, the letters, were all his; in the half year he lived in Chang'an he had seen him: tall, pale, and wouldn't drink cold water. Hnanisho heard it and said: thirty years old, and still a deacon for the time being. Isaac said yes; no one to lay on hands.

---

That spring Hnanisho was ill a while, coughing, and could not sleep at night. The one who came to treat him was Isa, keeper of medicines in the Caliph's palace.

Isa was of the Patriarchate's people; he came up from keeping a medicine shop, had sold medicines half his life in the city of Baghdad, and people called him Isa of the medicine shop; and in the end the medicine shop opened its way into the palace; all the medicines the Caliph al-Mahdi took he made up; he was called Abu Quraysh, and went into the palace more often than the vizier. He was a little over sixty, short, fat, his hands very soft; when he felt a pulse he laid three fingers on as if laying them on a lump of dough. He felt the Patriarch's pulse, looked at his tongue, said it was age, phlegm in the lungs, nothing serious, made up a prescription and told the secretary to fetch it from his shop, and said too that for the next few days he must not look at letters; looking at letters made his eyes ache, and when his eyes ached he could not sleep. Hnanisho said letters he could not not look at: the letters from Chang'an.

Isa, hearing the word Chang'an, raised his head and said that the year before last, when he asked Isaac to go east, he had put one question: what did the Tang over there call myrrh? Isaac came back and brought back that deacon's words: the official herbal didn't have it; the *hu* merchants of the West Market called it *moyao*. Isa said he had laughed when he heard it: *moyao, moyao*—that was our own Syriac sound, *mura*; the *hu* merchants said it awry and it came out *moyao*; the Tang name and the *hu* name, it turned out, were one sound. He said Isaac had said too that the deacon of the Chang'an monastery had translated the names of the medicines in the medicine chest into Tang one by one; on the labels, under the Syriac, he wrote the Tang, and under the Tang a line of small writing saying what the Tang called this medicine and whether the official herbal had it, and where it hadn't, wrote it as the *hu* merchants said it; written plain like that, from now on whoever made up a prescription would not get it wrong. In early years the Chang'an monastery had nearly given a child the wrong medicine because the writing on a label couldn't be read; it was the deacon's aunt who spotted it; after that the deacon changed every label and redid the medicine register. Isa said that in a lifetime of making up medicines what he feared most was labels; one character wrong on a label, and someone was dead.

Hnanisho said the one who wrote this letter was that deacon.

Isa heard it, put down the packet of medicine in his hand, and said: if the Patriarch asked him—he was a maker of medicines, he didn't understand canons; but he knew one thing: a man willing to change labels one by one, put him in charge of people, and he won't go wrong; people are like medicines; call them by the wrong name, and they'll be given the wrong things. When he had said it he laughed a little himself and said: that's medicine-shop talk; the Patriarch mustn't take it seriously.

Hnanisho did not laugh, and thought over these words all night.

---

The next day Hnanisho had the secretary find Yonan's letter, Yisi's letter, the elders' letter, and the file copy of his own reply to Yonan two years before, spread them on his knees, and had the secretary read them from the beginning, one letter and then the next. When they came to the file copy of his own reply, to the line *Alaha pitieth our weakness; for he knoweth our frame; he remembereth that we are dust*, he had him stop. The secretary stopped. Hnanisho sat in the old wooden chair, his two hands resting on his knees, looking out of the window, looked a long while, and said: "I pitied one man's weakness, and he had only three days."

The secretary waited for him to go on, and he did not go on. Isaac, beside him, said: Patriarch, he slipped. Hnanisho said: I know; he slipped, and the one who caught him meant well too; a man who means well, holding a knife—that's Chang'an's affair, and mine too; if I had not granted it, he would be giving out cakes at the Chang'an monastery gate today. He said he did not regret granting it.

That evening he dictated a reply; the secretary wrote; Isaac sat beside them, and where Tang names had to be written, said the sounds for him.

The reply was not long. He said that in the Chang'an matter Yisi had judged rightly, judged more rightly than what the canons had written; the canons had no article for catching a man and bringing about his death, and he would not add one either; let those who came after see to it themselves. He said Narsai was not to be cut off; his suspension was to last until the day this letter reached Chang'an, and on that day let him receive the Body; if he was still willing to gather the dead, let him go on gathering; for each one he gathered, the Chang'an monastery would pray once for that one, and once for Narsai. He sent greetings of peace to sister A-Lian: she was right not to accept the copper; that one cake, let her settle with Yonan herself.

Then he spoke of Chang'an's affairs. He said Chang'an needed a bishop; he knew it, and the Patriarchate knew it; but the road to the east was so far that of the Patriarchate's bishops none was willing to go, and those who were willing he was not easy about; he himself was old, and by the look of it would not live many years; if he made a bishop today and sent him, and the one sent was halfway there when he died, whether the new patriarch would recognize this man no one knew, and when that man reached Chang'an, Chang'an would not know whether to recognize him either. He said that rather than have Chang'an wait for a bishop who might arrive who knew when, better to let the place above Chang'an have a name in it first. He said the canons had no such standing; he would borrow an old title for him: in former times, in places so far that bishops could not reach them, the old men of the Patriarchate called the one who led there *papash*, the father of the multitude; he would use that, and write *papash* of Sinistan—Sinistan being what the westerners called that great expanse of the east, larger than Chang'an; this standing carried no laying on of hands, no breaking of the bread, but the monks and the faithful of Chang'an would all obey him, and he would obey only the Patriarchate; when one day a bishop reached Chang'an, or someone from Chang'an reached the Patriarchate, then hands would be laid on him. He said this standing was to be given to the deacon who wrote the letter, Adam. He said too: Yisi had been thirty-odd years in Chang'an and Superintendent more than ten; give him the standing of chorepiscopus, to help the deacon, and when one day a bishop arrived, hands would be laid on both together.

When the secretary had written this far, he stopped his pen and asked the Patriarch: this standing of *papash* had always been given to men of years; the deacon was only thirty. Hnanisho said: then let him come to years a little early. He thought a moment and said again: the boy said in his letter not to give thought to him; this time I will not give thought to him; I will send him to take charge of a whole city of people, and a whole city of people will need him to give thought to them, and he'll have no time to say not to give thought to me.

Last, he had the secretary add a paragraph, about something Isaac had seen in Chang'an. Isaac said the hall of the Chang'an monastery was newly built, and when it was being built they swept out from under the old foundations a stone with Tang characters carved on it, carved more than a hundred years ago in the time of the Emperor Taizong, saying that in such a year and month the Persian monk Alopen came to Chang'an bringing scriptures and images, and the emperor had a monastery built in the Yining Ward; that stone now leaned against the foot of the back wall of the hall, and people of Chang'an passing by all stopped to look at it, and when they had looked, knew this monastery was permitted by the emperor. Hnanisho had the secretary write: if the people of Chang'an wished to carve a stone to record events, to record what had happened since Alopen, it was permitted; if they wished to write the Patriarchate's name on the stone, to write the name of the reigning patriarch, that too was permitted.

When the secretary had written it, he asked the Patriarch whether to write the Patriarch's personal name. Hnanisho smiled; in four years at his side the secretary had seldom seen him smile. He said: write it; while he lived he had never been to the east; let his name make the journey; that would be good.

When the reply was written, the secretary guided his hand to sign his name at the end. He could no longer see writing clearly; bent close under the lamp, he signed very slowly, and when he had signed, wrote beneath his name one more word, meaning "granted," and that stroke went crooked, crooked right out beyond the line. He felt the sheet and asked whether it had gone crooked; the secretary said no. He said: this letter, send it with the fastest caravan, by the Uighur road, and make another copy for the sea road. The secretary said even the fastest would take most of a year. Hnanisho said: then most of a year.

---

That reply reached Chang'an in the spring of the thirteenth year of Dali.

The day it came was a Lord's Day. When worship was over Elder Azarel did not let everyone go, but had Jingjing read the letter to all; the letter was opened, Azarel glanced at its beginning, then took the letter back and said: this one Jingjing won't read; he would. Azarel's Syriac was slow, sentence by sentence; he read a sentence, and Marya in his hoarse voice put it into Tang for the few dozen Tang believers in the hall. In the last row sat Shiyiniang, Lingbao, and A-Lian. That winter A-Lian's cough had grown worse; Shiyiniang had gone to the north end of the ward to fetch her, and brought her in on her arm.

When Azarel came to Narsai, to the suspension lasting until the day this letter reached Chang'an, everyone in the hall looked toward the door. Narsai was not in the hall; he did not come into the hall; every Lord's Day he stood under the gallery outside the hall door, stood till worship was over, and then pushed his cart out. This day too he stood there; when Azarel read his name, he stood under the gallery and did not move. When Azarel read that he was to receive the Body, he turned round, facing the courtyard, his back to the hall door; when Marya put it into Tang he put it slowly, and when it was put, the hall was very quiet; only A-Lian could be heard coughing twice in the last row, and Shiyiniang patting her back.

Azarel read on. When he came to *papash*, to Adam, he paused a moment, raised his head, and glanced toward Jingjing, and bent his head to read again. Jingjing stood half a pace behind him, still holding the scroll used at worship, the scroll rolled up; he gripped it, gripped it till the wooden roller grew warm. When Marya put it into Tang, he thought a while and found no fit word, so translated it by the letter, saying: father of the monks of Chang'an. Of the few dozen Tang believers in the hall, someone said softly: isn't that the great abbot of Chang'an? Granny He's grandson's wife, standing in the middle with her child in her arms, said: bigger than a great abbot. Jingjing heard, his face went hot a while, and he did not turn his head.

When Azarel came to Yisi, chorepiscopus, Yisi was on the other side of the hall, kneeling where he always knelt; when he had heard it, he kept his head bowed, and after a while made the sign of the cross on his breast. Shiyiniang in the last row looked at his back, looked a while, turned her head and said something to A-Lian; A-Lian nodded and coughed once, and did not cough again.

When he came to the last paragraph—carving a stone to record events, writing the Patriarch's name—when Azarel had read it and Marya translated it, someone in the hall said "Ah"; it was Samaan. Samaan stood in the front row, took his two hands out of his sleeves and folded them back in, and said: carving stones—he'd never heard of such a thing; the canons don't have it either; and the Patriarchate has gone and granted it first. Azarel said: the Patriarch granted it; record it. So Samaan opened his account book and wrote a line.

That night Jingjing did not sleep. He went out of the monastery gate; by then the ward gates were shut, and he could not leave the Yining Ward, so he walked within the ward, walked to the north end of the ward, to the gate of that small courtyard; the courtyard gate was shut, no lamp inside; A-Lian had long gone to sleep; he could only hear her coughing, a few coughs, a pause, a few more coughs. He stood a while at the gate, did not knock, and walked back, back to the monastery gate, and stopped before the stone block. The lamp was still burning; the oil in the bronze dish was nearly dry; he went back for the oil jug, came out, and filled it.

Yisi had not slept either; he sat under the jujube tree at the side courtyard door, saw him come back with the oil jug, and called him. Jingjing went over and sat on the low stool beside him, and spoke first: he said he didn't want this standing; he was thirty-two, and no one had ever laid hands on him; a deacon for the time being, and the people of Chang'an to call him father of the monks; Elder Azarel past seventy, Elder Marya preached hoarse—and they to come under him instead; it wasn't fitting. Yisi heard him out, did not try to persuade him, but spoke of his uncle: when your uncle became Archdeacon he too was just past thirty, and the Patriarchate's elders said it wasn't fitting too: a priest's son from Balkh, in charge of the Patriarchate's affairs; so every night your uncle wrote the next day's business item by item on a wooden board, put it under his pillow, and the next day did it item by item; he did it a few years, and no one said it wasn't fitting. Jingjing said: Uncle was Uncle. Yisi said: and you're you; the Patriarch knows your words; take charge by your words; if you take charge unfittingly, Samaan will record it, and when he's recorded enough, the Patriarchate will take it back itself. When he had said it he stood up, said he had wood to split tomorrow—a chorepiscopus has to split wood too—and went back to his room. Jingjing sat alone under the jujube tree till nearly dawn, and went back to his room to say the morning office.

Coming back from the side courtyard, before light, Jingjing did not go back to his room, but took that small lantern and went round to the back of the hall. At the foot of the back wall of the hall leaned that old stone, the one he had swept out from under the foundations ten-odd years ago when the old hall was pulled down; the stone was not large, a foot and more wide, the characters carved, buried in earth over a hundred years, mud still lodged in the strokes. He squatted, brought the lantern close, and read it character by character; at "the Persian monk Alopen" he stopped. In Alopen's time this monastery was called the Persian monastery; later the Son of Heaven issued an edict and changed it, called it the Daqin monastery; the characters on the stone had not been changed, and still said Persian. He thought of that paragraph at the end of the Patriarch's letter: carve a stone to record events, write the reigning patriarch's name—that too was permitted. He squatted there, and in his head laid out the years one by one going back, back to the ninth year of Zhenguan, back to Alopen, and then one by one going forward, to the change of name in the fourth year of Tianbao, to the fall of Chang'an, to the recovery, to his own coming back from Ctesiphon, to Yonan dying by the well, to yesterday; laid them out till the sky was light, and still had not finished. He stood up, his legs numb, leaned on the wall a while, and went back to say the morning office.

That day when the hall had emptied, Narsai was still standing under the gallery. Yisi came out of the hall door, went up to him, and said: next Lord's Day, come into the hall. Narsai looked at him, and neither agreed nor said he would not come. The next Lord's Day, Narsai came into the hall and knelt in the last row; across the aisle was where the women knelt, and A-Lian knelt at the very edge. Yisi broke the bread; Jingjing carried that glass bowl; Yisi came to the last row and put a small piece of bread in Narsai's palm; Narsai looked down at the bread, put it in his mouth, held it there, held it a long time before he swallowed. Jingjing came over with the bowl, squatted, and held the bowl to his lips; Narsai drank a mouthful and raised his head and looked at Jingjing, and the two of them, a bowl between them, looked at each other once. From Narsai's cuff slipped a piece of wood on which two Tang characters he could not yet write were drawn in charcoal; it fell to the floor; Jingjing reached out and picked it up for him and glanced at it: the two characters *cibei*, compassion, drawn crookedly; he put the piece of wood back in Narsai's hand and said nothing. Across the aisle, A-Lian kept her head bowed, a bamboo stylus gripped in her hand.

---

After Jingjing became *papash*, his days were no different from before.

He still rose before light and knelt half a pace behind Yisi to say the morning office, still translated scripture in the west wing, still went to the West Market to see to the gruel kitchen, still made up medicines at the chest, looking at every label twice. What was different was that in Samaan's account book, where before it said "the Deacon of Charity so-and-so," it now said "the *papash* so-and-so," and wrote very awkwardly; Samaan wrote it a few times and found it awkward himself, and later simply wrote only the word "Adam." When Luo Fifteen of the Xiushan Ward in Luoyang, the Kang nephew at the Lingzhou monastery, those seven households in the gully at Zhouzhi, had business and wrote letters, the wrappers no longer said "To the Great Virtue Yi, Daqin Monastery, Chang'an," but "To the Great Virtue Jingjing, Daqin Monastery, Chang'an"; every time Jingjing received one he looked at those characters a while before he opened it. At the table, when Yisi took food with his chopsticks, he served Jingjing first. Shiyiniang saw and did not remark on it; Lingbao saw and said Pa played favourites; Yisi said: your brother is father of the monks of Chang'an now; your Pa comes under him too. Lingbao said: and me? Yisi said: you come under your Ma.

A-Lian's cough did not get better that whole winter. Yisi made up medicine for her—smoked plum, apricot kernel, liquorice, boiled down to a paste—to be mixed with water and drunk; she drank it and said it wasn't bitter, and it really wasn't bitter; Yisi had put in extra honey. She still went every day to open the shop in the West Market, left as soon as the ward gate opened, came back before the gong, and when she came back, in that small courtyard at the north end of the ward, at that little oven, baked two cakes, one to eat herself and one to crumble at the courtyard gate for the sparrows. Jingjing went every few days. Once when he went, A-Lian was at the little oven simmering the pot of paste Yisi had prescribed, the paste bubbling in the pot, the whole room smelling of smoked plum and apricot kernel. She asked him what *papash* meant; she'd heard Elder Marya read it in the hall and hadn't understood; she'd understood only the word *father*. Jingjing said it meant the faithful of a whole city came under him: he saw to their births, saw to their deaths, saw to them when they had something to ask. A-Lian heard it and laughed, and said: then a whole city of people will have to call you father; don't you forget to call... When she got this far she began coughing, coughed till she bent over; Jingjing went to support her; she waved him off, and when she had done coughing drank a mouthful of hot water, and asked how Lingbao's writing was coming on. Jingjing said it was good, better than his when he was small. That half sentence she did not go on with, and he did not ask.

Ordinarily when he went, he stood at the courtyard gate and called "Lady A-Lian," and A-Lian answered and told him to come in, poured him a bowl of hot water, and the two of them sat and talked a while—about the shop, about the monastery, about how well Lingbao wrote, about whom Narsai had buried now—talked till nearly dark, and he got up and left. He called her Lady A-Lian; she called him Deacon; after he became *papash* she still called him Deacon, and he did not have her change it. Once she asked him to read her once more the line in the Patriarch's letter about her; Jingjing read it: that one cake, let her settle with Yonan herself. A-Lian heard it and laughed, laughed till she coughed, and when she had done coughing said: this patriarch's one who knows how to keep accounts. She said that one cake she had down; it was traced over with the stylus and wouldn't rub off; she'd settle it with him slowly, settle it till the day she went to see him, settle it face to face. Jingjing heard it, made no answer, and filled her bowl to the brim with hot water.

The dead Narsai gathered in those years, the people of the wards afterwards could not count. In the burial ground of the monastery outside the Kaiyuan Gate there were at first a dozen-odd mounds; later that stretch at its edge was filled row after row with wooden stakes, every stake carved with Tang characters: where there was a name, a name; where there was none, the day, the ward, the age, the clothes. His Tang characters slowly got better: at first they were like two crossed spears; in those two years, like two spears standing side by side. In the city of Chang'an, from the West Market to around the Yanping Gate, there was no one who did not know the *hu* monk who pushed a handcart; on winter mornings, when the ward guards found someone frozen to death at the foot of a wall, they did not go to report to the county but went first to the gate of the monastery in the Yining Ward and shouted for the *hu* master who gathered the dead. The year Lingbao was twelve he went with him once, and came back and told Shiyiniang: when Elder Narsai buries people he says prayers, in Syriac, and when he's done he says it once more in Tang; the Tang he taught him; what he says is: Lord, remember this person.

In the fifth month of the fourteenth year of Dali, the Emperor Daizong died, and the crown prince took the throne. After the new Son of Heaven took the throne he issued several edicts: dismissed the musicians of the Pear Garden, released the palace women, forbade the provinces to present auspicious omens any more. In the seventh month he issued another edict saying that Uighurs and the various *hu* living in the capital should each wear their own clothes and not imitate the Hua. This edict was posted on the market gate of the West Market, and the *hu* merchants of the West Market all went to look; when they had looked, some cursed, some laughed, some went home and took off their Tang turbans and put on their felt hats again. The monastery had a few *hu* believers too who had been born in Chang'an and worn Tang clothes since childhood, and now did not know what to wear, and came running to ask Jingjing. There was a passage in the edict that Official Pei's son had copied out to show Jingjing, saying that formerly the Uighurs staying in the capital were often a thousand and more, and the merchant *hu* disguised as Hua living among the people were twice as many again; some wore Hua clothes, married Tang wives and concubines, and bought fields and houses; therefore they were to wear each their own clothes. Jingjing said the edict spoke of Uighurs and the various *hu* living in the capital; you were born in Chang'an, your household registers are in Chang'an, your fathers and grandfathers for generations were in Chang'an; you are men of Chang'an; dress as before. He said it very steadily, and when he had said it went himself to the Metropolitan Prefecture and asked Official Pei's son, and came back with the same answer as he had given, and only then were those few at ease. Samaan made an entry in his account book: The *papash* Adam answered the faithful on the matter of dress, and afterwards went himself to the Metropolitan Prefecture to ask; his answer was not wrong; the order says dealings with the government go by one line only, through the Superintendent and the official surnamed Pei; this errand was without the Superintendent; recorded. Jingjing saw it and did not argue; that evening he went to find Yisi and said he should have asked Pa first. Yisi said: you're *papash* now; that article of the order will wait till someone comes from the Patriarchate to change it; until it's changed, Samaan records his, and you go your way. That evening at supper, Yisi again served him a helping first.

Li Su came once too. He had served in the Directorate of Astronomy more than ten years and was now one of its officials; he came in official robes, on horseback, dismounted at the monastery gate, came in and talked a while with Jingjing; he said the new Son of Heaven did not like talk of stars and portents, and the Directorate's days were not easy; he said he himself had leisure now, and time to read some books. When he left, he stood at the monastery gate before the stone block, looked at the lamp, and said that Elder Yonan used to look at the stars with him on the roof of the scripture room, and said the stars seen over by the Two Rivers were the same as here, only a little lower; some days ago he had measured at the Directorate, and they were a little lower; he had always meant to tell Elder Yonan, and never had the time to come. Jingjing said the Elder was buried outside the Kaiyuan Gate. Li Su said he knew; he had been; the *heart* on the grave was well carved.

---

Hnanisho died in the spring of the first year of Jianzhong. Chang'an did not know it then.

He died in the night, and when he died there was no one beside him. The next morning the secretary went to bring letters and knocked, and no one answered; he pushed the door and went in, and he was sitting in that old wooden chair, his head fallen to one side, a blanket over his knees, as if asleep. By his hand the secretary found a sheet of paper; nothing was written on it; there were only a few marks scored with a fingernail, horizontal, vertical, scored very confusedly; the secretary could not see what they were. Isaac was in the city then; he heard, and ran to look, looked at that paper and could not see what it was either; afterwards he thought: in those years the Patriarch often had him read out the Tang characters in the letters from Chang'an, and when he had read them the Patriarch would score with his fingernail on his knee, scoring the shapes of those characters; he couldn't see them, so he could only score them. Isaac thought what was scored on this paper was probably Tang characters too; which one, he didn't know. The paper was later put away in the cupboard, together with the letters from Chang'an.

With Hnanisho dead, the Patriarchate fell into disorder. Metropolitans from every region hurried to Baghdad, and when they had come, wept before the bier by day and at night shut their doors and plotted. Plotting this way and that, they came out with one man: the bishop of Beth Bgash, named Timothy. The place was in the mountains east of Mosul, small and poor, and in every faction's eyes it counted as no faction; so every faction thought he could be let sit there a while first. Timothy spoke Syriac, spoke the speech of the Tazi, spoke Greek, could dispute with the scholars of the Caliph's palace, and when the disputation was done they would invite him to dinner. He and Isa of the medicine shop were old acquaintances, and one word from Isa to al-Mahdi was worth more than ten bishops quarrelling a month.

In the days of the election there was one incident. When Timothy came to Baghdad he brought several heavy sacks, their mouths tied with hemp cord; it took two men to carry each, and when one was set on the ground it went *thud*. Some of the bishops saw and made private inquiries, and Timothy's men said: these the bishop has brought; once the matter's settled, naturally they'll be of use. And those bishops inquired no further. The matter was settled; Timothy was elected; those bishops came to see him and spoke of the sacks. Timothy had the sacks carried out, and before their eyes untied the hemp cords: in the sacks were stones, stones from the riverbank, large and small, round and flat, still with mud on them. Those bishops' faces went green. Timothy said: the office of the priesthood is not bought and sold with money.

Joseph, metropolitan of Merv, would not accept it, and with a few bishops held another council, saying Timothy's patriarchate did not count; the two sides each excommunicated the other once; afterwards Isa of the medicine shop came forward and said a few words before al-Mahdi, and only then was it put down.

---

One night Timothy was alone in the library. The library was not large; three walls were wooden shelves, and on the shelves were piled rolls of parchment and of paper: orders of past patriarchs, letters from metropolitans of every region, old scriptures carried over from Ctesiphon, some of them more than a hundred years old, the parchment gone black, the edges curled, flaking when touched. That day another letter had come from Merv, in very ugly words; he had read it, not replied, and had it put away. He could not sleep, and went through the shelves a while, and came to the compartment for the east.

On top were the letters from Chang'an, a stack, tied with a fine cord. He untied the cord and looked at them one by one. One had big clumsy letters, scraped in several places, the parchment scraped thin and letting through the light; he read it through by the lamp, and at "The wrong is in Chang'an; Chang'an is in my charge" he paused. One was in a very upright hand, the strokes turning inward at their ends; where it came to "This year in Chang'an a man died; he slipped; the one who caught him had a knife in his hand," there was a little more ink; it was signed at the end "written by Adam, deacon," and beside it was a line of small writing added by Hnanisho's secretary, saying the Patriarch knew this man's words. Below that was the file copy of Hnanisho's reply, and its last paragraph: carving a stone to record events—granted; writing the name—that too granted. Below that again was one more sheet, with nothing written on it, only a few marks scored with a fingernail.

Timothy could not see what those marks were either. He folded the letters back just as they had been, tied them, put them back in that compartment, and on the wooden shelf of that compartment scored a small mark with his fingernail, so as to find it easily later.

Then he went back to his own room, lit the lamp, spread out a sheet of paper, and took up his pen. The first letter he wrote was to Joseph of Merv.

Note: Hnanisho II (Ningshu) reigned roughly 773–780; the stele says "the Lord of the Law, the monk Ningshu, has charge of the Luminous congregation of the East"; that in the second year of Jianzhong, when the stele was set up, Chang'an still took Ningshu as the reigning Lord of the Law is generally thought by historians to mean the news had not arrived. Timothy I was elected in 780; the story of the bags of stones appears in Syriac sources (Bar Hebraeus and others); his opponents included Joseph, metropolitan of Merv, and others; details are elaborated; dates given for Hnanisho's death vary between 778 and 779/780. Abu Quraysh 'Isa, who came up from a medicine shop, was a court physician under al-Mahdi and helped Timothy to his election. The Syriac of the stele calls Jingjing (Adam) "priest, chorepiscopus, and *papash* of Sinistan," and Yisi (Yazdbozid) "priest, chorepiscopus of Kumdan"; Hnanisho's conferring of these titles here is the novel's arrangement, and the laying on of hands is deferred to a later chapter. The edict of the seventh month of the fourteenth year of Dali that "Uighurs and the various *hu* in the capital shall each wear their own clothes and not imitate the Hua" follows *Zizhi tongjian* juan 226. Li Su's entering the Directorate of Astronomy early in the Dali years follows the *Epitaph of Li Su*.
