# Volume Nine　The Translation Hall

## Chapter 52　Mi Jifen

> They keep their beards, to show that they have outward conduct; they shave the crown, to show that they have no inward passions.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

Mi Jifen was an old soldier.

How old he really was, no one at the monastery could say for certain. He could not say for certain himself. Ask him, and he would say: born in the Kaiyuan years; which year of Kaiyuan I can't remember; anyway I'm older than everybody here.

He was from the land of Mi. The land of Mi lay beside the land of Kang, a small country, so small that many in Chang'an had never heard of it. In his father's generation the king of Mi sent his father to Chang'an as a hostage—held, that is, before the Son of Heaven, as a token of loyalty. The hostage was held his whole life, and never went back. Mi Jifen was born in Chang'an, grew up in Chang'an, joined the army when he grew up, and served until his hair was white, until he was a general in the Shence Army.

He spoke Tang with a touch of *hu* in it, not heavy; you had to listen carefully to hear it. His family had been of the Luminous Teaching for several generations. His elder son was in the army; his younger son had left the household life and was at the Daqin monastery, with the religious name Siyuan.

Every Lord's Day, Mi Jifen came to the monastery.

When he was younger he came on horseback. Later he could no longer ride, and came by cart. Later still he could not sit steady in a cart either, and had his servants hold him up, one on each side. When he came he did not sit at the front, but always in the last row by the door. When others rose, he rose too, leaning on his stick; when others knelt, he could not kneel, so he laid his stick across his knees and bowed his head.

When the preaching went on long, he dozed. Halfway through a doze he would wake with a start, look about, see that no one was looking at him, and doze on.

When Jingjing preached, he never woke him.

---

The first time Mi Jifen spoke with Jingjing alone was in the eleventh year of Zhenyuan.

That day when the preaching was over and everyone had gone, he did not leave. He had his servants help him across to Jingjing, stood, breathed hard a few times, and said: "That year Luo Haoxin came to find you—it was I who sent him."

Jingjing thought a while before he remembered which year it was. Nine years ago.

"He asked me who in the city wrote Tang well," said Mi Jifen. "I said Jingjing of the Daqin monastery. I didn't say you understood the Buddha."

"I don't understand the Buddha," said Jingjing.

"I know," said Mi Jifen. "When that decree came down later, I heard of it, and felt bad about it. I meant to come and tell you so, and I've been meaning to for nine years."

Jingjing looked at him. The old man's eyes had grown cloudy, the lids drooping over most of the eye. But the little of the eye that showed still looked at you, and did not shy away.

"Lord Mi needn't feel bad," said Jingjing. "What His Majesty said wasn't all bad."

"Which part wasn't?"

"*Jingjing should transmit the teaching of Mishihe*," said Jingjing. "That part isn't bad."

Mi Jifen was taken aback, and then laughed. He laughed without a sound; only his shoulders shook, shook till his stick tapped and tapped on the floor. The servants hurried to steady him.

"Your pa," he said when he had done laughing. "I've seen him."

"Where?"

"The year Chang'an was recovered," said Mi Jifen. "The tenth month, at the city gate. I was in the city then, I'd been hiding more than a year, and I came out to watch the government troops come in. Your pa rode a chestnut horse, not far behind Lord Guo, a scar over his eyebrow, not yet healed, red. Someone beside me said: that's a *hu* monk. I said: what *hu* monk rides like that?"

Jingjing said nothing.

"Only later did I learn he really was a monk," said Mi Jifen. "And one of ours."

When he said *one of ours*, he said it very slowly.

---

Siyuan was a man of few words.

He was thirty or so, and did not look like his father. Mi Jifen was square-faced, broad-browed; Siyuan was long-faced, narrow-chinned, his eyes thin, and when he looked at you it always seemed he was looking at something behind you. He wrote a fine hand, copied scripture fast and clean, and most of the drafts of Jingjing's translations were fair-copied by him.

At the monastery he had charge of the orphans.

This task had come down from Narsai's time. When Narsai was barred from the lectern, Yisi had sentenced him to take the orphans and gather the dead. Narsai did it many years, did it until he died. When he died there was no one to take it up, and Siyuan took it up.

The monastery's orphans—some were left when believers died; some had been abandoned at the monastery gate; some were brought by people of the ward, who said: doesn't your *hu* monastery take in this sort? Siyuan took them in one by one, recorded them one by one in a register, sent the older ones out as apprentices, and found believers' households to foster the little ones. Those who could not be kept alive, he buried too, one by one.

He did all this without ever saying much to Jingjing. At the end of every month he handed the register to Jingjing to look over. When Jingjing had looked it over he signed it and gave it back.

The names in the register were twenty-odd when they were many, seven or eight when they were few.

---

In the winter of the sixteenth year of Zhenyuan, Mi Jifen came again.

This time it was not a Lord's Day. It was an ordinary afternoon, overcast, as if about to snow. He came in a cart; the cart stopped at the monastery gate, and the servant lifted the curtain and helped him down. When he was down he did not go in, but turned back and lifted a child out of the cart.

He could not lift him. The servant hurried to take him, and set the child on the ground.

The child was five or six, in an old padded jacket that did not fit, its cuffs worn shiny. His hair was a little curly, black with a little yellow in it. His face was a Tang face—flat nose, round chin—but his eyes were deep, the sockets set in a little, like a *hu*.

He held half a *hu* flatbread in his fist, and stood there gnawing it.

When Jingjing came out, he had just gnawed the last mouthful, and was licking his fingers clean too.

"Lord Mi," said Jingjing.

Mi Jifen breathed hard a good while before he could speak.

The child's father had been a soldier in the Shence Army, a Sogdian, surnamed An. Some years before he had been baptised at the Daqin monastery; Siyuan knew him. This year the court was fighting in Huaixi, and part of the Shence Army had been sent; he had gone too, and died there, and his body had not been brought back. The child's mother was Tang, the daughter of a poor family outside Chang'an, and had died of sickness the year before last. The child had been living in the camp with a few old soldiers, eating from everyone's pot, for more than a year. Now the camp was moving out, and the old soldiers could not see to him any longer.

"They came to me," said Mi Jifen. "They said: Lord Mi, you believe in that teaching—what's to be done with the boy?"

He looked at the child, and the child looked at him, a few crumbs of bread still at the corner of his mouth.

"I'm old," said Mi Jifen. "My house can't raise him either. Siyuan said the monastery would take him."

Siyuan had already come out from inside and stood behind Jingjing, saying nothing.

Jingjing squatted down, to the child's height.

The child was not afraid of him. He looked at his face, then at the bit of leather cord showing at his neck, and reached out to touch it. Jingjing tucked the cord back into his collar, and the child's hand caught nothing; he did not mind, but drew his hand back and wiped it on his jacket.

"What's his name?" asked Jingjing.

"No name," said Mi Jifen. "His father called him A-Fu. The men in the camp called him Flatbread."

"Why Flatbread?"

"He can eat," said Mi Jifen. "Give him as much as you like, he'll eat it. And when it's gone, he holds out his hand again."

Jingjing looked at the child.

The child looked at him too. His eyes were very bright, and did not shy away at all.

Jingjing suddenly thought of something. He had never seen it, only heard it told. Forty-odd years before, in Chang'an too, a woman carrying a newborn child went to find a *hu* priest. The priest asked her nothing at all, only one thing.

That question came to the tip of his tongue.

"Can this child live?"

Mi Jifen said: "He can. He eats."

Jingjing said: "Then I'll own him."

When he had said it, he was taken aback first himself.

Siyuan behind him was taken aback too. He had had charge of the orphans a dozen years and more, and Jingjing had signed for a dozen years and more, and had never once said he would own any of them.

Mi Jifen was not taken aback. He nodded, as if he had known all along Jingjing would say so. He had his servant fetch a small bundle from the cart; in it were two sets of the child's clothes, a pair of shoes, and a little horse whittled from wood, very roughly, one leg long and one short.

"His father whittled it," said Mi Jifen.

The child saw the little horse, snatched it, and hugged it to him.

Jingjing held out his hand. The child looked at the hand, shifted the little horse under his other arm, freed one hand, and let himself be led by it.

Jingjing's hand was cold. He had sat all afternoon in the scripture room; the brazier had long gone out, his fingers had not warmed yet, and his sleeve smelled of ink. Led in through the gate, the child walked two steps, turned up his face, sniffed hard, and smelled his sleeve.

"Black," the child said.

Jingjing looked down, and sure enough there was a streak of ink on his cuff.

---

That evening Jingjing gave the child a name.

He sat under the lamp and thought a long time. He thought of using his father's name, and felt it would not do. He thought of a Syriac name, and then thought: this child was born of Tang people, grows up on Tang soil, and must live among Tang people after.

Then he thought of a line on the stele.

That line he had written himself. Twenty years before, in the scripture room, he had weighed it word by word for half a month before he settled it. *The Law flowed through the ten circuits, and the state was rich in peace; monasteries filled a hundred cities, and every house abounded in Luminous blessing.*

"He'll be called Jingfu," he said. *Luminous blessing.*

Siyuan heard it beside him and, for once, smiled. "The Master takes the words off his own stele."

"I wrote them. If I don't use them, who will?" said Jingjing.

The child sat by the brazier hugging his little wooden horse, nearly asleep. He heard someone say *fu*, opened his eyes and looked, and shut them again.

---

The first night, Jingjing did not sleep well.

The child slept in his room, on a newly spread straw mat beside his bed. In the middle of the night the child got up and felt his way out of the door. Jingjing thought he was going to the privy, and paid no heed. After a while he heard the well rope in the courtyard, the windlass creaking round.

Jingjing sat up at once.

He threw on his clothes and went out, and saw the child standing by the well with a wooden dipper in both hands, gulping water down. Well water in winter, cold enough to sting the teeth. The child drank one dipperful, drew another, and drank that.

"Jingfu," Jingjing called him.

The child turned his head, drops of water hanging at his mouth, glittering in the moonlight.

"Thirsty," said the child.

Jingjing wanted to say: you mustn't drink cold water. It came to his lips, and he did not say it. He went over, took the dipper, hung it back by the well, and led the child back into the room by the hand. The child's hand was icy.

He tucked the child under the quilt, and sat down by the bed himself, and did not lie down.

He was waiting.

He knew what came next. First the belly rumbling, then cramp after cramp, then trip after trip to the privy, running till daybreak, till the face was white with it. He had been like that since he was small—one mouthful of cold water, and a whole night's torment. He had already left the fire banked in the stove, put a piece of ginger beside it, and set a basin under the bed.

The child soon fell asleep.

He slept very soundly, snoring a little, one breath after another, as even as counting.

Jingjing sat and waited. He waited one watch, and nothing happened to the child. He waited another watch; the child turned over, smacked his lips, and slept on.

Near dawn, it was Jingjing's own stomach that rumbled.

He had drunk a bowl of hot broth the evening before, and the broth had probably gone a little cool. He held his belly and went to the privy once, and when he came back his face was white. The child was still asleep, snoring louder than before.

He sat back down by the bed and looked at that sleep-flushed face.

He thought: this child's stomach isn't like mine.

He thought too: and my stomach isn't like my pa's.

He took up the piece of ginger by the stove, cut two slices for himself, and chewed them.

---

Jingfu settled in at the monastery.

He ate a great deal. The first few days the woman who cooked for the monastery ladled him a bowl; he finished it and stood by the stove holding the empty bowl, saying nothing, just standing. The woman ladled him another, and he finished that. When the third bowl was ladled up, then he waved his hand and said: "Full."

The woman told Jingjing: Master, this child eats enough for three.

Jingjing said: let him eat.

Nothing he ate upset his stomach. Cold rice, cold bread, raw melon, well water, whatever the ward children pressed on him—he took it and put it in his mouth, and when it was in his mouth he swallowed it, and when it was swallowed nothing came of it. The old believers of the monastery all said: that boy has a belly of iron.

He was not afraid of strangers. When Mi Jifen came again to worship, he ran over, stood before the old man, and looked up at him. Mi Jifen held out one finger, and he gripped it, gripped it and would not let go, held it till the service was over.

He was not afraid of Jingjing either.

When Jingjing preached, he sat in the front row and could not sit still, wriggling this way and that. Jingjing glanced at him, and he stopped wriggling. After a while he began again.

Siyuan said: Master, shall I take him to the back?

Jingjing said: no need.

---

After the start of spring, Jingjing taught him his letters.

First he taught him Tang characters. Water, fire, gate, road. He wrote these characters very large on a wooden board and had Jingfu trace them with his finger in a sand tray. The board was the one used in Xizhou in those days, more than forty years old, its corners worn round, and on its face there were still a few gouges, made by Narsai writing the character *man* too hard.

Jingfu learned neither fast nor slow. He traced *water*, traced it crooked, and when he had traced it looked up and asked: "Can you drink it?"

Jingjing said: "You can't drink characters."

Jingfu was a little disappointed, and bent his head and went on tracing.

Later Jingjing taught him the monastery's tongue too. The twenty-two letters of the Syriac alphabet: Jingfu studied a month and learned seven, and said the other fifteen all looked the same. Jingjing wrote them out for him: this one is like this, that one like that; look, there's an extra dot here. Jingfu looked a long time and said: "The dots are too small."

But he sang well.

At the monastery's morning and evening offices they sang the scriptures, in Syriac. Jingfu understood not a word, and sang along, louder than anyone. The tune he remembered after one hearing; the words he could not remember, so he made up his own, and the sounds he made up were much like the real words, and not quite. The old believers standing beside him heard it and wanted to laugh, and dared not, and could only bow their heads lower.

Jingjing heard it from the front, and did not correct him.

His father had once laid down a rule: at table, speak Tang. That was because Jingjing was growing up in Chang'an and must live among Tang people after, and must speak Tang better than the Tang.

Jingfu had no need to learn Tang. He had been born with a Tang mouth—the country speech outside Chang'an, the rough talk of the old soldiers in camp, the curses of the ward children, he knew it all.

Jingjing thought, and turned the rule round.

"At table," he told Jingfu, "speak the monastery's tongue."

Jingfu thought and asked: "If I don't know a word of it, how can I speak it?"

"Then learn one sentence first."

The first sentence Jingjing taught him was the thanksgiving before meals. Jingfu studied three days and learned it. From then on, before every meal he said that sentence through once in those sounds of his own making, and when he had said it touched his right hand to his forehead, his chest, and each shoulder, and then grabbed his chopsticks.

He touched them very fast, as if afraid the food would get cold.

---

That winter, when it was nearly over, one evening Jingjing opened the cupboard.

In the cupboard were three bowls.

One was his own, used for decades, a little chip knocked out of its rim. One was his father's; since his father died it had sat upside down in here, and no one had used it. And one, older than both, with a notch in its rim and a pinch of earth in it. That was Du De's. Du De had died in Hebei and been buried with his sword; the bowl his father had brought back. All his life his father had kept it beside Jingjing's bowl.

Jingjing looked at the three bowls, looked a while.

He took his father's bowl out.

It was a coarse bowl, fired over Lingzhou way, its glaze greyish, a ring at the bottom unglazed, showing the colour of yellow earth. He took it to the stove, washed it in hot water once, and again, and dried it.

He ladled a bowl of mutton soup, with turnip in it, and meat. He set the bowl in front of Jingfu.

Jingfu looked at the bowl, and at him.

"Eat it," said Jingjing.

Jingfu ate it.

When he had eaten it, he pushed the bowl forward, and held it out again.
