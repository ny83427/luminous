# Volume Twelve　Jade Gate

## Chapter 79　Old He

> The Valley of the Dawn came in awe, and the Cave of the Moon gathered in full.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

At the opening of spring in the second year of Dazhong, a great thing happened in the town of Shazhou. In those days the townspeople hardly went out; in the streets walked Tibetan soldiers, troop after troop, on horseback, swords at their sides, going back and forth through the town. Jingfu and the rest lived under the colonnade of that small Buddhist monastery east of the town; every morning the monastery's old monk went out once, and when he came back his face was tighter each day; asked what was happening in the town, he would not say, only: don't you go out. One night in the third month the town suddenly began to shout—first the north of the town, then the east, then the whole town—shouting in Tang, very loud; some beat gongs, some beat basins, beat till the whole town was noise. Salang climbed onto the monastery wall to look, and saw fires in several places in the town, people running in the streets, people holding up torches, and under the torches a banner, red, with one character on it, too far to make out.

At daybreak the shouting in the town stopped. The old monk came back from outside, his face changed, his eyes red; he stood at the monastery gate and told those under the colonnade: the Tang of the town have risen; a man surnamed Zhang, called Zhang Yichao, one of the great families of Shazhou, led the town's Tang, and in one night they killed the Tibetan commander, drove the Tibetan soldiers out of the town, and changed the flags on the town gates—changed them to the Tang's flags. He said Shazhou had been governed by the Tibetans over sixty years; over sixty years the Tang of the town wore their hair in Tibetan braids, wore Tibetan robes, spoke Tibetan; this morning the whole town's Tang are cutting off their braids.

That day Jingfu went out of the monastery gate and into the town to look. The streets were full of people—men and women, old and young—standing by the road, some with scissors, some with knives, cutting off braids one by one; the cut braids were thrown on the ground, thrown the length of the street; some, having cut, felt the backs of their heads and wept; some, having cut, knelt on the ground and knocked their heads toward the east. An old woman past seventy sat on the threshold of a shop clutching a handful of cut braids, clutching them and weeping, and weeping told those beside her: when she was small her Pa told her, we're Tang; sooner or later we'll go back; her Pa died; her Pa didn't live to see it; she has.

Jingfu stood at the roadside watching. He was wearing the black robe brought out of Chang'an; the Office of Receptions' roll said he was a man of An, to be sent back to his own place; he had come out of the Tang's country and walked two years, walked out of the Tang's borders and into the Tibetans' country, and today the town he stood in was the Tang's again. He stood there watching a streetful of Tang cutting off their braids. The old woman on the threshold saw him, looked at his black robe, at his face, and asked him: are you Tang too? He said he was born in Chang'an, Yining Ward in Chang'an, but the Office of Receptions' roll said he was a man of An, to be sent back to his own place. The old woman didn't understand, and asked again: are you Tang or aren't you? He thought, and said he was a man of Chang'an, and Chang'an had sent him out. The old woman laid the handful of braids she was clutching on the threshold, reached out and felt his sleeve, and said: this robe of yours is Chang'an cloth. Jingfu stood a moment, turned, and went back to the monastery.

---

He Qi's caravan reached Shazhou in the fifth month of that year. The caravan came from Xizhou by the Moheyan desert, twenty-odd camels, carrying the white cotton of Xizhou, the jade of Khotan, the horses of Karashahr, to trade for silk and tea at Shazhou. The day the caravan arrived, a ring of people gathered at the town gate of Shazhou; the town's Tang had just driven out the Tibetans, and their own soldiers stood at the gate; the soldiers questioned the caravan—where from, who governs Xizhou, does he govern well; the caravan's headman said from Xizhou; the Uighurs govern it; better than the Tibetans. The soldiers laughed and let them into the town.

Jingfu met him in a hostel in the town. That day he went to the hostel to ask after caravans going west, and asked the hostel's master too whether he knew a man surnamed He in Xizhou, called He Qi, who used to keep a silk shop in the West Market at Chang'an; the hostel's master said he did: Old He, who ran the road from Xizhou to Shazhou; one had just come in—his, in fact—and the headman was unloading in the back courtyard. He went to the back courtyard to look; in the back courtyard stood a man with his back to him, talking with camel men—a man of fifty-odd, not tall, a little hunchbacked, an old cloth wound round his head, his beard white, his face burned blackish red by the sun, its wrinkles line on line as if cut with a knife. The camel men called him Old He; Old He's voice was husky, his throat worn by wind and sand. Jingfu stood at the gate of the back courtyard listening to him speak, listened a few sentences, and made out the accent—the accent of the West Market at Chang'an, the accent of a Sogdian speaking Tang; he stepped forward two paces, and the man turned round.

Jingfu knew him first, and called: He Qi. The man was taken aback, looked at the black robe Jingfu wore, looked at his face, and his mouth fell open, and he said: Leader? He said: Leader, how are you here?

---

He Siniang saw her brother that evening. Jingfu brought He Qi back to the small Buddhist monastery east of town; He Siniang was boiling water under the colonnade; Lingbao lay on his felt; Xiaoman and Ayi were picking up firewood in the courtyard. He Qi came in at the monastery gate, saw that woman squatting by the stove under the colonnade, stopped, and called: Sister. He Siniang turned round; the tongs fell from her hand to the ground; she stood up and looked at him, at his white beard, at that sunburned face, and knew him; she said: He Qi, you've grown old. He Qi said: Sister, you've grown old too.

He Siniang went to him first, reached out and felt his face, felt the lines on his face one by one, and said: when she saw you off outside the Kaiyuan Gate you were just past forty, and your face was pale; now your beard is whiter than Pa's when he died. He Qi said the sun in the west is fierce and the wind strong; half the year he walks in the desert; a man ages fast. He said: the year he left, Sister, you said you wouldn't go; he thought about it a hundred times—how you were getting on in Chang'an, whether the Great Worthy was still living, whether the monastery still stood—thought about it a hundred times, and never thought he'd meet you in a Buddhist monastery at Shazhou.

He Siniang brought him to Lingbao. Lingbao lay on his felt, heard, propped himself up, leaned his head forward, and said: is it He Qi? He Qi knelt before him and said: Great Worthy, it's him. Lingbao reached out, found He Qi's shoulder, found his arm, and said: He Qi, you've grown thin; your bones dig into the hand. He Qi said the food in the west doesn't fatten a man. Lingbao said: that day outside the Kaiyuan Gate he blessed your caravan; what he recited was *the Lord shall preserve thy going out and thy coming in*; he said he couldn't see you; today he's touched you; the Lord has preserved you.

He Qi, kneeling, took a little cloth packet from his robe and opened it; inside was a wooden cross, of jujube wood, two inches or so long, its four ends whittled round, strung on a red cord, the cord faded to white. He said this cross was the one Sister gave him, left by Sister's first husband Kang Shouxin; he had worn it eight years, travelled eight years by land; the land isn't damp; the cross didn't rot. He said: when he left, he said Chang'an had no future for the *hu*; he was right; Chang'an has sent you all out; but he was wrong too; he said he'd carry this cross west, and Xizhou has churches; he reached Xizhou and stood in the church north of the town; it had no priest; a few old people recited scripture empty-handed, and when they came to the offering bread they stopped; he stood at the back and stopped with them; every time he stopped he thought: in the Chang'an monastery today they're breaking the bread; the Great Worthy is breaking it. He said the half he got right was Chang'an; the half he got wrong was this cross; the cross goes with the people.

---

He Qi said the wife he took out of Chang'an died in Xizhou the year before last, of dysentery; he hadn't married again; his two sons were both in Xizhou—the elder ran the caravans with him, unloading in the back courtyard right now; the younger was learning the fur trade in the town of Xizhou; his daughter had married into a Sogdian family of Xizhou. He said: Sister, when you see them they won't know you; the elder was only ten the year they left.

The caravan rested half a month at Shazhou, traded its goods, and made ready to go back to Xizhou. He Qi said he'd take them. He said that stretch of the Moheyan desert was eight hundred *li*—no water, no grass, no people—eight or nine days' walking, and one wrong step and you walk into the sand; long ago the Dharma Master Xuanzang crossed this stretch, took the wrong road, went four nights and five days without a mouthful of water, lay in the sand waiting to die, and it was his horse that smelled water and carried him to the side of a spring. He said he had travelled this road eight years, a dozen-odd times; he knew every spring, every ruined beacon, every heap of bones; he'd take you; follow him; when he says stop, stop; when he says go, go; the water he'll share out, and no one drinks one mouthful more.

In his caravan was a young man of twenty-odd, a Sogdian called Kang Zhen, of Xizhou, in charge of the caravan's water-skins and camel bells. He Qi told Jingfu that Kang Zhen was of the church north of the town of Xizhou; his grandfather's grandfather had once been a deacon of that north church, the church that the Great Worthy Jingjing, coming east long ago with the seven elders, had repaired; afterwards the church's people grew fewer generation by generation, and now only a few old people were left; as a child Kang Zhen learned Syriac letters in the church, not many; at the offering once in seven, whenever he was in Xizhou, he went, stood at the back, and recited empty-handed with the old people. Kang Zhen stood by, listening to He Qi, looking at Jingfu, and asked him: the leader from Chang'an—what was the Great Worthy Jingjing to you? Jingfu said: his Master. Kang Zhen's eyes widened, and he said his grandfather's grandfather told his grandfather, and his grandfather told him, that the Great Worthy Jingjing had stayed in the north church that year, eleven years old; when the elders repaired the church he pulled the weeds in the courtyard, pulled them a month; when the weeds were all pulled, the elders cleaned the wooden cross on the east wall of the hall, and he stood on tiptoe underneath, steadying it.

---

They went out by the Jade Gate at the beginning of the sixth month. The Jade Gate was north of Guazhou; its pass-fort was earthen, half fallen; there was no one guarding the pass; the Tibetans had gone, and Zhang Yichao had not yet sent troops. He Qi had the caravan rest a night below the pass, fill all the water-skins, water the camels full, and the next day before light go out through the pass.

Beyond the pass, to the northwest, was the Moheyan desert. The first two days were gravel, black gravel to the end of sight, sand beneath the gravel; the camels' hooves crunched on it; once the sun was up the gravel grew hot, hot enough to feel through the soles of shoes. He Qi had them rest by day and travel by night: by day make the camels kneel, the people crouch in the camels' shade under felts rigged as awnings against the sun, and as soon as it was dark take the road. Lingbao lay in the cart, its awning covered with three layers of felt; He Siniang sat beside him wiping his face with a damp cloth; when the cloth dried she wet it again with a little water, water she saved from her own share.

Where they stopped at daybreak on the fourth day, all around were bones—camels' bones, horses' bones, and people's too, bleached by the sun, half buried in sand by the wind, heap after heap, on both sides of the road. Xiaoman cried with fright; Ayi held her close and covered her eyes with her hand. He Qi said these were all people who took the wrong road; some buried by the wind and sand, some out of water—once down, they couldn't get up. He said: don't look; follow the camels; the camels know the road.

On the sixth night a wind rose. It came from the north, carrying sand, striking the face like flung pebbles; the camels would go no further, knelt, and buried their heads in the sand. He Qi had them ring the camels into a circle, the people crouching inside it with felts over their heads, and they crouched half the night. There were voices in the wind—like someone weeping, someone shouting, someone in the distance calling names; He Qi said that was the sound of the sand, not people; no one was to answer; answer, and you follow it, and walk into the sand. Xiaoman shook under the felt; Ayi sang at her ear, sang in Uighur; Jingfu couldn't understand it, but he could tell the tune—a tune of the Manichaean monastery. He sang too; he sang "In the highest of the heavens, deep reverence and praise"; he sang, He Siniang sang along, Elijah sang along; Salang couldn't sing and hummed along; Lingbao in the cart recited along. In the voice of the wind, those few voices, thin and fine; each time the wind swept past, the voices were blown apart, and scattered, joined up again.

On the eighth day the water was nearly gone. He Qi gathered all the remaining water-skins in one place and shared them out one by one: a small bowl a day for each person, none for the camels; the camels could last two more days. That day Lingbao failed; his lips were so dry they cracked open and seeped blood; He Siniang fed him her own bowl; he drank a mouthful and would drink no more, and said: give it to the children. He Siniang said the children had theirs. He said: then give it to Salang; Salang carried that jar of ash all night and hasn't drunk. Salang said he wasn't thirsty. Lingbao said he could hear it: your voice has gone hoarse. That evening He Qi saw his sister pour her own bowl into Lingbao's water-skin again; he went over and snatched the skin away and said: Sister, the rule is his; one bowl each, and no one gives his away; if you give yours away, tomorrow you'll fall in the sand, and the Great Worthy still needs someone to hold him up—who'll hold him up? He Siniang said she wasn't thirsty. He Qi said: whether you're thirsty or not, he can see; your lips have split in three places. The two of them stood in the sand while the caravan's people all watched; He Qi poured the water in that skin back into her bowl, poured it full, handed it to her, and said: drink it; if you don't, he'll take back the Great Worthy's bowl too; he means what he says. He Siniang held the bowl, looked at him, and drank.

On the ninth day, when it was nearly light, He Qi stood on a camel's back and looked northwest, looked a while, jumped down, and told them: we're there. Ahead was a hollow, and in the hollow a few clumps of reeds, and beneath the reeds, water.

The camels smelled it first; they rose one by one and, without waiting to be led, ran down into the hollow, ran to the water's edge, lowered their heads, and drank in great gulps. The people ran after them; Salang ran at the front, reached the water, knelt, untied the clay jar from his chest and set it aside, scooped water in both hands and splashed it on his face, splashed his whole face, and scooped again and drank. Xiaoman and Ayi ran to the water and lay flat and put their faces in it. Jingfu did not run; he held Lingbao's cart and walked step by step to the water's edge.

He Qi was already at the water. He took a wooden bowl from his robe, scooped a bowl from the spring's eye—the water in the spring's eye was clear and cold—carried that bowl of water to the side of the cart, knelt, and held the bowl to Lingbao's lips. He said: Great Worthy, drink; this is a grass pool; past this one, two more days, and it's Yizhou.

Lingbao felt the bowl, held it, drank a mouthful, and another, and when he had drunk gave the bowl back to him. He said: He Qi, long ago you said Chang'an had no future for the *hu*. He Qi said yes. Lingbao said: you were right. He said: this mouthful he's drunk today is sweet. He Qi squatted by the cart, scooped another bowl, and handed it to He Siniang.

---

Note: In the second year of Dazhong (848) Zhang Yichao of Shazhou led his people to drive out the Tibetan commander and returned to the Tang; see the Tibetan chapter of the *New Book of Tang* and the Dunhuang manuscript *Transformation Text of Zhang Yichao*; Shazhou had fallen to the Tibetans in the second year of Zhenyuan (786). In the Tang the Jade Gate pass lay north of Jinchang county in Guazhou; the Moheyan desert ran over eight hundred *li* from the Jade Gate to Yizhou; Xuanzang went "four nights and five days without a single drop to wet his throat" and was led by his horse to the side of a spring; see the *Biography of the Tripitaka Master of the Great Ci'en Monastery*, book 1. In the mid-ninth century Xizhou was controlled by the Uighurs. Kang Zhen is the novel's invention.
