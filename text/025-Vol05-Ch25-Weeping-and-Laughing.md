# Volume Five　Chang'an

## Chapter 25　Weeping and Laughing

> Some of the old monasteries were kept, and some halls of the Law were rebuilt larger; their galleries and roofs were adorned, soaring like a pheasant in flight.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

They began pulling down the main hall in the tenth month.

The monastery had been under repair most of the year, but what had been repaired was all at the edges: the side-room roofs patched, the courtyard wall built up again, two rows of sheds put up in the back courtyard for the guard brothers to live in. The main hall had never been touched. It had half burned; on the east side a few pillars still stood askew, and the west side had collapsed into a heap, brick and tile and charred timber and ash all mixed together, piled higher than a man, and on rainy days it oozed black water. Sabrisho's view was that the hall must be cleared away completely and built again, not patched and darned on the old frame; Narsai had no objection, only said that before they set hand to it they must pray. So they prayed three days, and on the fourth day they began.

The twenty-four guard brothers, with several dozen people who came from the ward to help, took a full half-month and more to pull it down. The charred timbers were carried out one by one, the brick and tile carried out basket by basket, the ash carted cartload by cartload to the foot of the city wall and dumped. Sargis was strong; he could shoulder a beam by himself, swing it onto his shoulder and walk off steady as you like, and the young men of the ward watched and clicked their tongues. Ishaq stood to one side directing—to the left now, now to the right, now gently, more gently—and did not himself carry a single stick. Old Duan could not stand it and said a few words to him, and Ishaq patted his belly and laughed: "This flesh of mine can't carry timber. It carries your dinners." And when they thought about it, so it did: all these days the meals for several dozen mouths had been his doing at the stove.

The accounts for the pulling down, Yisi gave to Jingjing.

Early that morning he put a roll of old paper, a brush and a cake of ink in his son's hands and said: "The people who come to help—write them down one by one: which day they came, how many days they worked, how many meals they ate at the monastery; it all has to be counted. Sabrisho has only fifteen bolts of silk for the repairs, and from now on the timber, the craftsmen, everything comes out of that; every foot and inch has to be accounted for."

The people from the ward wanted no wages, only a midday meal at the monastery. So Jingjing brought out a little stool and sat on the steps at the door of the side room and asked their names one by one. The errand looked easy and was not simple to do. Some gave their proper names; some said only "the one from the vinegar shop at the east end"; and some gave a name and then waved both hands, saying: don't write it, whatever you do, it's unlucky on paper. Jingjing did not argue with anyone, and wrote them down all the same: *Second son of the vinegar house at the east end*; *Would not give name, left hand missing a finger*. In the evening he read the day's account to his Pa from the beginning, and Yisi sat under the lamp listening, said neither that it was good nor that it was not, and only when he read a number wrong reached across and tapped the place on the paper once. Then Jingjing knew it was wrong, and went red, and worked it out again.

On the tenth day or so of the pulling down, the old footings under the hall came to light.

The old footings were laid in bluestone, block against block, very neatly laid; only the fire had been over them, and the stone was marked with patch after patch of black, some blocks were cracked, and one had broken into three. That evening, when the helpers had all gone, Jingjing took a broom to sweep the loose ash off the footings. When he came to the north-east corner, the broom-head brushed the side of one stone, a layer of ash came pattering off, and beneath it a few incised strokes showed. He squatted and rubbed with his sleeve, could not get it clean, and dug at it bit by bit with his fingernail, and what he dug out was five characters: *Twelfth year of Zhenguan*.

These five characters he knew. He squatted there a good long while before it occurred to him to go and call someone, then scrambled up and ran for the side room, shouting for Babai as he ran.

---

At the beginning of the eleventh month, a reply came from Lingzhou.

In the ninth month Yisi had sent two bolts of silk with a caravan going to Lingzhou, for Shi Six's family; this time the caravan came back and brought the letter back with it. Since Ahad left, the Lingzhou monastery had been looked after by an old believer in the city surnamed Jiao. Old Master Jiao could not write Syriac, and had asked a scholar in the city to write for him, a whole page in Tang characters. Yisi knew few Tang characters; Jingjing read it.

The letter said three things. The first was about Shi Six's family. They had been found: they lived in the east of the city, and there was an old father at home, a widowed sister-in-law, and two nephews; the silk had been delivered. Old Father Shi had asked where his son was buried, and Old Master Jiao had told him in the words of the letter from Chang'an, and the old man, hearing it, said only one thing, after a long while: "So far." The second was about the Lingzhou monastery. When the Son of Heaven was at Lingwu he had issued an edict that Luminous monasteries be built again in Lingwu and four other commanderies. Yisi knew of the edict, only by then he had long since left Lingzhou with Lord Guo, and did not know what came of it. Old Master Jiao's letter said that when the edict came down, the work on the monastery had begun, and gone on more than a year in all; now the hall was repaired, and the roof did not leak any more. The third: Old Master Jiao asked how the Chang'an monastery's repairs were going, and what it was short of; Lingzhou had nothing else, but it had wool and hides, and after the winter they could send a few cartloads by caravan.

When Jingjing came to the line *the roof does not leak any more*, he stopped of himself and looked up at his Pa. He had lived in Lingzhou several years. That monastery's roof had leaked every year, Ahad had said every year it must be repaired, and every year they could not afford it, and on rainy nights father and son had often stood in the hall with basins catching the water. Yisi looked at him too, and after a while said: "Read on."

When the letter was finished, Jingjing copied it out again from the beginning on the blank pages at the back of the account book. When he had copied it, he thought, and beside the line about Shi Six's family added a line of small characters: *East of the city. Old father one, widowed sister-in-law one, nephews two.* Yisi saw it from beside him and said nothing.

---

Strangely, since someone had come from the palace in the ninth month, all had been quiet at the monastery gate.

Before, every few days someone had come asking questions. The ward warden had come, minor clerks of the Metropolitan Prefecture had come, and once two of the Golden Guard had come and stood at the gate a long while craning their necks to look in, asking who had approved the repair of this monastery, and whether there were documents. Official Pei had gone several times to speak about it; each time he spoke there was quiet for a while, and after a while someone came again. After the palace envoy's visit, none of them came again.

Yisi's purple robe was still locked in the cupboard in the side room. The letter to the Patriarchate had still had no reply.

---

The day for laying the foundation was fixed for the twenty-second of the eleventh month.

Some of the older people in the ward warned that winter was no time to break ground. Sabrisho said they were not breaking ground this time, only setting one stone down first; the hall would be built after spring opened. The old people heard it and said no more.

On the evening of the twentieth, the Sogdian owner of the silk shop in the West Market came back from Luoyang. In the ninth month, it was he whom Yisi had trusted to take the silk and that small wooden board to the Xiushan Ward. Behind his cart came three people, and as soon as they came in at the monastery gate, the old man at their head saw Yisi and stopped, stood a good long while, and then came tottering over and grasped Yisi's arm with both hands, grasped it hard.

It was Old Jacob.

In the dozen years and more since they had last met, Old Jacob had aged terribly. He had shrunk a whole size, his back was bent, the grey robe on him was washed nearly white, and round his neck still hung the same copper cross. Holding on to Yisi, he poured out a great stream in Sogdian mixed with Tang: the board had come, and the silk had come too; the board said to come to Chang'an at next year's Fast, and he couldn't wait till next year—as soon as he heard the Chang'an monastery was laying its foundation he had begged the shopkeeper to bring him along; "at my age, who can say what next year holds." Twenty days or so on the road, the cart had shaken him till his bones were coming apart, but come he had.

Yisi said: "It's good you've come, it's good you've come." He was a slow speaker always, but this he said fast, and said twice.

Two people had come behind Old Jacob. One was a young man of seventeen or eighteen called Luo Fifteen, his daughter's son, who had driven the cart and fed the beasts all the way. The other was a woman of middle years, also a Sogdian, holding a blue cloth bundle, who stood outside the threshold and would not come in. Old Jacob said she was a neighbour from the Xiushan Ward, married into a family named Mu; she was not one of the faithful, but when she heard he was coming to Chang'an she had insisted on coming too, saying she wanted to see the priest who had written the letter that time.

Jingjing went out and called her twice before the woman stepped over the threshold. She came into the courtyard, looked about her at the heap of charred timber not yet carted away, and at the old footings swept spotless, looked a good while, and held the bundle tighter to her.

For the next two days, Old Jacob said not one word about Luoyang. Nor did Yisi ask.

---

The day the foundation was laid was overcast. From morning the sky was grey, grey and very low, as if pressing down on the tops of the ward walls. There was not much wind, only cold, so cold that anyone stepping out of doors hunched his neck into his collar.

Many more came than Yisi had thought. The monastery's own people went without saying: the seven teachers, the twenty-four guard brothers, Xu Jing, Old Duan. Granny He, Old Mi and Widow Kang stood by the stove platform as always, where they had grown used to standing all winter giving out gruel. Official Pei came too, not in his official robe but in a plain one, mixed in among the crowd so that you would not know him unless you looked closely. A dozen or so of the old soldiers who had watched the blocks on the day of the rice at the Qujiang came, Hou Siyuan and Cheng Chongyi among them. Still more came from the ward—people who had drunk gruel at the monastery gate, people who had gone south of the city to dig the pits. The old man from south of the city came too with his two sons; the three of them stood at the courtyard gate, craning their heads to look in, and however they were called would not come further.

And there were some others whom Yisi knew by sight and for the moment could not put names to.

They were people of the monastery from the old days. In the Tianbao years, when the monastery was at its height, a seventh-day offering would bring several hundred: Persians, Sogdians, Tokharians, and Tang people of the place too. Then the war came, the city fell, and they fled or died or scattered, so that not one or two in ten were left. This past year and more, little by little, some had come back. Some had heard there was gruel at the monastery gate and come to drink it, and drinking it had recognised Yisi standing by the pot; some had heard tell elsewhere that people were living in the monastery in the Yining Ward again, that the lamp was lit at night again, and had asked their way there. Twenty-odd had come today, all old now, some on sticks, some held up by children or grandchildren, and one old woman pushed there by her grandson in a wheelbarrow.

Jingjing went round with the register asking their names one by one.

The old people spoke slowly, and their speech was mixed, some Sogdian, some Tokharian; when Jingjing could not understand he ran and dragged Haddad over to translate. When he had got it clear he wrote each name twice, once in Tang characters and once in Syriac letters. A Tokharian old man gave a name long and twisty, Haddad translated, Jingjing wrote, and the old man leaned in and looked a long time, did not know a single letter, and pointing at the paper asked: "That's me?" Jingjing said yes, and the old man grinned, only a few teeth left in his mouth.

Old Jacob gave the names of the Xiushan Ward people too. He gave them very carefully, household by household: how many in this household, who in that household had died, who in that one was left, not missing one. Jingjing filled a page, turned it over and filled half a page more, and by the end his hand ached from writing.

---

The old footings had been cleared clean. The cracked and broken stones had been carried away; the rest were left where they were. At the north-east corner a pit had been dug, its floor bedded with a layer of rammed earth, and at the edge of the pit lay a new stone.

The new stone had been brought out of the Zhongnan Mountains, bluestone too, the same stone as the old footings. Official Pei had found the mason through others, and the mason, hearing it was for the monastery, had taken no money. He had cut the stone square and true, polished one face smooth, and on the smooth face carved a cross, its four ends rounded like four leaves. The old stone carved with *Twelfth year of Zhenguan* had not been carried away; it lay right by the pit, beside the new stone.

Yisi stood at the edge of the pit, still in that grey robe. The scar at his left eyebrow had faded to white, and under the leaden sky looked whiter than ever.

Narsai prayed first. He prayed the way he always spoke, his voice hard, knocking the words out one at a time, as if he were driving nails. After him Sabrisho, then Haddad, Babai. When it came to Ishaq, he was shortest, done in two or three sentences, and when he was done he said "Mm" to himself, as if nodding to himself, so that a few of the guard brothers beside him nearly laughed aloud. Yonan did not pray. He stood at the very back of the seven, his two hands folded before him, his head bowed all the while.

When the prayers were done, Sargis and three other brothers bent and lifted the new stone, shifted it step by step to the edge of the pit, and lowered it slowly in. The stone came down on the rammed earth with a *thud*, a dull sound, and those standing near felt the soles of their feet shake.

---

Gabriel opened the scroll and read from the book of Ezra.

He read a sentence in Syriac and expounded it in Tang. His Tang had come on a great deal this past year, but when he expounded scripture he still liked to use the ways of putting things he had worked out himself, and did not much follow other people's words. He told of that year when the builders laid the foundation of the Lord's house, and the priests stood in their apparel with trumpets, and all the people stood and praised the Lord. Here there were of course no trumpets; the guard brothers stood in a row leaning on their staves, and that would do for a guard of honour. He told too how they sang together by course, saying the Lord is good, and his mercy endureth for ever, without end.

When he came to this, among the twenty-odd people of the old days, a few old ones began to sing. What they sang was a hymn handed down in the monastery from early days, in Tang, old words, and to an old tune. They did not sing together: some fast, some slow, and some forgot the words halfway and only hummed along with their mouths open.

Narsai heard, and turned his head to look at those old people. His brows drew together at once, and he stepped half a pace forward, as if to speak. Sabrisho, standing beside him, reached out and touched his sleeve lightly, and Narsai stood still, and in the end did not speak. When the old people had finished singing, only then did Sabrisho begin, and sang the same hymn again in Syriac, and the seven teachers sang with him, all perfectly together. The people of the ward understood not a word, but they all fell quiet, and listened.

Jingjing stood beside his Pa, looking now at Narsai, now at the old people who had sung, and did not know what it was that lay between them.

Gabriel went on expounding. He told how the foundation of the house was laid, and all the people shouted with a great shout; but many of the ancient men, that had seen the first house, when the foundation of this house was laid before their eyes, wept with a loud voice.

He had just come to this when, on the other side of the courtyard, someone wept aloud.

---

It was Old Jacob who wept.

He was standing beside the Tokharian old man, both hands clutching the copper cross at his neck. At first only his shoulders heaved; then he could not hold it, and wept aloud, his voice hoarse and deep, as if squeezed up from somewhere very far down. When he wept, the woman from the Mu family wept too; she squatted down with her bundle and buried her face in it, and wept till she shook all over. The Tokharian old man wept too. In the Tianbao years he had come to every seventh-day offering, and when he came he sat in the very front row, the third seat from the left, because he was hard of hearing and only at the front could hear clearly. The old woman who had come in the wheelbarrow leaned half out of it and stretched out her hand as if to touch the new stone, but could not reach, and the hand trembled and trembled in the air. The grandson pushing the barrow was only in his teens, and did not know why his grandmother was weeping; he lost his head, squatted down and asked her again and again, and she did not answer, only wept.

So, one drawing another after them, all twenty-odd old people wept, crowded together, weeping as one. Some wept aloud, some made no sound and only let the tears fall, and the tears ran down the lines of their faces all the way to their chins.

On the other side of the courtyard the people of the ward stared, dumbfounded. Most of them had only come to hear the teaching at the monastery since the rice at the Qujiang; they had never seen the old hall, and did not know what it had been like. In their eyes, what had been here was a heap of charred timber and a pile of broken brick and tile, and now it had been cleared spotless and a new stone set up—plainly a happy occasion. The young man who often helped Yisi ladle gruel at the monastery gate looked at that stone and grinned first, and when he grinned, a few young men beside him laughed too, and someone clapped. Hou Siyuan in his delight pursed his lips and let off a whistle, and got a hard slap on the back of the head from Cheng Chongyi, and clutching his head, went on laughing.

Gabriel was still expounding, but his voice was drowned under the weeping and the laughing, and those standing at the back heard it only in snatches. What he was expounding was this very passage: that at that time too many shouted aloud for joy, so that the people could not discern the noise of the shout of joy from the noise of the weeping of the people, and the noise was heard afar off. When he had finished, he closed the scroll and said nothing more.

Old Duan stood a few paces behind Yisi, neither weeping nor laughing, the scar on his face drawn tight. When he had stood a while he raised his sleeve and wiped it across his face. Xu Jing beside him saw, leaned over and asked whether he was crying. Old Duan swore and said the wind had got in his eyes. Xu Jing looked up at the sky and said: what wind today? Old Duan could not be bothered with him, and turned his face away.

---

When the Tokharian old man had wept enough, he shuffled step by step up to the new stone, looked down at the cross on it a while, then turned his head and looked at the characters on the old stone beside it. Tang characters he could not read; he only looked. When he had looked, he raised his head and looked over toward the seven teachers, and looking, he saw Yonan.

He looked a long time, narrowing his eyes. Yonan stood there and did not move at all. The old man took two steps forward, came up to Yonan, tipped up his face to look at that beardless face that seemed so young, and called out in Tokharian: "Bishop." Yisi understood some Tokharian and caught it. The old man said again: "Bishop, you've come back."

Yonan answered him in Tang: "I am not a bishop any more."

The old man did not understand Tang, and thought Yonan was answering to his name; he seized Yonan's hand, held it fast in his own, and wept again. Yonan did not draw his hand away, and let him hold it so.

---

As people were gradually leaving, Official Pei came over and stood a while with Yisi at the edge of the pit. Looking at the new stone, he asked: "When the hall's built, will it be three bays or five?"

Yisi said: "Five. Sabrisho has reckoned it up; the money isn't enough. We'll build it first and see."

Official Pei nodded, and then, as if something had come to mind, said: "I heard the old people of the ward say that in the Tianbao years General Gao, by imperial command, brought the portraits of five former emperors here and they were enshrined in this hall. The portraits are gone now. When the new hall's built, will you ask for them again? If you will, I'll find out the way to go about it for you."

He asked it in passing, still looking at the stone as he asked. Yisi did not answer at once. Narsai was standing not far behind them and heard every word clearly. Jingjing saw his face darken all at once, his two hands clasped before him, clasped till the knuckles went white; but he neither came over nor spoke.

It was Sabrisho who answered: "That matter—let's discuss it when the hall's built."

Official Pei looked at Sabrisho, and at Narsai, and probably saw something, for he asked no more.

---

The old woman in the wheelbarrow, when she was leaving, had her grandson push the barrow up to Jingjing. She took Jingjing's hand, tipped her head back and brought her face very close, narrowed her clouded eyes and looked him over, looked him over a long time, and only then said: "You're Priest Yi's boy, aren't you? Back then you were only this big." She showed with her two hands, smaller than a cat. "Your ma used to hold you and sit in the very last row of this hall. If you cried, she was afraid of disturbing the others, and carried you out and walked up and down under the locust tree by the door, and she'd not have gone up and down many times before you stopped."

Jingjing did not know what to say. His ma he remembered: she was a Sogdian, and her hands were soft and warm, like a cloth that has been in the sun. The year he was six, his ma had died of sickness.

The old woman looked at him a while longer and said: "Grown so big." Then she let go of his hand and had her grandson push her away. Jingjing stood where he was, watching the wheelbarrow creak out of the courtyard gate, put his hand in his coat, found the string of knots, did not take it out, and in his coat rolled a knot between his fingers.

---

That day, after the hour of *wei*, Old Jacob told Yisi in the side room what had happened in Luoyang.

Jingjing was in the room too. He had come with the register to ask Old Jacob a few more names, and when Old Jacob began he did not leave, and Yisi did not send him out. The woman from the Mu family sat in the corner, her bundle on her knees, her head bowed all the while.

"Your letter came five days before the Uighur troops entered the city," said Old Jacob. "A Sogdian brought it. I didn't know him, and he didn't say much; he took it from his sleeve and pushed it into my hand and went, without so much as a sip of water. As soon as I saw the hand I knew it was yours—your Syriac leans to the right, just the same as it always did."

He had done as the letter said: hidden the placard under the stove, then gone household by household to call people. "Some came as soon as I called. Some wouldn't come; they said the Uighurs were helpers the government troops had asked in, the government troops coming into the city was a happy thing, what was there to be afraid of? The ones who wouldn't come, I went and called twice, and when it came to the third time, I didn't go again."

Here Old Jacob stopped, as if waiting for that sentence to pass.

"The rebels left at night, without a sound. Early the next morning the government troops came into the city. I took the placard from under the stove and pasted it on the gate. Fourteen households came in all, eighty-odd souls, old and young; the few rooms in the back courtyard were packed full, and those who couldn't fit sat in the courtyard. I barred the gate from inside and wedged two beams against it. In the second half of the day, the Uighur troops came."

Jingjing asked: "Did they get in?"

"They didn't get in," said Old Jacob. "They pounded on the gate outside, pounding and shouting, and what they shouted I couldn't understand. In the back courtyard not one person dared make a sound; when the children cried for fright, the grown-ups held their hands over their mouths. When they'd shouted a while, there was someone outside talking Tang, I couldn't hear what. Luo Fifteen climbed up on the wall and peeped, and said it was a man in Tang army clothes, following behind the Uighur troops. The man pointed at the placard on the gate and said a few words to the Uighurs, and the Uighurs stood there looking at the placard, looked a while, and turned and went next door."

"Who was next door?" Jingjing asked again.

Old Jacob did not answer at once. He rested his two hands on his knees, looked at his hands, and only after a good while said: "Next door was the Mu family that sold flatbread. Not our people, but neighbours of the monastery twenty years; every few days I went to their house to buy bread."

The brush in Jingjing's hand stopped on the register; a drop of ink fell and spread into a big blot on the paper, and he did not wipe it.

"That night, someone pounded on the back gate," Old Jacob went on. "Pounding hard, and a child crying. I went to see, and it was the Mu family's youngest son, only ten or so, who'd crawled through the hole in the wall between the two houses. I opened the gate and pulled him in, and behind him came someone else, the Mu family's daughter-in-law, holding a smaller one in her arms. I pulled them in too."

The woman in the corner, who had kept her head bowed all the while, shrank into herself a little at this, and still did not raise her head.

"The second night, someone came pounding on the gate again. This time it was other families, several of them; I could tell—men's voices, and women's too. But there was no room left to stand in the back courtyard, people pressed against each other, not even room to lie down. Someone in the room took hold of me and said: open the gate again, and the people outside will see and all come running here, and the Uighur troops will come in after them, and then we're all finished." Here Old Jacob's voice sank. "So I didn't open. The pounding went on most of the night, and later, little by little, it stopped."

The room was silent a long time.

"The third day, the elders of the city got together ten thousand bolts of patterned silk and gauze and sent it to the Uighur camp, and only then did the Uighurs stop," said Old Jacob. "I opened the gate and went out to look. The Mu family's door stood open. Old Mu was sitting on the threshold, already cold. Their eldest daughter was gone; we looked everywhere afterwards, and never found her."

When he had finished he simply sat there, his two hands on his knees all the while. Yisi had not said a word from beginning to end. Jingjing closed the register, closed it hard, and pressed down on it with both hands, pressed till his fingers went white.

The woman in the corner stood up now and untied the bundle on her knees. In the bundle was a stack of flatbreads with jujube paste in them, wrapped in oiled paper; they had been twenty days on the road and had long gone hard. She said she had baked them herself. Her father-in-law used to bake this kind of bread, and Old Jacob would come every few days and buy some, and when he had bought it break it up and share it among the monastery's children. Her father-in-law was gone now, but she could still bake this bread. She had baked these on the way, and brought them for the priest in Chang'an who wrote the letter.

Yisi stood up and bowed deeply to her.

The woman started, and stepped back, but her back was already against the wall; not knowing what to do, she hastily pushed the bundle of bread into Yisi's hands, turned, and went out.

---

When everyone had gone, the courtyard emptied. The ground was covered with muddy footprints, layer on layer, all trampled together. In the pit was the new stone, and by the pit, the old one. The sky grew darker and darker, and toward evening it began to snow.

The snow came from the west, at first a speck at a time, as if someone up in the sky were sifting flour; later it came thicker and thicker, flake by flake, falling very slowly, very lightly, without the least sound.

Yisi stood alone at the edge of the old footings, watching the snow fall on the new stone, fall on the carved cross, fall on the cross's four leaves. When the snow fell on it, at first it melted, melted into little drops of water that soaked into the bluestone, and the wet bluestone turned black; later more fell than could melt, and it lay in a thin layer, white. Snow fell on the old stone beside it too, fell on the characters *Twelfth year of Zhenguan*, and filled the cuts in bit by bit.

Jingjing came out of the side room with a broom, went to the edge of the pit, and was going to sweep the snow off the stone. Yisi said: "Let it fall." So Jingjing leaned the broom against a wooden stake nearby, came over, and stood beside his Pa.

When they had stood a while, Jingjing spoke. He spoke very low, as if afraid someone would hear: "Pa, that letter Grandpa Jacob talked about—did you write it?"

Yisi said: "I wrote it."

"Then when you wrote the letter, you already knew the Uighurs were going to plunder Luoyang?"

"I knew," said Yisi. "When the army was outside Chang'an, everyone in camp knew."

Jingjing lowered his head and looked at the snow at his feet, looked a long time. His lips moved several times, as if a sentence were turning over at the edge of his mouth and would not come out. In the end he said it, his voice even lower than before: "And the Mu family? And those families that pounded on the gate that night?"

Yisi opened his mouth to say something, and did not say it.

Jingjing waited. He waited a long time, and Yisi never answered. The snow fell on father and son, on their shoulders and their hair, settled and melted and settled again. Over in the back courtyard Yonan was drawing water at the well; the windlass creaked a while, stopped, creaked again.

When it was full dark, Jingjing went back indoors. At the door of the side room he turned and looked back once. Yisi was still standing there.
