# Volume Five　Chang'an

## Chapter 21　The Yining Ward

> The hungry came and were fed; the cold came and were clothed.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

On the third day after Chang'an was recovered, the day before the grand army marched east, Yisi went to Guo Ziyi to ask for carts.

The wound on his face had not healed. The patch a little left of the middle of his brow, where the skin had been torn off, the surgeon had sewn with needle and thread, seven stitches, crooked, like a centipede lying there. Half his left eyebrow was gone. The two arrow wounds in his back and side were bound round and round with white cloth, bound so that he could not bend. The surgeon had told him to lie still a full month. He had lain two days.

Guo Ziyi was living in the main room of an empty mansion, and Yisi in the side wing. When Yisi went in, Guo Ziyi was eating gruel: millet gruel, a dish of pickles, a steamed bun. The room was piled with the document chests to be taken tomorrow, and guards went in and out tying them up. Guo Ziyi glanced at Yisi, and what he looked at was the centipede.

"You ought to be lying down."

"I have lain down," said Yisi.

Guo Ziyi said no more about it. He broke the bun and soaked it in the gruel, and said: "What do you want?"

"Carts."

"What for?"

"South of the city," said Yisi.

Guo Ziyi's chopsticks paused.

Thirty *li* south of the city, north of the Xiangji Temple, east of the River Feng, that stretch of fought-over farmland had still not been cleared. The Tang army's own dead the camps had gathered up, some of them, not all; the rebels' dead, nobody had gathered. The army marched tomorrow, and once it was gone, there would be nobody to gather them at all. There had been a few clear days; the sun had been on them, the wind blew north, and in the southern wards of the city you could smell it.

"How many?"

"As many as there are."

Guo Ziyi thought, and said: "You can have two. Old Duan goes with you. He's idle, and when he's idle he drinks."

He had paper and brush brought, wrote a warrant, stamped it with his seal, and handed it to Yisi.

"The camp will leave you twenty *dan* of rice. The men who bury have to eat," said Guo Ziyi. "When that's eaten, take this to the Regency Office. Whether they give, and how much, is out of my hands."

Yisi took the warrant and tucked it into his coat.

"When will Lord Guo be back?"

"When the Eastern Capital's taken."

Guo Ziyi lowered his head and went on with his gruel.

---

In fact Old Duan was not drinking. He was sitting in the doorway grinding his short knife on a whetstone; he had ground it all morning, the blade had long been sharp, and still he ground. When he heard it was south of the city, he slid the knife back into its sheath, stood up, and slapped the dust off his backside.

"Hauling the dead?"

"Mm."

"The rebels' too?"

"Those too."

Old Duan looked at Yisi once, said nothing, and went to yoke the carts.

Two carts, two oxen, four spades, a bundle of straw mats. The mats had been found in a half-burned shop in the West Market; their edges were scorched. Yisi could not bend, and to get up onto the cart he needed Old Duan to boost him. Holding him up by the arm, Old Duan said: "The state you're in, when we get there, are you going to haul the dead, or are the dead going to haul you?"

Yisi said: "The dead don't move."

Old Duan thought a moment and laughed, and when he laughed only half his face moved.

Out of the Mingde Gate they went south. In the fields on either side the wheat stubble still stood; no one had gathered the autumn harvest. A few farmhouses had fallen in, with marks of smoke black on their walls. A dog followed the carts a while, so thin you could count its ribs, followed as far as a ditch, and stopped, and sat there watching them.

When they reached the place, neither of them spoke.

---

Yisi had been back and forth across these fields several times, the first two in fog. What he remembered were the ditches. The first ditch, the second ditch, the third. He remembered the warm horse dung in the ditch, the ditch where Shi Six went down, the sluice-gate where Wei Three went down; remembered how the Uighurs, when they raised their cry, sounded like wolves.

He did not remember there being so many people on the ground.

In the fog you could not see. Now the fog had gone; the sky was very high, very blue, and a crow wheeled slowly overhead, wheeled once, came down, rose again. On the ground, some lay on their faces, some on their backs, some were only half there. Their armour had all been stripped. Who had stripped it, no one knew—probably farmers from round about, probably deserters, probably others who had lived. With the armour stripped, there was no telling which were Tang and which were rebels.

Old Duan squatted and looked at one dead man's face, and then at another.

"Can't tell," he said.

Yisi said: "Look at the hair."

Among the rebels there were many *hu* soldiers—Tongluo, Xi, Khitan—who plaited their hair differently. But among the Tang too there were *hu* soldiers—Uighurs, Tiele, Arabs—who plaited their hair differently again. Old Duan squatted there looking a while, stood up, and said: "Still can't tell."

Yisi thought, and said: "The ones we can tell, we bury apart. The ones we can't, we bury together."

"Buried together—would they want that?"

Yisi did not answer. He picked up a spade, stood at the edge of the first ditch, and began to dig.

He dug very slowly. With the cloth bound round his waist he could not bend, and could only work with his arms, shovelling down one spadeful at a time. Before he had dug many, the cloth on his back was wet, a small patch gone red. Old Duan saw, came over, took the spade off him, and said: "You haul. I'll dig."

So Yisi hauled.

Hauling meant bending too. He worked out a way: spread a straw mat on the ground, roll the man onto the mat, then take one end of the mat and drag it to the edge of the ditch. The mat rasped over the stubble. He walked very slowly, walked a stretch, stopped, took a breath, walked on.

On the first day, the two of them buried forty-one.

When they knocked off, Yisi looked once toward the east. At the north end of the third channel, by the sluice-gate, there was a crook-necked willow, and under the willow a stick of wood had been planted. He did not go over.

On the second day he went over. Two names were carved in the wood, carved very crooked. The upright stroke of the character *Wei* had been carved too long, through the top.

He stood a while, and came back and went on hauling.

---

On the second day, three people came.

They were farmers from south of the city: an old man and his two sons. The old man was past sixty, his back bent, every line of his face full of dirt, a wheat straw in his mouth. He stood on the ridge between the fields and watched a long time, watched Yisi haul and Old Duan dig, and then came over and asked: "Is the government paying?"

Old Duan said: "No."

The old man said "Ah," squatted down, bit off a length of the straw in his mouth, spat it out, and chewed the rest. He did not go. His two sons stood behind him, and they did not go either.

When he had chewed through the straw, the old man stood up, took a spade out of Old Duan's hands, and said: "You're digging that pit wrong. Too shallow. Too shallow and the dogs dig them out."

He dug a pit to show Old Duan. It was dug square and true, its four sides straight, as deep as his chest.

Old Duan looked at it and said: "You've done this before, old uncle?"

"I have," said the old man. "In the Kaiyuan years there was a pestilence in Guanzhong. I buried people then."

He did not say how many.

That day, the five of them buried a hundred and thirty.

On the third day a dozen came. On the fifth day, more than forty. Some were farmers, some were from the city, some were from no one knew where; they came, took up a spade, and did not talk. Yisi asked one young man where his home was. The young man said the Changle Ward. Yisi asked him why he had come. The young man said: my elder brother was with the rebels, they took him, I don't know where he died. I've come to look.

He came every day, and every day he looked. He looked for half a month, and did not find him.

With so many people, the work south of the city could not be left. Thirty *li* there and back, the city gates shut at dark, the ward gates too; no one could do the journey. The old man put up two straw sheds at the edge of the fields, the helpers slept in the sheds at night, and the rice Guo Ziyi had left was carted out to the sheds and boiled there.

Old Duan stayed south of the city. On the third day Yisi knocked off early, and before the drums for the closing of the gates had finished, went in by the Mingde Gate alone.

---

He went back to the Yining Ward.

The Yining Ward was in the west of the city, near the Kaiyuan Gate. In the ward there was a monastery, built by imperial command in the Zhenguan years. The Emperor Taizong had had his own portrait copied on to the monastery wall; later the Emperor Xuanzong had given the monastery a name-board in his own hand, and portraits of five former emperors. When Yisi came to Chang'an from the west, it was by this monastery's gate that he came in. He had lived here more than ten years; here he had been made a priest, here he had married, here he had had a son.

The name-board was gone. The portrait on the wall had been scraped with a knife, scraped until only a white patch was left. The portraits of the five sages were nowhere to be found. Over the gate a cross had once been carved, its four ends rounded like four leaves. Now the four leaves had been chiselled off, leaving only a cross-shaped hollow in the middle, with ash gathered in it.

Horses had been tethered in the courtyard; the ground was covered in dry dung. The main hall had half burned: on the east side the pillars still stood, on the west they had fallen, and there was a great hole in the roof through which the sky showed. In the back courtyard the jujube tree was still there, its leaves all fallen, its branches spread like an open hand. The well under the jujube tree was still there too.

Yisi drew up a bucket of water. The water was clear, and very cold.

He did not drink it. He poured it back into the well and stood by the well a long time.

When he left, three people had been left in the monastery: a deacon, a boy who read the lessons, an old man who kept the gate. In the back courtyard he found the room they had lived in. There was a straw pallet in it, with no one on it, the straw black and mouldy. In the corner lay a single worn-out shoe.

Then he went to the side room on the east.

The side room had not burned. Its door had been kicked in and hung askew. Along the wall inside there had stood a row of medicine cabinets, dozens of little drawers, on each drawer a slip of paper, Tang characters on one half, Syriac on the other. Now the cabinets lay on the floor, the drawers all pulled out and flung everywhere. Of what had been in them, whatever was worth anything had been taken, and whatever was worthless was strewn over the floor, mixed with dust and rat droppings, trodden to mush.

Yisi squatted and picked up a slip of paper. On one half was written *huanglian*, coptis root; on the other, a Syriac word. The hand was his. He had been in his twenties then, and had written better than he did now.

He tucked the slip into his coat and went to find the bottom row.

In the bottom row, the third drawer from the left was still in the cabinet; it had not been pulled out. The drawer was broken and would not pull; the rebels had probably pulled at it too, found it would not move, and given up. Yisi knew why it would not move. He reached behind the cabinet, felt over the back panel a while, found a wooden wedge, and drew it out.

The drawer came loose.

Under the bottom of the drawer was another bottom. He prised it up with his fingernail, and under it lay a small earthenware jar, the size of a fist, its mouth sealed with wax, and on the wax the print of a cross.

He took the jar in his hand and weighed it.

Still full.

---

Early the next morning, smoke rose at the monastery gate.

The pot had been found in the side room; there was a crack across its bottom, but it could still be used. The firewood was charred rafters, split, which crackled as they burned and gave off black smoke. The rice was five *dan* brought back from the sheds south of the city; Yisi and Old Duan had agreed that the people out there would go without one solid meal.

On the first day, only seven or eight came for gruel. They were all people of the ward who had not got away: the old, the sick, women with children. They stood outside the monastery gate and watched the pot from a distance, and did not dare come near. The chiselled hollow over the gate they could see; and the *hu* by the pot with a centipede sewn on his face, they could see him too.

Yisi ladled a bowl, carried it over, and held it out to the woman nearest him. She had a child in her arms; the child did not cry, did not move, its eyes open.

The woman took the bowl and fed the child first.

An old woman behind her had been staring at Yisi all this while. She stared a long time, shuffled two steps forward, and asked: "Are you... the *hu* master who used to treat the sick at the monastery?"

Yisi said: "I am."

The old woman said: "You saw to my old man's leg. Early in Tianbao."

Yisi thought a while, and asked: "By your door—there's a locust tree?"

The old woman nodded.

"The left leg," said Yisi. "He fell off a roof."

The old woman stood with her mouth open and did not shut it for a long time. Her surname was He; she was an old believer of the monastery, and when the city fell she had hidden in a cellar in the ward, hidden more than a year. She said that the deacon had been cut down by marauding soldiers at the monastery gate the day the city fell, and she and her old man had dragged him off at night and buried him; that the boy who read the lessons had gone with the refugees toward the Southern Mountains, and not come back; that the old gatekeeper had starved to death last winter.

Yisi heard her out, and said nothing.

Granny He turned and said a few words to the people behind. And slowly they came over. That afternoon she called two more old believers from the ward—an old man surnamed Mi and a widow surnamed Kang—who had both once come to worship at the monastery.

On the second day more than thirty came. On the third, more than a hundred.

---

One pot was not enough, and Yisi found another. Old Mi and Widow Kang helped watch the pots, and Granny He saw to the bowls. There were not enough bowls; when they had been emptied they were collected, washed at the well, and filled again.

Five *dan* of rice lasted five days. The rice south of the city was nearly gone too.

On the sixth day, with Guo Ziyi's warrant in his coat, Yisi went to the Regency Office.

At the Regency Office there were many people and much business, and everyone going in and out was in a hurry. The man in charge of the granary was a minor clerk surnamed Lu, forty or so, his fingers all ink. He read the warrant three times, turned it over and read the back, and when he had read it, said: "Lord Guo's warrant, I honour. But the Sage is coming back to the capital from Fengxiang, and the granary has to serve the welcoming of His Majesty first. The army, the officials, the palace—every one of them is more urgent than you. Gruel for a *hu* monastery isn't an item in the register."

Yisi said: "Then add an item."

Clerk Lu raised his head and looked at him, and then at the centipede on his face, and said: "Which camp are you with?"

"Lord Guo's."

"Were you at the Xiangji Temple?"

"I was."

Clerk Lu said nothing more. He lowered his head, leafed through a register a while, found a page, wrote a few characters with his brush, and when he had written them, said: "Ten *dan*. There's no more than that. Come again next month—maybe there'll be some, maybe not."

Yisi said: "Good."

Some days later, the Sage returned to Chang'an. That day the drums beat in the city all day, incense burned along the Vermilion Bird Avenue, and the street was full of kneeling people. There were fewer people at the monastery gate; they had all gone to watch. By evening they had all come back, and queued before the pots, a queue longer even than in the morning.

Ten *dan* of rice—five for the city, five for south of the city—lasted another ten days or so.

---

On the day the ten *dan* ran out, Yisi went to the West Market.

Half the West Market had burned; in the other half, some shops had opened their doors again. Yisi went into a silk shop, took a roll from his coat, and laid it on the counter. It was silk, ten bolts of it, white, folded very neat. After the Xiangji Temple, Guo Ziyi had privately given him these ten bolts, saying they were for him to recover on. He had never touched them.

Old Duan had come back from south of the city that day to fetch rice. He was following behind, saw, and put his hand flat on the roll of silk.

"Lord Guo gave you this to recover on."

"It's only sitting there," said Yisi.

"That face of yours," said Old Duan, "is what bought these ten bolts. Do you know that?"

"I know."

Old Duan kept his hand on the silk a while, and let go. He turned round and stood facing out of the shop door, and did not watch any more.

The owner of the shop was a Sogdian. He looked at the silk, and at Yisi, and named a price. Yisi did not bargain. These days a *dou* of rice was several hundred cash, and the money the ten bolts fetched would buy only two *dan* and a little more.

That evening, there were a few more sacks of rice at the monastery gate.

---

It turned cold.

At the end of the tenth month, the first snow fell on Chang'an. Of those who came for gruel some were barefoot, some had only a single layer of clothing; their lips were purple with cold, and the hands holding the bowls shook so that half the gruel spilled.

Old Duan, coming back from south of the city, began to bring a big basket.

Yisi asked him what was in the basket, and Old Duan said: "See for yourself."

Yisi lifted the lid and looked. It was clothes. Torn ones, filthy ones; some had dried blood on them, some had slashes from blades. They had been stripped from the dead south of the city, and when they were stripped, the men had already gone stiff.

Yisi put the lid back on.

That night Old Duan saw Yisi at the well in the back courtyard washing those clothes, one by one. The well water was very cold, and Yisi's hands were soaked red. He washed very slowly: washed one, wrung it out, hung it on a branch of the jujube tree. The jujube's branches were hung full, as if the tree had grown a whole tree's worth of leaves again—grey, brown, black.

The next day those clothes were on the backs of people at the monastery gate.

There was one robe with a few characters embroidered inside the collar: the mark of one of the rebel companies. A man took it, turned back the collar, looked once, would not put it on, and threw it back in the basket. Yisi picked it up and with a knife unpicked the few rows of embroidery stitch by stitch, unpicked it clean, and handed it to him again.

The man looked at the collar, and put it on.

Those who wore them knew where they came from. No one said.

The dead south of the city were buried, all of them, around the time of that snow. On the last day, Yisi rode Old Duan's horse out to the south of the city.

---

How many they had buried in all, Yisi had not counted. The old man had. The old man said: of the Tang, two thousand three hundred and some; of the rebels, a little over four thousand; of those they could not tell, a thousand and more. And there were some who could no longer be buried, and had only had earth heaped over them where they lay.

On the last day, when they knocked off, the old man sat on the ridge between the fields chewing a wheat straw. His two sons were patting the last pit flat at the edge of the ditch. Yisi sat beside him. Old Duan was washing spades a way off.

It was nearly dark. The sky in the west was red, red going purple. The crow still wheeled overhead.

The old man finished one straw and pulled another from the ground; he did not put it in his mouth, but held it pinched in his fingers, broke it, broke it again. He looked at the stretch of newly turned earth, looked a long time.

"*Hu* master," said the old man, "what house are you people?"

Yisi did not answer at once.

"I've heard folk in the city say," said the old man, "that that monastery of yours isn't a Buddhist temple, and isn't a Daoist one either. That thing carved over the door isn't the swastika."

"It's a cross," said Yisi.

"What's a cross?"

Yisi thought, and said: "It's where a man died."

The old man was taken aback, and turned his head to look at him.

"Died on a cross?"

"Mm. Nailed to the wood."

"That's how men who've committed a crime die."

"Mm."

The old man said nothing. He threw away the bits of straw in his hand, pulled up another, held it.

"You worship a man who died like that?"

Yisi said: "Afterwards he rose up again."

This time the old man was not taken aback. He looked at Yisi a while, his eyes narrowing, as if he were looking at a young man talking big.

"Rose up?"

"Rose up."

The old man lowered his head and looked at the new earth. The new earth was yellow, in clods, with wheat stubble still mixed in it. His two sons had finished patting the pit and stood waiting for him.

"Then these," said the old man. "Does he care for these?"

Yisi looked at the earth too.

"He does," he said.

"The Tang ones he cares for, and the rebels too?"

Yisi did not speak at once. The wind blew from the north, and drew the centipede on his face tight.

"They're all his," he said.

The old man asked nothing more. He stood up, nodded to Yisi, and went off with his two sons. When he had gone a few steps he turned his head back and said: "*Hu* master, that pot of yours in the city—you're short of firewood. I've got some. In a day or two I'll have one of the boys bring it to you."

Yisi said: "Good."

That night Yisi and Old Duan slept one night in the straw shed, and early the next morning took down the sheds and drove the carts back to the city.

---

After the snow, more people in the city fell sick.

First it was the flux. One, two, later a dozen a day. Not watery—with blood, with pus. Those who had it squatted at the foot of the monastery wall and could not get up, their faces grey, their eyes sunk.

Yisi looked at them one by one. He looked at faces, looked at tongues, felt bellies, pressed the pulse at the wrist. When he had looked at them all, he went to the open channel that ran through the ward.

The channel was led in from the south of the city; it passed through several wards before it flowed here, and its water was muddy, with grass floating in it, and rotten vegetable leaves. He squatted at the edge and smelled it: a rank smell he could not name. He followed the channel upstream, walked through two wards, and stopped beneath a stretch of fallen ward wall. At the edge of the channel lay a heap of dead horses, thrown there when the city fell and never cleared, lying in the water, soaked through and rotting.

He went back to the monastery gate, stood up on the stove platform, and said to the people: "From today, no one drinks the water from the channel. If you want to drink, come to the monastery and draw from the well. And well water too—don't drink it cold. Boil it first."

Someone asked: "Why?"

Yisi said: "Upstream there are dead horses lying in it."

The one who had asked said nothing more.

That night Old Duan helped him boil water all night. Boil a pot, ladle it into the vat to cool, boil another. Feeding wood into the stove, Old Duan asked: "Cold water, boiled water—is there such a difference?"

Yisi said: "You've seen Jingjing sneaking cold water from the vat at night."

Old Duan remembered. It had been the first time he went to eat at Yisi's house; the boy had broken wind right in front of him, and Yisi had asked whether he'd been sneaking cold water again, and said that in future, if he was thirsty at night, he was to get up and boil some.

"At the time," said Old Duan, "I thought you were scolding him for bad manners."

"It is a rule," said Yisi. "My Pa made it."

---

Those who had it badly Yisi would no longer let squat at the monastery gate.

The main hall had half burned, but the east half still had a roof. He had people sweep the east half clean and lay straw, moved everyone with the flux in there, and kept them apart from those who came for gruel. Those who came for gruel were not allowed in; what those inside ate and drank was brought separately from the monastery. Granny He and Widow Kang took turns keeping watch inside, and Old Mi saw to the boiling of the water.

"This sickness passes from one to another," Yisi said to Granny He. "Going in and coming out, you must all wash your hands."

Granny He did not know what *passes from one to another* meant, but she did as he said. She did it more carefully than anyone; when she washed her hands she dug even under her nails.

He found some earthenware bowls, one for each of the sick, and had them pass water into the bowls. When they had done, he picked up the bowl and held it to the light: looked at the colour, looked whether it was cloudy, looked whether anything had settled at the bottom, and what it looked like.

Old Duan stood in the doorway and stared.

"What are you looking at that for?"

"For the sickness."

Old Duan's mouth hung open a long time. He said: "You can doctor?"

Yisi put the bowl down.

"I can."

"Why didn't you say so before?"

"The army has its surgeons," said Yisi. "Lord Guo wanted me to find the way."

Old Duan stood in the doorway and thought a long time, and could think of nothing to say. At last he said: "You told me about that old soldier at Changshan, the one called Du De, who changed your dressings for twenty days. If you can do it yourself, why didn't you change them yourself?"

"I couldn't lift my hands," said Yisi.

---

He went to the West Market for medicines.

The West Market's medicine trade had once had a whole street of its own: the Tang apothecaries on the east side, the *hu* apothecaries on the west. Now three shops on the east side had their doors open, and on the west side, not one.

At a shop on the east side kept by a man surnamed Wang, Yisi asked for coptis, for pulsatilla, for dried ginger, naming each and its weight in turn. Shopkeeper Wang was fifty or so, and saw patients in his shop as well. Watching Yisi name the medicines without looking at the labels on the cabinet, he stood behind the counter and looked at him a long while, and asked: "What are you treating?"

"Flux. With blood."

The steelyard in Shopkeeper Wang's hand stopped.

"Flux with blood is hot flux," he said. "Coptis, pulsatilla—right. And you're putting in dried ginger?"

"They're eating cold gruel and sleeping on cold ground," said Yisi. "There's heat within, and cold without."

Shopkeeper Wang thought a moment, and said nothing. He weighed out the dried ginger and wrapped it, and then put out his hand and said: "Let me see your wrist."

Yisi held out his wrist. Shopkeeper Wang laid three fingers on it, at the three places of the pulse, pressed a while, and let go.

"You ought to be lying down yourself," said Shopkeeper Wang.

"I have lain down."

Shopkeeper Wang smiled, took a handful of something from the cabinet, wrapped it in paper, and pushed it into Yisi's hands.

"Smoked plums," he said. "For the ones who have it worst, steep them in water to drink. No charge."

Back at the monastery Yisi made up the medicines and boiled them, dose by dose. The decoctions he carried into the east half of the main hall with his own hands and fed them, bowl by bowl. When he had fed them, he had a bowl of rice water put by each one's head, with a little salt in it.

Granny He asked him what the salt was for.

Yisi said: "That's how my Pa fed them."

---

Among those with the flux was the child of the woman who had held it in her arms.

The child was not yet two. It had had the flux three days, and by the end there was nothing coming but a little bloody water. Its lips were so dry they were peeling, its eyes half open; it did not cry, did not move. When they fed it rice water, it went in and ran out again at the corners of its mouth.

The woman sat on the straw holding the child, not moving. She did not weep, and did not beg; she only looked at Yisi.

Yisi squatted in front of her and looked at the child a long time.

Then he got up, went to the side room on the east, and brought back the small earthenware jar.

He broke the wax at the mouth of the jar a little, and with a thinly whittled sliver of bamboo picked out a little of what was inside. It was blackish brown, like a lump of paste. He measured it again and again against the lamp on the bamboo sliver, scraped a little off, scraped a little more, until what remained was the size of a grain of rice.

Shopkeeper Wang had come that day to bring smoked plums. He stood in the doorway, saw the jar, and asked: "What's that?"

"*Diyejia*." Theriac.

Shopkeeper Wang had heard of it. "It's in the *Materia Medica*," he said. "'Comes from the Western Rong.' The book says it's made by boiling down the galls of various creatures, that it looks like a pill gone bad with age, red-black in colour, that the *hu* sometimes bring it, and it's very precious."

"It isn't galls," said Yisi. "What matters most in it is a thing you here call *minang*. We take its sap."

Shopkeeper Wang frowned. *Minang* was the poppy, a flower grown in gardens; its seeds could be eaten, cooked in gruel. He had never heard of its sap being used as medicine.

"That stuff cures flux?"

"It quiets the belly," said Yisi. "And it makes a man sleep. Give too much, and he doesn't wake."

Shopkeeper Wang said nothing more, and looked at the grain-of-rice thing in his hand.

Yisi dissolved it in half a spoonful of warm water, raised the child's head, and fed it in a little at a time. When he had fed it, he laid the child down flat.

That night the child slept, slept very deep. Yisi kept watch half the night, every little while feeling for the child's breath under its nose. The next day the child still had the flux, but thinner; when the rice water went in, a little of it was swallowed. On the third day, a little more.

On the fifth day the child opened its eyes, looked at its mother, and gave a cry.

Only then did the woman, holding the child, weep.

Shopkeeper Wang came by again later, looked at the child, said nothing, and looked at the earthenware jar again. Yisi had already sealed it up once more.

Shopkeeper Wang said: "*Hu* master, from now on, whatever medicine you're short of here, come to my shop and take it. Money—if you have it, give it; if not, I'll put it on the slate."

---

That month more than a hundred people with the flux lay in the east half of the main hall. Eleven died. Of the eleven, two were ones Yisi had given the theriac.

The eleven who died, Yisi and Old Duan carried to the south of the city and buried at the edge of those fields.

In the middle of the eleventh month, Guo Ziyi came back from the Eastern Capital.

Yisi went to the mansion to thank him, but Guo Ziyi had gone to court and was not there. Three days later a cartload of rice came from the camp, with a warrant stuck on the cart. The warrant said nothing else; it had only the amount of rice written on it, twenty *dan*, and beneath it Guo Ziyi's seal.

---

Yisi's own wounds had never quite healed. The one at his waist was all right; but the one on his back, where the arrowhead had had a barb and been dug out deep—after half a month and more of hauling the dead and more than a month of carrying loads, it kept healing and breaking open, breaking open and healing again. He could not reach it himself, so he had Old Duan change the dressing for him.

The first time, Old Duan stood behind him holding the cloth, not knowing where to begin.

"First soak the old cloth soft with warm water," said Yisi, lying on his front on the pallet. "Peel it off a little at a time. What won't come off, don't force it. Soak it again."

Old Duan did as he said. As he peeled it, Yisi made no sound; it was Old Duan who bared his teeth.

"Wash it with salt water. When it's clean, sprinkle on the powder. Then bind on new cloth," said Yisi. "Not too tight—too tight and the blood won't move. Not too loose either—too loose and it'll slip."

Old Duan did as he said. When he had bound it, he straightened up and looked at his two hands.

"When Du De changed your dressings," he said, "was that how you taught him too?"

"No," said Yisi. "He was an old soldier, he'd been changing dressings for twenty years. He taught me."

Old Duan said nothing more. He rolled up the cloth that was left, put it back in the basket, and sat on the edge of the pallet, sat a long time.

---

More and more people came.

The burying south of the city was done, and people from the city still came. From the ward, from other wards, people who had fled outside the city and come back to find their homes gone. More than three hundred a day, then more than four hundred. Two pots, boiling from before light until dark: boil a pot, ladle out a pot, and when it was ladled out, there were still people queuing behind.

One day those at the back of the queue saw the pot was nearly empty and pushed forward. Those in front were shoved and pitched forward against the stove platform. The platform was a few bricks stacked up; at the shove it tilted, and a whole pot of boiling gruel nearly went over. Old Duan threw himself at it and held up the rim of the pot with his shoulder, held it up with his teeth bared and his face twisted.

An old woman was knocked down, fell on the ground, and those behind trod over her. Yisi came round from behind the stove and dragged her out. One of her hands had been trodden on and was swollen, purple, the fingers would not bend.

That evening Yisi first put a splint on the old woman's hand, then went to look at Old Duan's shoulder. A patch of blisters had come up on it, glistening.

"Don't prick them," said Yisi. "Soak it in cold water."

Old Duan soaked his shoulder in a basin of well water, soaking it till he shivered. He said: "This won't do."

"Mm."

"One man can't see to it all."

"Mm."

The next day Yisi set a rule at the monastery gate: one ladle each, no more, no less; the old, the sick and those with children first, the able-bodied after. Granny He, Old Mi and Widow Kang stood among the people in the queue, one watching each stretch, and whoever pushed was sent to the back.

There was still pushing, but less.

In the twelfth month, the Metropolitan Prefecture put up gruel sheds in the East and West Markets too. More than half of those at the monastery gate went off there, leaving a hundred-odd, all old faces from the ward. And these hundred-odd, slowly, learned to queue in order.

---

That evening, the Sogdian owner of the silk shop in the West Market came to the monastery to bring the last of the money for the silk, and mentioned one thing in passing. He said that someone at his shop had come back from Luoyang and said that the *hu* monastery in Luoyang's Xiushan Ward had been burned when the rebels held the city, its people scattered, no one knew where.

Yisi asked: "Not one of them left?"

"They say there's still one old one," said the shopkeeper, "keeping watch over the heap of ash, who won't leave."

Yisi said nothing. He thought of the monastery in Lingzhou, whose roof leaked every year, and whose elder said every year that it must be mended, and every year could not afford to mend it.

---

That night, Yisi did not go in at once.

He stood at the monastery gate and looked up at the chiselled hollow over the gate. The ash in the hollow had been soaked by snow-water and turned into a black stain that ran down the doorpost.

The ward was very dark. The city had been recovered for nearly three months, and still not many households lit lamps.

Yisi went in, searched the side room a while, and found a small oil dish, one the monastery had once used for lamps before the altar, bronze, gone green with verdigris. He wiped it with his sleeve, poured in a little lamp oil, rolled a wick, and laid it in.

He took his flint from his coat and struck it three times before it caught.

The flame was very small, the size of a bean, tilting this way and that in the wind. He cupped it in his hands, cupped it a while, until the flame steadied.

He set the lamp on the steps at the monastery gate, below the lintel, right beneath that cross-shaped hollow.

Then he went in.
