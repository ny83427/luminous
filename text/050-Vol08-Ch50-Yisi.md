# Volume Eight　The Daqin Monastery

## Chapter 50　Yisi

> Among the pure and upright *tarsa*, such goodness was never heard of; among the white-robed Luminous scholars, now we see the man.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

In the tenth month of the fourth year of Jianzhong, Chang'an fell into turmoil once more.

The troops of Jingyuan were passing through Chang'an on their way east to fight. The court feasted the army, and what it gave them was coarse food. The soldiers began to curse, and cursing, rebelled, and fought their way in from the east of the city right into the palace city. The Son of Heaven fled by the north gate with a few men and ran to Fengtian. The mutineers set up a man named Zhu Ci as emperor.

Chang'an was lost for eight months.

In those eight months, the gate of the Daqin monastery in the Yining Ward stayed shut. Shut by day, shut by night. The mutineers came twice. The first time seven or eight of them came, battering the gate, demanding money. Yisi opened the gate and stood in the gateway, and behind him stood Sargis's son and the few old monk-soldiers who were left, holding those shoulder-high staves shod with iron at both ends. Yisi held nothing in his hands. He was fifty-eight that year, his hair all white, his back still straight.

He said a few words to the mutineers. What he said, the people of the monastery afterwards each told differently. The mutineers looked at him, looked at the staves behind him, looked at the stele in the front courtyard, and went.

The second time more of them came. This time Yisi did not open the gate. The mutineers cursed outside the gate a while, threw a bucket of dung over the gate, and went too.

That small lamp at the gate was not hung outside in those eight months. Every day at dark Yisi lit it and set it inside the gate, at the foot of the crack between the leaves. A thread of light leaked out through the crack; anyone outside not looking for it would not see it.

In the fifth month of the first year of Xingyuan, Li Sheng's troops fought their way back. Zhu Ci fled, and the Son of Heaven returned. The monastery gate was opened. Yisi took a broom and scraped the dung off the gate, then drew several buckets of well water and sluiced it down three times.

When it was sluiced, he hung the lamp back on its iron hook.

---

That autumn, Yisi fell ill.

First his right shoulder. That wound had been left at Changshan twenty-eight years before, the blade cutting into the bone. All these years it had ached in wet weather, ached twenty-odd years, and he had made nothing of it. That autumn there was much rain, and his right shoulder ached so that he could not raise it, could not even get his robe on. Jingjing dressed him: first the left sleeve, then slowly working the right arm in.

Then his side. That arrow at Xiangji Temple had gone in on the left; the head had been taken out, the wound had healed, but there was always a hard lump inside. That autumn the lump swelled, red and hot, and if it was pressed he broke into a cold sweat.

Then the fever came.

The physician came and prescribed medicine. When the medicine was drunk the fever went down two days, then rose again. Once it rose, it did not go down. Yisi lay in bed, in his room in the little courtyard east of the monastery. On the wall hung the small round shield, and under the shield two wooden pegs, one with the curved sword on it, one with the short mace. These things had hung there many years, and no one had touched them.

Jingjing moved into his room.

By day he went to the scripture room as usual, to the front hall, saw to the monastery's affairs, and whenever he had a moment ran back. At night he slept on the floor beside Yisi's bed on a spread felt. If Yisi coughed in the night, he woke. If Yisi turned over, he woke too.

Yisi said to him: "Go back and sleep."

Jingjing said: "Mm."

He still slept on the floor.

---

One night Yisi woke and called Jingjing.

Jingjing got up and lit the lamp. Yisi lay there, his face burning red, but his eyes very bright. He looked at Jingjing, looked a while, and said: "The cloth bundle in the cupboard."

Jingjing looked in the cupboard. There was a cloth bundle in it, wrapped in several layers; he opened it, and it was a bronze basin, old, with a dent in its rim.

"Lord Guo gave it," said Yisi. "It goes with me."

Jingjing held the basin and looked at it. He knew Lord Guo had sent it before he died; three years it had sat in the cupboard, and his father had never once taken it out to use. He did not know what story it had. His father had never said.

"Goes with you?"

"Put it beside me."

Jingjing wanted to ask why. He looked at his father, and did not ask. He wrapped the basin up again and set it at the head of the bed.

Yisi looked at the bundle, and seemed at ease. He closed his eyes, and after a while opened them again.

"Is the road still open?" he asked.

Jingjing was taken aback.

These words he had heard his uncle ask in Ctesiphon. He had been ten then, standing before a man with only one arm, his father's letter clutched in his hand. He remembered how he had answered.

"It's open," said Jingjing. "I've walked it."

Yisi looked at him. Looked a long time, and then the corner of his mouth moved.

"Good," he said.

He closed his eyes again.

That night he said nothing more. Near dawn, Jingjing heard his breathing grow longer and longer, slower and slower. A breath drawn in, and a long while before it went out. Jingjing knelt by the bed holding his hand, waiting.

The last breath went out, and was not drawn in again.

Jingjing knelt there holding that hand, knelt a long time. The hand was very big; once it could cover the whole back of his head.

The window paper turned white.

Jingjing stood up, went out of the courtyard, through the front courtyard, to the monastery gate. He took down the small lamp at the gate, blew it out, and hung it back.

It was the first thing his father did every morning.

---

It was Narsai who prepared him for burial.

Narsai was nearly seventy that year, his back bent, his brow ridges as high as ever. All these years he had been doing what Yisi had sentenced him to: taking orphans, gathering the dead. How many unclaimed dead in Chang'an he had gathered—frozen, starved, dead of sickness by the roadside—he had never counted.

That morning he came and stood at the gate of the little courtyard. Jingjing saw him, was taken aback, and stood aside.

Narsai drew a basin of water, warm. He undid Yisi's clothes one by one, took a cloth, dipped it in the water, and began to wipe him from the head down.

He wiped very slowly.

He wiped the brow. A little left of the middle of the brow was a scar, the skin over it puckered, like a centipede. That was the third arrow at Xiangji Temple, the arrow Yisi pulled out himself, the skin he tore off himself.

He wiped the right shoulder. On the right shoulder a scar, deep and long, and under the scar a ridge could be felt in the bone. That was Changshan, the twelve riders, twenty-eight years ago, the blade cutting to the bone.

He wiped the left ribs. On the left ribs a round scar, a spear thrust. The same time too.

He turned Yisi over and wiped his back. On the back a scar, where the arrow went in through the gap between shield and armour. On the side another, the left, its swelling not yet gone down. Xiangji Temple, the first and second runs east, when he came back with two arrows in his back and still would not dismount.

Narsai wiped these scars, one after another. His hands were very steady, as steady as when he had cut paper and trimmed pens in the old days.

When he had finished, he put the cloth in the basin; the water had gone cloudy. He took up the basin, went out into the courtyard, and threw the water at the foot of the wall.

Then he came back and dressed Yisi in a clean white robe.

Jingjing stood at the door watching all the while. When Narsai had finished he walked past him to the courtyard gate, stopped a moment, and did not turn round.

"He judged rightly," said Narsai.

He went.

---

The funeral was on the third day.

A great many came that day, more even than on the day the stele was raised.

Everyone at the monastery came. The three elders Azarel, Marya and Samaan stood at the very front; behind them those still left of the seven who had come east from Ctesiphon in those days—Babai and Sabrisho were both there, their hair all white, some leaning on sticks. Behind them the priests, deacons, monks, and the believers.

The people of the ward came too. Those who came every day for bread under the locust tree at the gate, the vinegar seller, the shoe seller, the man with the *hu* bread oven, crowded at the courtyard gate.

Xu Jing came. When he came in at the gate, he said nothing. Everyone at the monastery glanced at him. They had known him thirty years, and it was the first time they had seen him come in at the gate without talking.

And there were some whom the people of the monastery did not know. A few old soldiers, some lame, some missing an arm, men of the Shuofang army of those days, standing by the courtyard wall. Someone had come from the Guo household too, that grandson who had brought the basin; he stood beside the old soldiers, very properly.

And there was a woman.

She stood outside the courtyard gate, under the locust tree, and did not come in. She was nearly sixty, in a half-worn blue cotton gown, her hair combed very neatly. She simply stood there, her two hands clasped before her, looking in.

Jingjing saw her.

He looked at her once, and she looked at him. The two of them, with a courtyard between them, with a courtyard full of people between them, looked at each other a while.

Jingjing moved his eyes away. He turned and went to the front of the coffin.

---

The coffin rested in the front courtyard, before the stele.

Jingjing stood at the head of the coffin, facing the people.

He was to lead the funeral. The elder Azarel had said: it's yours. He did not decline.

First he recited the prayers in Syriac. When he had finished, he stopped and looked at the courtyard full of people. Those in front could understand Syriac; those behind could not. He looked at those behind, looked at the people under the locust tree at the gate.

Then he spoke in Tang.

At table, speak Tang. His father had said so.

He spoke not fast, like his father, saying each word clear.

He said: Father was born in Balkh. That is very far away—further than Xizhou, further than the Pamirs. Grandfather was called Milis, and was a priest there. Father came to Chang'an with a caravan at seventeen; to this year, forty-two years.

He said: Father married a Sogdian woman; they were married nine years, and she died of sickness. Father never married again.

He said: in the winter of the fourteenth year of Tianbao, on the bank of the Yellow River, a caravan was set upon. Father, one man, one shield, one mace, knocked down five. Killed not one. He told a child to hide under a cart and count the horses, and when he'd counted them clearly, he wouldn't be afraid.

He said: that child was me.

The courtyard was very quiet.

He said: afterwards Father followed Lord Director Guo. Lord Director Guo said: I don't want you to kill men; I want you to watch the road. So Father went and watched the road. From Shuofang to Hebei, from Hebei to Fengxiang, from Fengxiang to Chang'an. In Hebei he went alone to carry a letter to Changshan, and on a stretch of riverbed met twelve rebel riders. Twelve, and not one got away. That was the first time he killed a man. The cut on his right shoulder went to the bone.

He said: there was a man called Du De, a soldier at sixteen, who changed Father's dressings for twenty days at Changshan. Later in Hebei he held the rear; he said he'd hold for the time it takes to drink a cup of tea, and he held for a cup of tea. When he died, his hand and the hilt of his sword were glued together with blood and could not be parted, and they buried him with the sword. Father brought back his wine bowl, with a pinch of earth in it. That bowl is still in the monastery's cupboard, with Father's bowl and mine.

He said: there was a man called Old Duan, with a sword scar across his face, and when he laughed only half his face moved. He took a ten-year-old boy to Ctesiphon, and brought the boy's journal back into Father's hands. Later at Xiangzhou he took a crossbow bolt for Father. Before he died he asked Father a question, and Father had no time to answer.

He said: in the ninth month of the second year of Zhide, at Xiangji Temple, in thick fog, Father went east into the fog three times. The first time, the two men he took with him did not come back. The second time he took an arrow in the back and one in the side, came back and reported the ambush, and led the men round again. The third time he took an arrow in the face. He pulled the arrow out himself, and tore off the flap of skin hanging over his eye. He said: the road is open.

By the courtyard wall, one of the old soldiers bowed his head.

He said: afterwards the Son of Heaven granted Father the purple robe and gave him several titles—Grand Master of the Golden Seal and Purple Ribbon, Acting Director of the Palace Administration, Associate Deputy Military Governor of Shuofang. With the stipends of those titles Father bought this stele. The purple robe he wore once a year, at the New Year.

He said: Father spoke little. Of what Father said, I remember not much.

He paused.

"When I was little," said Jingjing, "once at supper I broke wind. In front of Father. I said: Pa, it's shameful."

Someone in the courtyard stood stunned.

"Father said," said Jingjing, "what shames a man is a lie, not wind."

When he had said it, he said no more.

The courtyard was silent a while. Then someone at the back laughed once, and hurriedly covered his mouth. Then someone else laughed, very softly, holding it in. And laughing, someone wept. Xu Jing stood in the crowd with his mouth open, tears running down in streams, unable to say a word.

Jingjing stood at the head of the coffin, and neither laughed nor wept.

---

When the body was laid in the coffin, Jingjing put that bronze basin in, at his father's right hand.

He also took down the short mace from the wall. Its head was no bigger than a fist, iron, chased with fine grooves. He laid the mace at his father's left hand.

The round shield and the curved sword he kept, and hung back on the wall.

The coffin was carried by eight men. Priests of the monastery, old monk-soldiers, and two of the old soldiers who walked over from the courtyard wall by themselves—no one asked them, and they did not ask—and put their shoulders under the poles.

Sargis's son was at the very front. He was too tall; the pole on his shoulder rode a good way higher than the others', and the whole coffin tilted back. As he carried it out of the monastery gate he ducked his head. When he reached the locust tree he suddenly began to weep, weeping like a child, weeping as he walked, and his feet did not miss a step.

As the coffin passed under the locust tree, the woman in the blue cotton gown stepped back and made way.

---

The burial ground was west of the city, outside the Jinguang Gate, where the people of the monastery were all buried. Yonan was there too.

The day of the burial was overcast, and it did not rain. The earth was yellow, and soft.

When the earth was filled in, the people slowly dispersed. The old soldiers went, the people of the ward went, the Guo grandson bowed with clasped hands and went too. As Xu Jing went he looked back once, and at last opened his mouth and said one thing: "He owes me a bowl of mutton soup." When he had said it, he himself did not know what it meant, and went.

At the end only a few of the monastery were left.

Jingjing stood before the grave, stood a long time.

The elder Azarel came up beside him. The elder was old, his back more bent than ever, leaning on a stick. He stood beside Jingjing looking at the heap of new earth, and stood a long time too.

Then he said: "From today, you are in charge."

Jingjing said nothing.

Azarel patted his shoulder, and went off leaning on his stick.

---

When Jingjing got back to the monastery, it was nearly dark.

He went first to his father's room. The room was empty. The bedding had been taken from the bed; the small round shield and the curved sword still hung on the wall, and of the two wooden pegs under the shield, one was empty.

He opened the cupboard. In the cupboard were two bowls, one stacked on the other: one his, with a little chip in its rim; one Du De's, with a notch in its mouth and a pinch of earth in it.

He went back to the table and picked up his father's bowl. It was the bowl his father had eaten from for decades, its bottom worn white. He put it in the cupboard, beside the other two.

Three bowls.

He shut the cupboard door, went out of the room, through the front courtyard, past the stele, to the monastery gate.

It was dark. In the ward, lamps came on house by house.

Jingjing took down the small lamp from its iron hook by the gate. He tipped out the dregs of last night's oil, wiped it clean with a cloth, ladled in a spoonful of new oil, trimmed the wick, and cut it even.

Then he struck the flint, and lit the lamp.
