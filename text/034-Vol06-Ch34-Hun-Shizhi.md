# Volume Six　The Shuofang Army

## Chapter 34　Hun Shizhi

> That wind and rain should come in their season, the world be at peace, men be orderly and things be pure, the living prosper and the dead be at rest.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

In the winter of the first year of Guangde, the Tibetans entered Chang'an.

They came from the west, and no one stood in their way all the road. His Majesty left the city and went east to Shanzhou. The Tibetans stayed in Chang'an fifteen days, burned some houses, plundered some things, set up an emperor, and then left.

The man who drove them out was Guo Ziyi. Guo Ziyi had had no troops to begin with; he gathered up a few thousand scattered soldiers on the spot, made a great show of banners and drums outside the city, and at night had fires lit all over the hills and drums beaten. The Tibetans thought a great Tang army had come, and struck camp and went west.

In the first month of the following year, the edict came down sending Guo Ziyi to Hezhong to take command of the old Shuofang troops.

When the Shuofang troops in Hezhong heard, they ran to tell each other. Over at Fenzhou, the men in Huai'en's camp grew fewer night by night. Ten left today, a hundred tomorrow, and all who left went south, to Hezhong, to Guo Ziyi.

Huai'en's son, leading troops at Yuci, was killed by his own men.

Huai'en took the few hundred horse he had left about him, crossed the Yellow River, and went north.

North was Lingzhou.

---

At Lingzhou in the second month, the ice on the river was beginning to melt.

When the ice melts, it makes a sound. Lying awake at night you could hear it from across the river, a *dong*, and after a while another *dong*, dull, as if someone a long way off were beating a broken drum. The people of Lingzhou called it the opening of the river. After the river opens, it is spring.

That year Hun Shizhi was forty-nine.

His beard was half white, and his left ear was still half missing. He was Acting Military Governor of Shuofang; Guo Ziyi had gone, Huai'en had gone, and the city of Lingzhou had been left to him to hold. He had a few thousand men under him, most of them old soldiers who, like him, had lived half their lives in this city.

Every morning when he got up he rode once round the city wall.

At the west of the city, he passed a side courtyard. Its gate was shut, the lock rusted fast. In the courtyard was a jujube tree whose branches reached out over the wall. A few steps further on was a small church, its gate shut too; a wooden board had once hung on its lintel, and the board had long since disappeared.

Last winter there had not been enough firewood, and a few soldiers had wanted to pull the little church down and burn its beams. They had brought a ladder and just climbed to the top of the wall when Hun Shizhi rode up.

"Come down," he said.

The soldiers came down. One of the bolder ones grumbled: "The *hu* monks have been gone years. What's the use of keeping this wreck? Can't burn it, can't live in it."

Hun Shizhi looked at him from the saddle.

"When your pa dies," he said, "will you pull down your family's house and burn it for firewood?"

The soldier opened his mouth, and dared say no more.

"There are two rotten stables north of the city," said Hun Shizhi. "Go and pull those down. This one, nobody touches. Anyone who touches one rafter, I'll take one of his bones."

The soldiers went off with their ladder on their shoulders.

Hun Shizhi did not go at once. He dismounted and sat a while on the step at the church gate. Dry jujube leaves had fallen on the step; he pushed them about a little with the toe of his boot, pushed them to one side, and only then mounted and went back.

---

One day in the middle of the second month, around the hour of *si*, a horseman came to the north gate.

His horse was lathered with sweat, and he was covered in dust. He reined in under the gate, craned his neck up and shouted: "Military Governor Pugu returns to his command! Tell Acting Governor Hun to open the gate and receive him!"

A soldier on the gate tower ran down to bring the news.

Hun Shizhi was at his residence eating. When he had heard it he put down his bowl and said nothing. He wiped his hands and sent for several of his officers. And he said: "Have Zhang Shao come too."

---

Zhang Shao was Hun Shizhi's nephew.

Hun Shizhi had had a younger sister who had married a Han man named Zhang in Lingzhou. The Zhang husband died young, and the sister died young too, leaving a child, who came into Hun Shizhi's tent at eight and grew up with Hun Jian. Hun Jian shot the bow, and so did he, though not as straight as Hun Jian. Later Hun Jian went to war with Guo Ziyi, and he stayed in Lingzhou with his uncle.

That year he was about thirty, quick of speech and quick of mind; the city's accounts, its grain and fodder, its army pay, were all in his charge.

They sat down in the hall.

Hun Shizhi said: "Pugu Huai'en wants to enter the city."

No one spoke.

Hun Shizhi said: "At Fenzhou he had tens of thousands. Now he comes back with a few hundred. A few hundred can't take Lingzhou; he knows that better than I do. What he wants isn't this city. It's the few thousand soldiers in it."

An old officer nodded.

Hun Shizhi said: "I'm thinking of shutting the gate and letting him drink the wind outside."

Zhang Shao spoke.

"Uncle," he said, "shutting the gate won't do."

Hun Shizhi looked at him.

"He's the Military Governor," said Zhang Shao. "Uncle is Acting Governor. Has the court issued an edict saying his office is stripped? No. His seal is still in his hands. A Military Governor comes back to his own command, and the Acting Governor shuts the gate and won't let him in—when that reaches Chang'an, what will the men in Chang'an say?"

He answered himself: "The men in Chang'an will say Lingzhou has rebelled."

Again no one in the hall spoke.

"Besides," said Zhang Shao, "he has only a few hundred, and there are several thousand in the city. If he comes in, what can he do? Uncle quarters his men outside the city and lets him bring only a few in; with no troops in his hands, he's a Military Governor with nothing but a stick."

Hun Shizhi did not answer. He sat there with his two hands pressed on his knees, looking at the floor before the hall. The floor was paved with blue bricks, and in the cracks between the bricks a little dry grass grew.

After a long while he said: "Send two men out to see how many he has behind him."

---

The men sent out did not come back until past noon.

They said Military Governor Pugu had camped thirty *li* to the north, not many men, three hundred-odd horse, the horses all thin, a good many men wounded. No baggage carts, no siege gear. Military Governor Pugu himself was not wearing armour; he was sitting on a stone at the camp gate eating a dry flatbread.

Hun Shizhi asked: "Did he see you?"

"He saw us. He even had water sent out to us."

Hun Shizhi sat a while longer.

He stood up and said: "Open the gate. His men stay outside; he brings only twenty horse in with him. I'll go to the gate to meet him."

And he said: "Tomorrow."

---

That night Hun Shizhi called Zhang Shao to his room.

A brazier was burning in the room. Hun Shizhi sat beside it with a jug of wine and two bowls before him. He poured two bowls and pushed one across to Zhang Shao.

Zhang Shao picked it up and drank a mouthful.

Hun Shizhi did not drink. He looked at the brazier, and after a while said: "You can write."

"I can."

"Write a letter to Jian for me."

Zhang Shao fetched paper and brush, spread them on the low table, ground ink, and waited with brush raised.

Hun Shizhi thought a long time, thought until his brows drew together. He had fought all his life and written few letters.

"All's well in the city," he said.

Zhang Shao wrote it.

"The river's opened."

Zhang Shao wrote it.

Hun Shizhi thought a long time again, thought until a piece of charcoal in the brazier fell in, before he said: "That bow of yours from when you were little—I've still got it, hanging on the wall of the east wing. When the war's over, come back and get it."

Zhang Shao wrote it. When he had written it he looked up, waiting for the next line.

Hun Shizhi waved his hand and said: "That's all."

Zhang Shao folded the letter, put it on the corner of the table, and said: "First thing tomorrow I'll have someone take it to Hezhong."

Hun Shizhi nodded. He picked up his own bowl of wine and drank it off in one, and poured himself another.

"When your ma went," he said, "you were eight. She held my hand and told me to look after you."

Zhang Shao kept his head bowed and said nothing.

"I've looked after you twenty years and more," said Hun Shizhi. "You're better at accounts than Jian."

He laughed once, drank off the second bowl too, stood up, clapped Zhang Shao on the shoulder and said: "Go to bed. We've an early start tomorrow. Tomorrow in the gate passage, you stand on my left. You've sharp eyes; watch his men's hands for me."

Zhang Shao said: "Yes."

Zhang Shao stood up and went out.

The letter lay on the corner of the table all night.

---

The next day at the hour of *si*, the north gate of Lingzhou opened.

The wall at the north gate was very thick, the gate passage very long, some twenty paces. Inside the passage it was dark and both ends were bright; standing in the middle, ahead of you was a blank of white and behind you a blank of white too. The wind poured in from outside the city, through the passage, moaning.

Hun Shizhi stood in the gate passage, at the city end.

He wore his armour, but no helmet. Beside him stood twenty of his guards. Zhang Shao stood on his left, half a step behind.

The hoofbeats outside the city drew near.

First two horsemen came in, then Huai'en.

Huai'en rode a grey horse, very thin, its ribs showing one by one. He himself was thinner too, his face longer than before, his cheeks hollowed, his cheekbones standing higher. He wore an old robe, no armour, a sword at his belt. Behind him came a dozen-odd horse, all his guards, every one grey with dust.

He reined in in the middle of the gate passage and dismounted.

The two men stood facing each other ten-odd paces apart.

Huai'en looked at Hun Shizhi, those long narrow eyes narrowed, as they always had been, as if behind a veil.

"General Hun," said Huai'en, "you've grown old."

"So have you," said Hun Shizhi.

Huai'en smiled a little. The smile was still only at the corners of his mouth.

"My son is dead," he said.

"I heard."

"Killed by his own men," said Huai'en. "Men who'd followed him several years."

Hun Shizhi said nothing.

Huai'en walked forward a few steps, up to Hun Shizhi. He looked at Hun Shizhi, and glanced past him behind.

Hun Shizhi said: "Your men camp outside the city. I'll have grain and fodder sent out to them. You'll lodge in the east wing of the residence; I've had it made ready."

Huai'en nodded and said: "Good."

He came no further.

Hun Shizhi felt a coldness at the left side of his waist.

---

The blade went in through a gap in the armour.

Between two plates of the armour, a little below the armpit, there was a gap, usually laced with leather thongs. The blade went in slantwise, and deep. It was a short blade, a foot or so long, its hilt wound with blue cloth.

Hun Shizhi turned his head.

He saw Zhang Shao's face. Zhang Shao's face was very white, his lips pressed together, his eyes not on him but on something behind him. Zhang Shao's hand still gripped the hilt.

Hun Shizhi reached out and seized Zhang Shao by the wrist.

His hand was very big, his forearm very thick, like a length of tree trunk. Once he had seized it, he did not let go. Zhang Shao tried to pull his hand back, pulled once and could not, pulled again and still could not. Zhang Shao's face went whiter still.

The guards stood stunned. One shouted "General!" and drew his sword. The dozen-odd horse behind Huai'en drew their swords too. The gate passage rang with iron leaving scabbards, the sound knocking back and forth between the walls.

Huai'en said: "Nobody move."

No one moved.

Hun Shizhi still held Zhang Shao's wrist. He opened his mouth as if to say something, and nothing came. His legs gave way, his body sagged back against the wall of the gate passage. The wall was brick, and very cold. He slid down it, and as he slid his hand still held on, dragging Zhang Shao down with him until he was bent double.

He sat on the ground.

Zhang Shao stood bent over before him, his wrist still clenched in his grip.

Hun Shizhi raised his head and looked at him.

"Shao," he said.

Zhang Shao did not answer.

Hun Shizhi's hand let go.

He sat against the wall, his head turned towards the end of the passage that opened outside the city. That end was white, very bright, and the wind poured in from it, stirring his beard. His eyes were open, looking at that white.

After a while, his beard stopped stirring.

---

Huai'en walked past him, out of the gate passage, into the city.

When the soldiers in the city heard, some wept, some cursed, some took up their swords and ran for the north gate. Halfway there, a few officers stopped them. The officers said: the Military Governor is in the city, and the Military Governor's troops are outside; what can you do by going?

That afternoon Huai'en sat in the hall of the governor's residence and called in Hun Shizhi's officers one by one, and spoke with them one by one. When he had spoken with them, he sent them out. Some went out and back to their camps. Some went out and never came back.

By evening, the soldiers of Lingzhou were all Huai'en's soldiers.

A few old soldiers secretly took up Hun Shizhi's body, rolled it in a straw mat, carried it outside the city, and buried it by the river.

That night, the river sounded once again.

*Dong.*

---

That day, Yisi was in Hezhong.

Guo Ziyi's main camp was pitched outside the city of Hezhong, and men came to it every day. Some had run from Fenzhou, some from Taiyuan, some from further away. Those who came gave their names, were put into companies, drew their rations, and settled in camp. The camp grew more crowded every day, and Guo Ziyi busier every day.

The news came on the ninth day.

The one who brought it was an old soldier of Lingzhou. He had escaped from Lingzhou by the byways, crossed the Yellow River, and walked eight days, and when he reached Hezhong both his shoes were worn through.

Yisi was in the command tent at the time, reading a grain register aloud to Guo Ziyi.

The old soldier came in, knelt, and told it. He told it in a jumble, a sentence from the front, a sentence from the back, some things twice. Guo Ziyi listened all the way through and did not interrupt.

When the old soldier had finished, he lay on the ground and wept.

Guo Ziyi had men help him out, give him food, give him new shoes.

Only Guo Ziyi and Yisi were left in the tent.

Guo Ziyi said nothing. Yisi still held the grain register, read halfway, stopped at a number. He put the register down.

---

Yisi thought of that campfire at Fengxiang.

The fire was nearly out, nothing left but a heap of red embers. Hun Shizhi came out of the shadow, squatted by the fire warming his hands, that half-missing ear twitching in the firelight. He said: if anyone wags his tongue again about him preaching in camp, send the fellow to me. And when he had said it, clapped Yisi on the shoulder, and went.

That clap had been very hard; Yisi's shoulder had hurt half the day.

He thought too of the drill ground outside Lingzhou. When the three bouts were over, Hun Shizhi had asked him: what do you do all day following Lord Guo about? He had said: watch the roads. Hun Shizhi had looked at him a long time and said: then watch them true. If you get one step wrong out there, all of us following behind will have to jump into the pit one after another.

When he had said it, Hun Shizhi turned and went, and Hun Jian was waiting for him at the edge of the ground, and father and son went off to the camp one behind the other.

---

Guo Ziyi said: "Hun Jian is in camp."

Yisi said: "I'll go."

---

Hun Jian was at the archery ground behind the camp.

He already knew. When that old soldier from Lingzhou came into camp, it was Hun Jian he had looked for first.

The archery ground was very empty; there was only him. He stood behind the line, a hundred paces from the target, shooting one arrow after another. There were thirty arrows in his quiver; he shot one, drew one, nocked it, drew, loosed. The bowstring sounded once, the target sounded once.

Yisi went over and stood beside him, and said nothing.

Hun Jian said nothing either, and did not turn his head.

When he had shot all thirty, the quiver was empty. He put down his bow, went to the target, pulled the arrows out one by one, gathered them in his fist, came back, put them in the quiver, and took up the bow again.

He shot another thirty.

The arrows in the target were packed together in a knot; several had split the shafts of the ones before them.

He put down the bow again and started towards the target.

Yisi went over first.

He went to the target and pulled the arrows out one by one. They were in very deep, and some would not come; he held the target with one hand and gripped the shaft with the other and slowly twisted it out. When he had pulled them all, he gathered them into a bundle, came back, and held them out to Hun Jian.

Hun Jian looked at the bundle of arrows a while, and took it.

He drew one out, and nocked it on the string.

---

Note: The Tibetan entry into Chang'an and the setting up of Chenghong, Prince of Guangwu, as emperor; Guo Ziyi's gathering of scattered troops and use of decoys to recover the capital; the death of Pugu Yang at Yuci; Pugu Huai'en's flight north across the river to Lingwu with three hundred of his own men; Hun Shizhi's wish to refuse him, his nephew Zhang Shao's urging that he be admitted, and Hun Shizhi's murder after Huai'en entered the city—all follow the two *Tang Histories* and the *Zizhi tongjian*, chapter 223 (first and second years of Guangde). The histories record the manner of the killing very briefly; the stabbing in the gate passage, the letter and the opening of the river are the novel's own working.
