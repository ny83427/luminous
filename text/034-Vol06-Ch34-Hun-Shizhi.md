# Volume Six　The Shuofang Army

## Chapter 34　Hun Shizhi

> That wind and rain should come in their season, the world be at peace, men be orderly and things be pure, the living prosper and the dead be at rest.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

In the tenth month of the first year of Guangde, the Tibetans entered Chang'an.

All that autumn the frontier reports from Longyou went up to the capital one after another, reached the palace, and were heard of no more; only when the Tibetans' two hundred thousand had crossed the Wei did the palace panic. The Sage went out by the park gate and east to Shanzhou; few of the officials went with him, and everyone in the city who could run ran into the Southern Mountains. The Tibetans came from the west, entering by the Kaiyuan Gate and the Jinguang Gate; the Yining Ward lay just inside the Kaiyuan Gate, and on the very first night the streets outside the ward wall were full of hoofbeats.

Yisi did not leave, and neither did the monastery's people. From dusk on the first day, the households of the ward who could not run—the old, the sick, those with children in their arms—came one after another to pound on the monastery gate. Elder Samaan, hugging his account book, reckoned the monastery's rice and said it could take thirty people; any more, and the rice would not last ten days. Jingjing was Deacon of Charity then; he stood inside the gate listening, listened a while, and looked once at Yisi. Yisi said only: "Charity is yours. Whether this gate opens is yours to decide." Jingjing thought of that second night in the Xiushan Ward at Luoyang, when the gates of other houses were pounded on and not opened, turned round, and told the guard brothers to open the gate. In those ten-odd days the monastery took in one hundred and forty-odd people all told; the felt tents in the back courtyard were full, and the hall was full of sleepers too; Elder Samaan cut the gruel from two meals a day to one, and from thick to thin, and drank the thin himself along with the rest.

Tibetan soldiers came to the monastery gate twice. The first time it was seven or eight riders; they went round once outside the gate, saw the cross-shaped hollow chiselled in the lintel, pointed at it, said a few words, and left. The second time twenty or thirty came and beat on the gate with the backs of their swords. Sargis drew the guard brothers up inside the gate into a wall of shields; Yisi opened the wicket and went out; an old Tokharian believer of the monastery, who had traded horses in Tibetan lands in his youth, followed behind him to interpret, and talked a long while; then Yisi had two sacks of rice carried out of the storeroom and set outside the gate. The Tibetan in the lead looked at the rice, looked at the wall of shields inside the gate, had the rice loaded onto a horse, and left. Elder Samaan entered those two sacks of rice in his account book, with a line of Syriac beside it, which Jingjing later translated for Yisi: "rice to ransom the gate."

The Tibetans stayed fifteen days in Chang'an, looted the treasuries, burned many houses in the wards, set up a man of the imperial clan as emperor, and then left. It was Guo Ziyi who drove them out. Guo Ziyi had no troops in hand at the time; he gathered a few thousand stragglers about Shangzhou and had banners flown and drums beaten on the hills south of Chang'an to make a show; at night fires were lit all over the hills and drums beaten, and people in the city took up the shouting; the Tibetans thought a great Tang army had come, struck camp, and went west. In the twelfth month the Sage came back to Chang'an.

---

In the first month of the next year an edict came down sending Guo Ziyi to Hezhong to command the old Shuofang troops. A few days later an administrative officer of the Guo household came to the monastery with a dispatch: Lord Guo had memorialized asking that Yisi be attached to the vice-marshal's headquarters, charged with receiving and settling the foreign troops coming over from Pugu Huai'en's side, and he was to pack up and go with them to Hezhong. The officer handed him the dispatch, drank a bowl of water, and left. Guo Ziyi himself Yisi did not see. Jingjing stayed in Chang'an to keep his gruel shed; Yisi took his mace down from the wall, shouldered his shield, and took the road again.

When the Shuofang troops at Hezhong heard Lord Guo had come, they ran to tell one another. Over at Fenzhou the soldiers in Huai'en's camp grew fewer night by night—ten gone today, a hundred tomorrow—and all who left went south, to Hezhong, to Guo Ziyi. Among those who came over were many foreign soldiers of the Tiele, the Tongluo, the Pugu tribes; every day Yisi stood at the camp gate questioning them batch by batch: which tribe they came from, which general they had followed, whether they knew anyone in the camp; and when he had asked, he reported to the officer to be entered on the rolls. Guo Ziyi drank his millet gruel in the mornings as ever; in all those days at Hezhong, Yisi was not once called into the rear tent. Huai'en's son Pugu Yang, holding Yuci with his troops, was killed by his own men. Huai'en, with the three hundred-odd riders left about him, crossed the Yellow River and went north. North was Lingzhou.

---

In the second month at Lingzhou, the ice on the river began to break. Ice breaking makes a sound; lying at night you could hear a thud from across the river, and after a while another thud, dull, like someone far away beating a cracked drum. The people of Lingzhou called it the opening of the river; when the river opened, it was spring.

Hun Shizhi was forty-nine that year, his beard half white, his left ear still missing its half. He was Acting Governor of Shuofang; with Guo Ziyi gone and Huai'en gone, the city of Lingzhou was in his hands. Under him were a few thousand men, mostly old soldiers who, like him, had lived half their lives in this city. Every morning he rose and rode once round the foot of the walls.

That morning, at the east of the city, on a patch of open ground under the wall, a few recruits were wrestling in the snow, muddy all over, with more cheering than wrestling. Hun Shizhi watched a while from the saddle, got down, threw his outer robe to a guard, and walked into the ring.

"All of you. One at a time."

The first recruit was a big fellow; he stood stunned a moment, then rushed in to grab him round the waist. Hun Shizhi drove both hands under his armpits, twisted his waist, and the man was lying on his back in the snow. The second, the third—the same. The fourth was a slight young man of seventeen or eighteen who did not go for the waist but the legs, and held on for dear life; Hun Shizhi swayed twice before he could lift him and throw him.

"What's your name?"

"Chai San." The young man scrambled up out of the snow, his face red with cold.

"Chai San, you're better than the three of them," said Hun Shizhi. "Wrestling's like war: with a big one, go for his legs first. You remember that. In battle, you grab like that, and once you've got hold, don't let go."

He patted Chai San on the head, a pat that made Chai San duck his neck. Hun Shizhi took his robe and threw it on, and rode off toward the west of the city. The recruits in the ring looked at one another, and someone said under his breath: the general's forty-nine.

At the west of the city his way passed a side courtyard; it was Yisi's old home. The gate was shut, its lock rusted solid; over the wall the branches of the apricot tree in the back courtyard of the monastery reached across into the side courtyard, not yet in bud. West of the side courtyard was the little monastery, kept by Master Jiao alone; its roof had been mended a few years back and no longer leaked, and every evening Master Jiao still lit a lamp at its gate.

Last winter, short of firewood, a few soldiers had meant to pull down the empty courtyard and burn its beams. They had brought a ladder and just climbed onto the wall when Hun Shizhi rode up and told them to come down. They came down; one bold one grumbled that the foreign monk had been gone years, was an official in Chang'an now, might never come back; and leaving these few empty rooms standing—you couldn't burn them, couldn't live in them. Hun Shizhi looked at him from the saddle and said: "Your pa goes off to war and you pull down your own house to burn? There are two rotten stables north of the city; go and pull those down. This courtyard's not to be touched. Whoever touches one rafter of it, I'll pull out one of his bones." The soldiers went off with their ladder. Hun Shizhi did not go at once; he got down and sat a while on the step at the courtyard gate; dry apricot leaves from last year lay on the step, and he pushed them aside with the toe of his boot before he mounted and went back.

---

One day in mid-second month, around the hour of *si*, a horseman came to the north gate, his horse lathered with sweat and he himself covered in dust; he reined in under the gate and called up, craning his neck: "Governor Pugu returns to his command! Let Acting Governor Hun open the gate and receive him!" The soldiers on the gate tower ran down to report. Hun Shizhi was eating in the governor's office; when he had heard, he put down his bowl, wiped his hands, had a few officers sent for, and said to send for Zhang Shao too.

Zhang Shao was Hun Shizhi's nephew. Hun Shizhi had an elder sister who long ago married a Han man of Lingzhou surnamed Zhang; the Zhang husband died early and the sister died early too, leaving a child, who at eight came into Hun Shizhi's tent and grew up with Hun Jian. When Hun Jian shot, he shot too, not so straight as Hun Jian; afterwards Hun Jian went off to war with Guo Ziyi and he stayed in Lingzhou with his uncle. He was about thirty that year, quick of speech and quick of mind; the city's grain and fodder, its clothing allowances, were all in his charge.

The few of them sat down in the hall. Hun Shizhi spoke first.

"Pugu Huai'en wants into the city. At Fenzhou he had tens of thousands under him; now he's come back with a few hundred. A few hundred can't take Lingzhou, and he knows it better than anyone," he said. "What he wants isn't this city; it's the few thousand soldiers in it. I mean to shut the gates and leave him outside drinking the wind."

The officers in the hall looked at one another, each waiting for another to speak first.

"Uncle, shutting the gates won't do." Zhang Shao spoke up. "He's the governor; you're the acting governor. Has the court sent down an edict stripping him of office? No. His seal's still in his hand. A governor comes back to his own command and the acting governor shuts the gates and won't let him in—when that's heard in Chang'an, Chang'an will say only one thing: Lingzhou has rebelled."

He paused, and said: "Besides, the grain in the city will only last till the fourth month. The clothing allowances Chang'an owes haven't come in two years. If you shut the gates, Uncle, he drinks the wind outside, and we drink the wind inside; drink it till the fourth month, and who breaks first isn't certain. He has only a few hundred; the city has a few thousand. Settle his men and horses outside the city, Uncle, let him bring only a few in; with no troops in his hand he's a governor with a seal and nothing else. In the city, what can he do?"

Hun Shizhi sat with his two hands pressed on his knees, looking at a wisp of dry grass in a crack between the grey bricks before the hall, and said nothing for a good while.

"Shao'r's said one layer of it," he said. "There's another layer in my heart."

The officers all raised their heads.

"Pugu Huai'en and I are both Tiele—he's of the Pugu tribe, I'm of the Hun tribe. Since An Lushan rose—fighting out of Shuofang, fighting the Tongluo, fighting in Hebei, taking back the two capitals—twenty years we've eaten from one pot. He's a man who won't spare you with his tongue, and his eyes have a layer over them; but in battle he's never left a single brother behind," said Hun Shizhi. "If he's really come to seize the city, I'll shut the gates and fight him; I'm not afraid. But what if he's really turned back? A brother of twenty years, at the end of his road, coming back to his own command, and I shut the gate and leave him outside drinking the wind—then he truly has no road left."

No one in the hall answered.

"Send two men out," said Hun Shizhi, "to see how many he really has behind him."

The men sent out came back only after noon: "Governor Pugu has pitched camp thirty li to the north; not many men, three hundred-odd riders, the horses all thin, a good many of them wounded. No baggage carts, no siege gear. The governor himself wore no armour; he was sitting on a stone at the camp gate eating dry flatbread, and when he saw us he even had a skin of water sent over to us."

Hun Shizhi sat a while longer, and stood up.

"Open the gate. His men stay outside; he brings only twenty riders in. Tomorrow at the hour of *si* I'll receive him at the gate myself."

---

That night Hun Shizhi called Zhang Shao to his room. A brazier burned in the room; Hun Shizhi sat by it with a jug of wine and two bowls before him, poured two bowls, and pushed one to Zhang Shao. Zhang Shao took it up and drank a mouthful; Hun Shizhi did not drink his, but looked at the brazier, and after a while said: "You can write. Write a letter to Jian'r for me."

Zhang Shao took paper and brush, spread them on the low table, ground the ink, and waited with brush raised. Hun Shizhi knitted his brows and for a long time could not squeeze out a sentence; he had fought all his life and written few letters.

"All's well in the city."

Zhang Shao wrote it.

"The river's opened."

Zhang Shao wrote that too. Hun Shizhi thought a long while again, thought till a piece of charcoal in the brazier fell in, and only then said: "That bow of yours from when you were small—I've still got it, hanging on the wall of the east wing. When the war's done, come back for it."

When Zhang Shao came to the word *bow*, the tip of his brush stopped a moment, and the ink spread a little on the paper. He too had come into this tent at eight; that year his uncle whittled a bow for Hun Jian and whittled one for him too, a size smaller than Hun Jian's. His own had long since been lost who knew where. He finished the sentence and looked up for the next. Hun Shizhi waved his hand and said that was all. Zhang Shao picked up the letter and was about to fold it when Hun Shizhi stopped him.

"One more line," he said. "The backward thrust—till you're at that point, don't use it."

Zhang Shao bowed his head and wrote it, folded the letter, and said that first thing tomorrow he'd have the post send it to Hezhong.

Hun Shizhi took up his own bowl of wine, drained it at one gulp, and poured another.

"When your ma died you were eight. She took my hand and told me to look after you. I've looked after you twenty-odd years," he said. "You're better at accounts than Jian'r."

He smiled, drank the second bowl too, stood, and clapped Zhang Shao on the shoulder: "Go to bed. Early start tomorrow. Tomorrow in the gateway, you stand at my left hand. You've sharp eyes—watch his men's hands for me."

Zhang Shao answered, rose, and went out.

---

The next day at the hour of *si*, the north gate of Lingzhou opened. The wall at the north gate was very thick, the gateway ten paces or so long, dark inside and bright at both ends; a man standing in the middle saw white ahead and white behind, and the wind poured in from outside, through the gateway, moaning. Hun Shizhi stood at the city end of the gateway in armour, without a helmet, twenty guards about him—Chai San among them—and Zhang Shao at his left hand, half a step behind.

The hoofbeats outside drew near. First two horsemen came in, then Huai'en. Huai'en rode a grey horse, so thin that its ribs showed one by one; he himself had grown thin too, his face longer than before, his cheeks sunken, his cheekbones the higher for it; he wore an old robe, no armour, a sabre at his waist, a dozen-odd guards behind him, every one of them grey with dust. He reined in at the middle of the gateway and got down, and the two men stood facing each other ten-odd paces apart. Huai'en's long narrow eyes were half-closed, still as before, behind a layer of something.

"General Hun, you've grown old."

"So have you."

Huai'en smiled, the smile still only at the corners of his mouth. "My son is dead. At Yuci—killed by his own men, men who'd followed him for years."

"I heard."

Huai'en walked forward a few steps, up to Hun Shizhi, looked at him, and glanced once behind him.

"Your men camp outside the city; I'll have grain and fodder sent out to them," said Hun Shizhi. "You'll lodge in the east wing of the governor's office; I've had it made ready."

"Good," said Huai'en, and came no further.

Hun Shizhi felt a chill under his left ribs; the blade had gone in through a gap in his armour. Between two plates, a little below the armpit, was a seam, usually laced with leather thongs; the blade went in slanting, and went in deep. It was a short blade, a foot or so long, its hilt wound with blue cloth. Hun Shizhi turned his head and saw Zhang Shao's face. Zhang Shao's face was very white, his lips pressed together, his eyes not looking at him but at something somewhere behind him, his hand still on the hilt. Hun Shizhi put out his hand and seized Zhang Shao's wrist. His hand was very big, his forearm thick as a length of tree trunk, and once it gripped it did not let go; Zhang Shao pulled back, pulled once and could not move it, pulled again and still could not, and his face grew whiter.

The guards stood stunned. One shouted "General!" and drew his sword; the dozen-odd riders behind Huai'en drew theirs too, and the gateway was full of the sound of steel leaving scabbards, ringing back and forth off the two walls. Huai'en said: "Nobody move," and not one moved. Hun Shizhi still held Zhang Shao's wrist; he opened his mouth and could not get a word out; his legs gave, his body fell back against the brick wall of the gateway, and the wall was cold. He slid down the wall, his hand still gripping, dragging Zhang Shao down bent over with him, slid till he was sitting on the ground. He raised his head, looked at Zhang Shao, and said: "Shao'r." Zhang Shao did not answer. Hun Shizhi's hand let go. He sat against the wall with his head turned toward the outer end, where it was white, very bright, and the wind poured in from there, stirring his beard. He looked at that whiteness with open eyes, and after a while his beard did not stir.

---

Huai'en walked past him, out of the gateway, into the city. When the soldiers in the city heard, some wept, some cursed, some ran for the north gate with swords in their hands and were stopped halfway by a few officers: "The governor's in the city; the governor's troops are outside it. What good will it do if you go?" That afternoon Huai'en sat in the hall of the governor's office and had Hun Shizhi's officers called in one by one to talk; when he had talked to each he sent him out; some went out and back to their camps, and some went out and did not come back again.

The last called in was Zhang Shao. He had changed into a clean robe and washed the blood off his hands; under his nails it was still black.

"Your uncle treated you well," said Huai'en.

"Well." Zhang Shao stood. "His bow he kept for Hun Jian; his city, from now on, he'd have kept for Hun Jian too. Ten years I managed the grain of this city, and every grain of rice in the accounts I counted."

Huai'en looked at him, looked a good while.

"That backward thrust," he said. "Did he teach it to his son?"

Zhang Shao was taken aback. "He did. That winter Hun Jian worked the stirrups in the stables for a month."

Huai'en said nothing.

"What I promised you—Lingzhou, you have charge of it for now," he said. "Go."

Zhang Shao clasped his hands and withdrew. In the hall only Huai'en and Baye the Third were left. Baye the Third had followed him thirty years; his brother's body was one that Huai'en had carried back off the field on his horse. He came up, unbuckled the sabre from Huai'en's waist, hung it on the rack behind the folding chair, and squatted to pull off his boots. The boots were all mud, frozen hard; with one hand he held the heel and with the other gripped the leg of the boot, and worked it off bit by bit.

"That man—use him for now," said Huai'en. "Use him till autumn."

"Yes."

"A man who can stab his own uncle—when I sleep, I won't have him standing at the tent door."

Baye the Third stood the boot he had taken off on the floor and went to the other. "Understood. From now on, at the tent door, I'll have our own men stand."

Huai'en leaned back in the folding chair, his two feet stretched out, the felt socks on them soaked through. He looked at the roof beams and said: "That lance-stroke of Hun Shizhi's—did you ever see it? That year on the parade ground at Lingzhou, Guo Ziyi's foreign monk took one from him and got a white patch on his chest. I called out 'Good!' from the side of the ground—called it grudgingly, but good is good."

"I saw it." Baye the Third fetched a felt from the corner, shook it out, and spread it over the other end of the folding chair.

"In the Tianbao years I ate from one pot with him. When we fought the Tongluo, he took a blade for me," said Huai'en. "If the court had trusted me, why would I have needed to come back to Lingzhou? If I hadn't come back to Lingzhou, how would he have come to die in a gateway? A fine man like Hun Shizhi..."

He did not go on, but looked out of the hall. Outside the light was fading; someone in the courtyard was lighting torches.

"Then there's still one man who knows it," he said.

Baye the Third did not answer; he moved the brazier closer to the folding chair, withdrew to the door, and drew it shut for him.

By night every soldier in the city of Lingzhou was Huai'en's soldier. Hun Shizhi's body a few old soldiers secretly took up—Chai San among them—rolled in a reed mat, carried out of the city, and buried by the river. That night there was another sound from the river.

---

That day Yisi was at Hezhong. Guo Ziyi's great camp was pitched outside the city of Hezhong; men came to the camp every day—some running from Fenzhou, some from Taiyuan, some from further still; those who came gave their names, were put into companies, drew rations, and settled into the camp, and the camp grew more crowded day by day.

The news came on the ninth day. The bearer was an old soldier of Lingzhou who had escaped from Lingzhou by the back roads, crossed the Yellow River, changed horses twice on the way, and by the time he reached Hezhong was worn so thin he was hardly recognizable. Yisi was in the command tent just then with two newly arrived Tongluo, waiting for an answer, and the officer was reading a grain roll to Guo Ziyi at the table. The old soldier came in, knelt, and began at once; he told it confusedly, a sentence here and a sentence there, some things twice. Guo Ziyi listened throughout and did not interrupt him. When the old soldier had finished, he lay on the ground weeping; Guo Ziyi had him helped out, given food, and told to rest. The grain roll in the officer's hand had been read halfway; it stopped at a figure and was read no further.

Yisi stood in the corner of the tent and thought of the campfire at Fengxiang. That night the fire was nearly out, only a heap of red embers left, and Hun Shizhi came out of the dark, squatted by the fire warming his hands, the ear that was missing its half twitching in the firelight, and said that if anyone chewed his tongue again about him preaching in the camp, let the fellow come and find him; and having said it clapped Yisi on the shoulder and left. That clap was very heavy; Yisi's shoulder hurt for half the day. He thought too of the parade ground outside Lingzhou: when the three bouts were done, Hun Shizhi asked what he did all day following Lord Guo, and he said he watched the road, and Hun Shizhi said then watch it true; if you see one step wrong there, all of us behind you will jump into the pit one after another. And having said it Hun Shizhi turned and left; Hun Jian was waiting for him at the edge of the ground, and father and son went back into the camp one behind the other.

That evening the post brought another letter, from Lingzhou, with Hun Jian's name on the cover; the courier said it had been sent from Lingzhou ten-odd days ago. The officer presented it; Guo Ziyi looked at the cover, did not open it, handed it to Yisi, and said: "Hun Jian is at the archery ground behind the camp. Take it to him."

---

Hun Jian was at the archery ground behind the camp. He knew already; when the old soldier from Lingzhou came into the camp, it was Hun Jian he looked for first. The archery ground was empty but for him, standing behind the line a hundred paces from the target, shooting arrow after arrow. He had thirty arrows in his quiver: shoot one, draw one, nock, draw, loose; the bowstring sounded once and the target sounded once.

Yisi went over and stood beside him, waited till he had shot all thirty, and only then held out the letter. Hun Jian put down his bow, took the letter, opened it, and read. The letter was very short, and he read it very slowly; when he had read it he folded it and tucked it into his robe, then took up his bow again, drew an arrow, and nocked it. Yisi did not know what the letter said; he saw only that as Hun Jian tucked it away, his hand pressed once on his breast.

He walked to the target, pulled the arrows out one by one, gripped them in his hand, walked back, put them in the quiver, and shot thirty more. The arrows in the target were crowded in a cluster; several grazed the feathers of the one before. He put down the bow again and started toward the target. Yisi went over first, reached the target, and pulled the arrows out one by one; they had gone in very deep, and some would not come; then he pressed the target with one hand and with the other gripped the shaft and slowly worked it out, turning. When he had pulled them all, he gripped them in a bundle, walked back, and held them out to Hun Jian. Hun Jian looked at the bundle of arrows, took it, drew one, and set it to the string.

---

Note: The Tibetans' entry into Chang'an, Daizong's flight to Shanzhou, the setting up of the Prince of Guangwu, Chenghong, and Guo Ziyi's recovery of the capital with stragglers and a feint; in the first month of the second year of Guangde, Guo Ziyi's appointment as vice-marshal of Guannei and Hedong and governor of Hezhong; Pugu Yang's death at Yuci; Pugu Huai'en's crossing the river north to Lingwu with three hundred of his followers; Hun Shizhi's wish to refuse him, his nephew Zhang Shao's urging him to admit him, and Hun Shizhi's killing after Huai'en entered the city—all follow the *Comprehensive Mirror*, chapter 223 (first to second years of Guangde); the old biography differs (the *Old Book of Tang*, biography of Hun Jian, has Hun Shizhi die fighting the Tibetans). The gates by which the Tibetans entered, the people taken in at the monastery in the Yining Ward and the rice to ransom the gate, the post to which Yisi was attached, the stabbing in the gateway, the letter, the opening of the river, the words of Huai'en and Baye the Third in the hall, and Chai San are the novel's arrangement; that Huai'en afterwards had Zhang Shao govern Lingzhou and then killed him for betraying his uncle follows the *Comprehensive Mirror*.
