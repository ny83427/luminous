# Volume Ten　Huichang

## Chapter 59　Packing Up

> His work being done, at high noon He ascended to the Truth.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

On the fifth day after the stele was buried, the county scribe came again.

This time the sheriff did not come. The scribe came alone, with two constables, the stack of registers still under his arm. He said the sheriff was counting a great temple south of the city these days, with hundreds of bronze images and a dozen-odd bronze bells, more than he could count, and had sent him to take this place in first.

He began counting in the front courtyard.

The seals on the hall doors were still there; he looked at them and nodded. The seal on the scripture cupboard was still there too, and he nodded at that too. He opened the register and checked item by item, and for each item checked, drew a tick. So many bays of hall, tick. So many of chapel, tick. So many monks' cells, tick. Wooden cross, one, tick. Wooden board, one.

He looked up. "The wooden board?"

An Yan took the board down from the gallery and held it out in both hands.

The scribe took it and looked at it. An old wooden board, two feet or so long, a shallow hollow worn into the middle by the mallet, its edges rubbed to a shine. He turned it over and looked at the back; there was nothing on the back.

"This," he asked, "what's it for?"

"For striking," said An Yan. "To get people up."

The scribe weighed it in his hand, and looked at An Yan once more. Beside that line in the register he wrote a few small characters, and handed the board back.

"Wooden," he said. "To be disposed of as appropriate."

An Yan stood holding the board, taken aback a moment, and stepped back into the gallery.

The scribe went on checking down the list. At the last line, he stopped.

He raised his head and looked toward the middle of the front courtyard. Where the stele had stood there was now level ground, its earth darker than that around it, a few locust-tree leaves fallen on it. An edge of the base showed, blue-black. At the edge of the new earth there was a corner of white hemp, wetted by these days' dew and dried, dried and wetted, and already gone yellow.

The scribe looked a long time.

"The stele?" he asked.

"Buried," said Jingfu. "Under that new earth."

No one in the courtyard spoke. The two constables looked at each other, and one laid his hand on his sword hilt.

The scribe did not turn to look at them. He went up to the stretch of new earth, stopped, and looked down at the corner of hemp. He stood a long time, so long that the two constables grew a little impatient, and one coughed.

Then he went back to the small table, sat down, and beside the last line of the register wrote a few characters.

What he wrote, Jingfu did not see. No one in the courtyard saw. When the scribe had written it, he closed the register, put it under his arm, and stood up.

"Three days from now," he said. "Outside the Kaiyuan Gate. The hour of the Dragon. The Zoroastrian shrine's people and the Muhu will be there too, going together. Someone will lead you."

He went to the gate, and turned his head back again.

"That cloth," he said. "Don't let anyone pull it up."

---

For three days, the monastery's people packed their things.

There was not much they could pack. The hall and the scripture cupboard were sealed, the deeds to the fields taken, the money taken too. What was left was what each had in his own room: a few robes, a pair of shoes, a quilt, a bowl.

The two old Persian monks packed fastest. They had been in Chang'an over thirty years, and what they packed came to one small bundle each. In the elder's bundle there was a Syriac scripture he had brought from the Patriarchate, which he had always kept under his pillow, and which had never gone into the scripture cupboard. He wrapped the scripture in three layers of cloth, tucked it into his coat, and said to Jingfu: this one, the county doesn't know about.

Jingfu said: the county didn't ask.

The old monk thought, and nodded.

The monastery's young monks whose registered home was in Tang lands had already gone. The one from Lantian, on the morning he left, came and knocked his head once to the ground before Jingfu, and said he was going back to farm. The one from Liangzhou said Liangzhou was far, and he would have to wait for the county to give him a travel pass; meanwhile he would find work in the city. And the two Sogdians born in Chang'an returned to lay life and went back to their families; one sold spices in the West Market, one said he would go and stay with kin in the Eastern Capital.

None of them said much when they left. What was there to say.

---

Salang came on the second day.

When he came in at the door he had a big bundle on his shoulder and the iron-shod staff in his hand.

Jingfu looked at him. "What's this for?"

"The county's hiring escorts," said Salang. "To take people to Fengxiang, thirty cash a day and meals. I gave my name."

"And after Fengxiang?"

"After Fengxiang," said Salang, "Fengxiang will hire again."

Jingfu looked at him a long time.

"You're on the Chang'an register," said Jingfu. "Your mother was Tang. No one is telling you to go."

Salang thought and said: "My grandfather came from the west guarding people. I'm guarding people going back."

When he had said it, he put the bundle down in the gallery, leaned the staff against a pillar, and went to the well to carry water. As though the matter were already settled, and needed no more said.

Jingfu stood in the gallery and watched him carry water. He wanted to say something, and did not say it.

---

On the last day, Jingfu packed his own room.

Half of the things in his room his master had left. He took them out one by one and laid them on the bed.

First, the three bowls.

One was the one he used every day, his grand-teacher Yisi's bowl, used for more than forty years, its bottom worn white. One was his master's, with a small chip knocked out of its rim; his master had always drunk his soup from the other side. And the last had a notch in its lip, and in it was a pinch of earth, long since dried right through and caked into a lump, grey in colour.

He wrapped his grand-teacher's bowl and put it in his bundle. Then he took up the bowl with the earth, wrapped it in a cloth, wrapped it very tight for fear the earth would spill, and put that in his bundle too.

His master's was left.

He held it in his hand and looked at it a while.

Xiaoman was poking his head round the door. Jingfu beckoned him, and Xiaoman came in.

"This one's for you," said Jingfu.

Xiaoman took it and looked at the little chip in the rim.

"It's broken," said Xiaoman.

"Drink your soup from the other side," said Jingfu.

Then the wooden board. The one that leaned at the head of the bed, written all over, written and wiped, wiped and written, layer pressed on layer, all of it the same character. Jingfu had never wiped it. He picked the board up, looked at it a long time, wrapped it in cloth, and gave it to Lü Shao.

"Keep it at your house," he said.

Lü Shao nodded and did not ask why.

And there was the paper An Yan had given him, folded into a small square, tucked in his coat, not once taken out these three days.

Last, the lamp.

---

The lamp still hung on the iron hook outside the gate.

The monastery gate was sealed; Jingfu went round from the back door to the front. The seal was still on the gate, but after these days of wind and sun its edges had curled up, and one corner had been lifted by the wind and was flapping against the door-leaf.

He took the lamp down.

Xiaoman had followed at his heels and come out too. He looked at the lamp in Jingfu's hand, looked a while, and asked: "Are you taking it?"

Jingfu did not answer.

He poured the dregs of oil out of the saucer, wiped it clean with a cloth, ladled in a spoonful of new oil, drew out a little length of wick and trimmed it even. One thing after another, done very slowly.

When he had finished, he held the lamp out to Xiaoman.

Xiaoman was taken aback, and did not take it.

"You said," said Jingfu, "that you'd light it too, someday."

Xiaoman looked at the lamp. A bronze saucer, half a palm across, rubbed bright by hands for decades, with a small dent at the edge.

"Light it for who to see?" Xiaoman asked.

"For whoever's walking at night," said Jingfu.

Xiaoman reached out both hands and took the lamp. He held it low, as if afraid of spilling it.

"Uncle Lü's door," he said, "hasn't got an iron hook."

Lü Shao said behind him: "I'll put one up."

---

That evening, twenty or thirty people came.

They came from every ward—Sogdian merchants, Tang people, a few of mixed Tang and *hu* birth. They did not dare come by the front gate, the front gate was sealed; they all came in by the back door, one at a time, one coming in every little while, and once in they did not speak loudly. The hall could not be entered, so they stood in the back courtyard. The back courtyard was not large; they filled it.

Merchant Kang came too, hurried back from Luoyang, and said Luoyang had been sealed too. That stone pillar, he said, was still standing; no one had touched it. Probably because no one could read what was carved on it.

An Yan stood in the gallery holding the wooden board, and did not strike it.

When it was nearly dark, Jingfu stood on the steps.

He had not meant to say anything. His master had not liked to talk, and his master's Pa had not liked to talk. He stood on the steps looking at these people below, looked a while, and felt he ought to say one thing.

"Chang'an is a place where we lived," he said. "It is not our everlasting place."

When he had said it, he came down the steps.

No one took it up. The back courtyard was very quiet; there was only the rustle of the jujube leaves. A leaf fell and landed on an old woman's shoulder, and she did not brush it off.

After a while, the elder of the old Persian monks spoke. He said one sentence in Syriac, very slowly, word by word. When he had said it, he looked at Jingfu.

Jingfu knew the sentence. His master had translated it. In the scripture room he had seen his master write that sentence on paper, write it several times, strike it out, and write it again.

"My kingdom is not of this world," said Jingfu.

The old monk nodded.

Then they all sang the hymn together, *The highest heavens all deeply revere and praise; the great earth longs again for peace and harmony*, sang it very softly, for fear the ward would hear. The old Persians sang to the Syriac tune, the Tang people sang the Tang words, one fast, one slow, and by the end, they came together.

An Yan did not sing. His voice had gone altogether these last days. He stood in the gallery holding the board, tapping out the beat on it with his finger, tap by tap, without a sound.

---

That night, Jingfu went to the well and drew a dipper of water.

It was the monastery's well, under the locust tree, the windlass creaking. When he was small he had got up in the middle of the night and drunk cold water at this well, and when he had drunk it, turned his head and seen his master standing in the gallery, a coat thrown round him, frowning.

He drank the whole dipper. He drew another, and drank that too.

After this, he would not drink from it.

---

Early the next morning, they went to the Kaiyuan Gate.

The Kaiyuan Gate was the gate by which Chang'an went west. The tunnel of the gate was very deep; walking in it, footsteps hummed. Outside the gate stood a stone, carved to say that from here westward to Anxi it was nine thousand nine hundred *li*. The number was so large it looked like a mistake.

Beside the stone a crowd was already standing. People of the Zoroastrian shrine, people of the Muhu, some Persians, some Sogdians, old and young, each with a bundle on his back. The old priest from the Buzheng Ward was there too, supported by a young man, holding in his arms a small earthenware jar, its mouth tied over with cloth. Jingfu looked at him once, and he looked at Jingfu once, and the two of them nodded.

The man leading them was a minor county clerk of twenty or so, holding a list, calling names one by one. When he came to Daqin, he read four names. When he came to "A-Fu," Jingfu answered. The clerk looked up at him once, lowered his head again, and drew a tick on the list.

Salang stood among the escorts, two bundles on his shoulders, one his own and one the two old Persian monks'. The staff was stuck through the strap of the bundles, iron tip upward, a length higher than his head.

The people seeing them off stood by the gate tunnel. Lü Shao, Xiaoman, An Yan, and a few of those who had come the night before.

Xiaoman held the lamp in his arms. The lamp was not lit; it was already day.

---

When the clerk had finished calling names, he shouted: "Moving off!"

The crowd began to move.

Jingfu turned round and bowed to those seeing them off. First to Lü Shao, and Lü Shao returned the bow. Then to An Yan. An Yan was seventy-four, standing there with his back bent, still holding the wooden board, and Jingfu thought: this bow should go deep.

He bent down. The bundle weighed on his back, very heavy; he bent very deep.

Just then, there was a *pfft*.

The sound was very loud, very long; it turned a corner in the gate tunnel and hummed there a while before it died away.

Everyone stopped.

The young man supporting the old priest stood with his mouth open. Several of the Muhu turned their heads. The clerk calling names, the list still pinched in his hand, turned his face aside, his shoulders twitching. Salang, among the escorts, bowed his head and looked at his feet.

Jingfu stayed bent, and did not straighten. In a moment his face had gone red to the roots of his ears.

All his life his stomach had been good. Cold rice, leftover soup, flatbread a night old, cold water from the well—he could get anything down. In fifty years, there had been only twice. Once at nine, at morning service. Once today.

He thought of those two dippers of well water last night.

The first to laugh was Xiaoman.

Holding the lamp, he burst out laughing, laughed till he doubled over, and laughing, the tears came. He laughed and wept at once, wept till his nose ran, and could not free a hand to wipe it, the lamp still in his arms.

Then Lü Shao. Lü Shao turned away and covered his face with his sleeve, covered it a while, and a patch of the sleeve went wet.

Then An Yan. An Yan, his voice gone, could not laugh aloud; he only stood with his mouth open, panting, his beard quivering, his eyes full of water.

All of those seeing them off laughed. Laughing, weeping, no telling which was which. Those few Muhu laughed too. The old priest did not know what had happened and asked the young man supporting him; the young man leaned to his ear and told him, and the old priest laughed too, laughed till he coughed.

Jingfu was still bent over.

He remembered the year he was nine, when his master had called him into his room, poured two bowls of hot water, and said: I'll tell you something, a thing from when I was small.

Xiaoman, weeping and laughing, said to him: "Master, that's shameful."

Jingfu straightened.

He looked at Xiaoman, looked a while.

"What shames a man is a lie," he said, "not wind."

Xiaoman stopped laughing. He stood there holding the lamp, his mouth open, the tears still on his face.

Jingfu reached out and stroked his head. His hand was very large; it covered the whole back of Xiaoman's head.

Then he turned and fell in with the column.

---

Outside the city gate a wind had risen.

The wind came from the west, blew past the stone carved with nine thousand nine hundred *li*, blew into the gate tunnel, made it hum. Those walking in front hunched their necks and hitched their bundles up their backs. Salang walked on the outside, the iron tip of his staff flashing in the sun.

Jingfu did not look back.

He walked out of the gate tunnel, into the wind, and went west.
