# Volume Nine　The Translation Hall

## Chapter 55　Shiyiniang

> The dark void was changed, and heaven and earth were opened; sun and moon began to move, and day and night were made.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

Early in the spring of the tenth year of Zhenyuan, Granny He's grandson came to take his leave.

Granny He had been dead more than twenty years. In her day she had waited under the scholar tree by her gate for Yisi to change her dressing; in the first year of Qianyuan, when the city fell and the deacon left to keep the monastery was cut down by rioting soldiers, it was she and her old man who buried him. Her grandson was called He Jingzong. As a child he had come to worship with his grandmother, sat dozing in the last row of the hall, and dozed until he toppled into his grandmother's lap. Later he studied for the classics examination, sat it three times, and last year passed; the Ministry of Personnel appointed him Commandant of Lantian County, and in the spring he was to take up his post. He came to the monastery in a new blue robe, bowed before the hall, went into the scripture room to see Jingjing, and spoke very courteously.

"Your Reverence, I'm going to Lantian to take office. There's a great deal of business in a county, and I probably won't be able to come to the offering once in seven any more. My colleagues and superiors in the county are all men of letters; most believe in the Buddha, and there are Daoists too. A county commandant with a cross enshrined in the county office—if people asked about it, I couldn't answer." He kept his head down. "My granny's name—I beg Your Reverence, when you pray hereafter, to say it for me. I myself… won't say it any more."

Jingjing said he would, and asked him: "Do you still remember the word your granny used to say?"

He Jingzong thought and said, "I remember. *Aluohe*. Granny said she'd said it forty years, and what she had in her heart was just old Grandfather Heaven all the same." When he had said it he was the first to laugh, and Jingjing laughed too.

When He Jingzong had gone, Samaan came in, turned the ledger to the page of the register of names, and asked how this one should be entered.

"Enter it as it is."

What Samaan entered was: *He, one, gone to office in Lantian, will not come again; asks prayers for his grandmother, Mistress He.* When he had written it he turned back a few pages and said, "Master, since the start of spring this year, this is the fifth entered like this. The one from the south of the city who wanted a son: his wife's belly didn't stir, so his mother took the wife to burn three sticks of incense before the Guanyin image at the Daxingshan Monastery, and when they came back they stopped coming. The old medicine merchant of the West Market—his grandson fell in love with alchemy and followed a Daoist into the Zhongnan Mountains. When he left he said, your Lord says when people die they'll rise again; what he wants is not to die, and not dying is quicker than rising. That pair of brothers in the Shence Army: the officer in their camp believes in the Buddha, and at New Year they have to go with the officer to a monastery to give alms, and having given alms, it doesn't look well to keep running off to a *hu* temple. And then this one of the He family." He closed the ledger and tucked his hands into his sleeves. "Not one of these has ever cursed the monastery, or said the Lord is no good. They just don't come."

"I know," said Jingjing.

That spring the character on his board was the same many times over. What he was reading was the Lord saying, *In my Father's house are many mansions.* He wrote the character *jia*, house, home. These people had gone out of this house, and having gone out, gone into other people's houses, and in other people's houses there was incense, there were images, there were officials, there was the elixir, there were many things this house did not have. He faced that character and thought until dawn, and could not think of words to call them back.

---

In the fourth month He Jingzong came once more. This time with him was his father, Old He, Granny He's son, past sixty, in a new silk robe, with two servants behind him carrying a small wooden chest. Old He came in and first bowed to Jingjing, and spoke even more courteously than his son.

"Master Jing, my mother has lain more than twenty years in the monastery's burial ground outside the Kaiyuan Gate, and we're obliged to the monastery for looking after her. Now that my son is an official, the family has acquired a plot for an ancestral cemetery in the south of the city, and has asked for a place in the pagoda courtyard of the Jianfu Monastery too, and means to move my mother's bones there to be buried with my father. The monks of the Jianfu Monastery have already chosen the day: the third of next month. My mother believed in yours while she lived; that I know. But my mother is one of the He family, and people of the He family must be buried in the He family's ground. As her son, I can't let my mother lie alone in a *hu* burial ground and have people say the He family's county commandant has his granny buried beside a *hu* temple."

Jingjing said, "Granny He was baptized. Before she died she told Priest Yisi that when she died she was to be buried at the monastery, together with the monastery's people. Shiyiniang was there that day; Shiyiniang can bear witness. Granny He's name is carved, too, on the side of the stele in the front courtyard, in Syriac letters, written by Priest Yisi's own hand. If her body is moved, her name is still on the stele."

"If her name's on the stele, let it stay on the stele," said Old He. "I'm not concerned with the stele. I'm concerned with my mother's bones."

He Jingzong stood behind his father, red in the face, unable to get a word in. Samaan said softly at the side in Syriac, "The burial ground is monastery property; it's written in the register. If we don't allow it to be moved, the government can't force it either."

Jingjing knew this. He knew too that He Jingzong was a county commandant, and that if the He family meant to move her, sooner or later they could. He looked at Old He. Old He's hair was white; in his hand he was gripping a string of prayer beads, gripping it until his knuckles were white. What this man wanted was the same thing Pa had wanted when he brought Du De's bowl back from Hebei: a son wanting to take his mother home.

"Move her," said Jingjing. "Before the third of next month the monastery will raise her itself; don't have the Jianfu Monastery's people come. The bones that are raised, the monastery will wrap in cloth and give to the He family, and the He family can bury them together."

Old He was taken aback; he had probably not expected him to agree so quickly. He bowed again and left with his people.

Samaan said, "Master, give way this once, and every family after will come to move theirs."

"Whatever family comes to move theirs, the monastery will raise them for that family," said Jingjing. "They're the monastery's people, and they're also people of their own families."

The day of the raising, Jingjing and Lingbao dug with spades, spadeful after spadeful, down to a layer of rotted wood, and beneath it the bones, very small, very light. Jingjing knelt in the pit and picked them up piece by piece and handed them to Shiyiniang, and Shiyiniang laid them on a piece of blue cloth washed pale, left over from mending Yisi's robe that year, laid them in order, head at the top, feet at the bottom. At the bottom of the pit there was also a small wooden cross, the character *mi* carved on the back, which Yisi had put in that year. Jingjing thought, and laid it too on the blue cloth, at Granny He's breast, and said, "Wrap it." The He family's servants carried off the bundle. Lingbao took the spade to fill in the earth, and Jingjing said not to fill it; leave it empty, so that the monastery's people passing here would know a person had lain here.

---

In the waste garden west of the Qujiang Pool in the south of the city there were more people every year. The first year there were four households, the second year nine; by the tenth year of Zhenyuan, Samaan had counted, there were seventeen households, forty-odd people. Baaluo had put up a straw shed in the garden, and in the middle of the shed stood a wooden cross he had whittled himself, without lotus, without clouds, just two pieces of wood nailed together. Every day before light he poured cold water over himself at the well, and when he had poured it sang the offices in the shed, told of the visions he had seen in Lingnan, told how the monastery in Chang'an had become unclean. Those who went to him were the people from other wards who couldn't get gruel outside the shed in the ward street, a few young monks from the monastery, and three or four Tang young men who had heard that the people there poured cold water over themselves every day, ate no meat, never went in at an official's gate, and thought that was true practice, and found their own way there.

They came to the monastery too, and did not go into the hall but stood at the gate. At the offering once in seven, when the hall came to the hymn in Tang, they sang in Syriac outside the gate, very loudly, drowning the voices in the hall. Some in the hall stopped and turned to look; some sang louder.

Sabrisho was so angry his beard shook. "Have the guard brothers drive them off!"

"No driving," said Jingjing. "They're standing in the ward street, and the ward street isn't the monastery's. What they're singing is the psalms, and the psalms aren't the monastery's either."

"And they're cursing the Master! They say the Master is the Son of Heaven's monk, they say the Master's Tang scriptures speak through the mouths of Buddhists and Daoists, they say that stele is an idol!"

"The first two, I've said myself. The third I don't accept, but I won't stop them saying it. The people in the hall hear it; they'll think for themselves, and when they've thought, decide for themselves."

"And if they decide wrong?"

"If they decide wrong, I'm still here, and the gate is open."

There was a young monk named An Yan, a Sogdian in his early twenties, whose Tang was the best of the monastery's young men. Every year Jingjing picked three who could preach in Tang, and An Yan had been picked in the seventh year of Zhenyuan. He went to the south of the city three times, and when he came back the third time, he came to the scripture room to find Jingjing and said he wanted to go and live in the south of the city a while.

"Why?" asked Jingjing.

"What Elder Baaluo says—I've listened, and half of it I think is right," said An Yan. "The monastery's people go to the Commissioners of Merit's office to be registered, the monastery treats the wounds of the Shence Army's soldiers, on the stele there are many of the Son of Heaven's names and few of the Lord's—I've seen all of it. I want to go and live there a while and see how the people there keep the rule."

"Go," said Jingjing. "When you've lived there enough and want to come back, the gate is open."

An Yan knelt and knocked his head to him, and left. Samaan made a line in the ledger: *The monk An Yan goes to the south of the city; the Master allowed it.* He raised his head and said, "Master, keep allowing it like this, and the monastery will empty."

"It won't empty. And if it empties, it'll be they who chose to go. The ones I stop will stay in the monastery with their hearts in the south of the city."

---

Xu Jing was past eighty that year and deaf, and still lived in that little room at the north end of the Yining Ward. Every day he leaned on his crutch and came to sit a while at the monastery gate, sitting beside the stone block watching people come and go, talking to everyone: about the rules of the road, about drinking ginger broth before you cross the Pamirs, about the beacon towers of Hexi, about those two fingers of his frozen off. "Gave the fingers to the beacon fire," he said, dozens of times a year, to the monks fetching water, to the children drawing gruel, to the pedlars passing by. "Gave them to the beacon fire. No loss." On the hottest day that summer, at noon, Lingbao went out to call him to eat, and he was leaning against the stone block as if asleep, an empty water skin gripped in his hand. Jingjing wrote the name Xu Jing on his stake, and beneath it a line in small characters: *Crossbowman of Hedong.*

Of the seven who had come east from Ctesiphon in those days, Yonan and Narsai were already gone, and the rest were all men of seventy or eighty. Babai's eyes had dimmed and he could no longer copy scripture; every day he sat in the sun at the scripture-room door listening to Lingbao read. Gabriel's voice had gone. Haddad's ears were still good; he heard everything, and said nothing about any of it. Ishaq had been fat all his life and now had grown thin, though his belly was still round. Sabrisho still kept the rules every day, and kept them, and argued with Jingjing. Samaan's hand shook, and the characters of his accounts grew bigger every year.

---

In the winter of the twelfth year of Zhenyuan, Shiyiniang fell ill. She was past fifty that year, her hair half white. After winter set in she coughed, coughed a month without getting better, ran a fever at night, the fever went down and came up again. The son of Shopkeeper Wang came to see her and wrote a prescription; she took the medicine, and the cough eased a few days and then grew worse again. She knew herself.

In the twelfth month she sent Lingbao to ask Jingjing to come. She was sitting up in bed with two pillows behind her, her face worn down until there was only a pair of eyes left, and the eyes as bright as ever. She sent Lingbao out, waited until the door was closed, and only then said, "Jingjing, there are three things I want to entrust to you."

"Auntie, tell me."

"The first: when I die, I'm to be buried outside the Kaiyuan Gate, beside your Pa. I told your Pa this, and he said yes."

"I know."

"The second." She stopped and coughed a while. "My first husband, Cheng Yuan, died at Xiangzhou in the second year of Qianyuan, and only a scabbard came back. The scabbard is buried in the Cheng grave in the south of the city. The Cheng mother-in-law died later in the monastery; it was you who laid her out, too. All these years, every Cold Food, I've gone to the Cheng grave and burned paper once. I never hid it from your Pa, and your Pa never stopped me. When I'm dead there'll be no one left to go for the Chengs. I'd like Lingbao to go every Cold Food." She looked at Jingjing. "That Baaluo in the south of the city says that when the monastery's people burn paper they're sacrificing to ghosts. I know it puts you in a hard place too. You say the word—whether Lingbao goes, whether he burns or not, I'll do as you say."

Jingjing thought a while and said, "Let Lingbao go. Whether he burns paper or not, let him decide himself. If he goes and reads a passage of scripture at the grave for the Cheng family, that's good too."

Shiyiniang nodded. "If your Pa were here, he'd probably say the same."

"And the third?"

"The third is Lingbao." Her voice sank. "That child was born in this monastery. On the third day, at his washing, Elder Narsai said no, and it was you who tried the water. From four years old Elder Narsai taught him his letters, and from his teens he copied scripture with you. When his great-uncle was alive he told me several times that Lingbao wrote a fine Tang hand and was sure to pass the classics examination, and if he passed, the Pei family still had a few people in the Metropolitan Prefecture who could look after him. I asked Lingbao, and Lingbao said he wouldn't sit it; he'd stay with his brother. I'm his mother, and in my heart I'm glad; but I'm afraid too—afraid that staying with you he'll have a hard life all his life. What the monastery is like these days, I can see."

She reached out and took hold of Jingjing's sleeve. "I don't ask you to look after him. He's your brother; there's no need for me to ask. I ask only one thing: from now on, if you're tired, whatever you have on your mind, tell him. Don't carry it alone. Your Pa carried things alone all his life, carried them till he died, and only then told you that thing."

Jingjing sat by the bed and lowered his head. Shiyiniang reached out and touched his head, the way, when he was small, she had touched his head after she finished mending his robe.

She died in the night of the twenty-third of the twelfth month. Before she died she had Lingbao read scripture to her, the passage in Tang, *In the highest heavens, deep reverence and praise; the great earth holds the thought of peace for all.* Lingbao read and read and could not go on, and Jingjing took it up and read it to the end, and when he had finished Shiyiniang was asleep, and sleeping, her breath was gone. She was buried beside Yisi. On the day of the burial An Yan came from the south of the city, alone, knelt before her grave and knocked his head three times, rose, and said to Jingjing, "Master Yi's lady mended my robe once, that year." He said nothing else, and said nothing of whether he would come back, and left.

---

At Cold Food the next year Lingbao went to the Cheng grave in the south of the city.

He took a stack of paper, a small jar of wine, and the book of the Tang scriptures. The Cheng grave lay at the edge of a wheat field. The mound was lower than he remembered. Ma had come every year and every year added a double handful of earth, and this year no one had added any. He knelt before the grave, first read the scripture, and when he had read it, took out the stack of paper and held it in his hand.

He remembered how Ma had crouched here every Cold Food: crouched, not knelt, burning sheet by sheet, saying a sentence for each sheet she burned. What she said, he had never heard clearly. When he was small he asked her, and she said she was telling Brother Cheng about things at home. He asked whether Brother Cheng could hear, and she said, whether he hears or not, once you've said it, your heart can let it go.

Holding the stack of paper, he knelt before the grave a good while. If he burned it, the people in the south of the city would say he sacrificed to ghosts. If he didn't, he would have done only half of what Ma had entrusted to him. The wind came across from the wheat field and lifted the corners of the paper, and he gripped it tight. In the end he didn't burn it. He picked up a stone from the foot of the grave and weighed the stack of paper down with it on top of the mound, added a double handful of earth to the mound, poured the jar of wine out before the grave, knocked his head, and came back.

When he came back he did not tell Jingjing whether he had burned it or not. But the people in the south of the city told. One household in Baaluo's garden had a family grave in that same stretch, and on the day of Cold Food saw Lingbao kneeling before the Cheng grave with paper in his hands; they did not see whether he burned it, and went back and told it. A few days later the people from the south of the city stood at the monastery gate, and when they had finished singing in Syriac at the offering once in seven, someone shouted: "A deacon of the Chang'an monastery went to a grave to burn paper and sacrifice to ghosts! The Father of the Monks' own brother sacrifices to ghosts!"

Everyone in the hall heard it. Lingbao, kneeling in the hall, went scarlet and was about to stand, but Jingjing looked at him once from the front, and he knelt again.

When worship was over Lingbao went to the scripture room to find Jingjing. "Brother, I didn't burn it! I'll go and tell them."

"You didn't burn it," said Jingjing. "But you held paper in your hands, and you poured out wine before the grave. Didn't you?"

"Yes."

"Then they say you sacrificed to ghosts, and you say you didn't burn anything; then they say you poured wine, and you say the wine wasn't a sacrifice; then they say what was it then. By the end, all you're talking about is yourself, and you never get to the Lord."

Lingbao stood a long while and said, "Then let them say it."

"Let them say it. You know in your own heart what you were reading as you knelt there. Your Ma knows, and the Lord knows. That's enough."

Lingbao did not leave. He stood there looking at his brother, and suddenly said, "Brother, this is how you've been your whole life. Whatever people say, you let them say it. Aren't you tired?"

Jingjing looked at him and remembered what Shiyiniang had said.

"I'm tired," he said. "Every morning I get up and stand a while in the middle of the courtyard, tell the knots once, read a passage of scripture, write one character. When it's written, I'm not so tired. The character I've written, I carry through the day, carry it till dark, and set it down, and the next day take up another. From the day Auntie fell ill, what's been written on my board has been the one character *home*, all winter, and it's still that character today." He paused. "If you're willing, tomorrow morning, get up with me."

The next morning, before the clapper, Lingbao got up and stood in the middle of the courtyard, and Jingjing stood beside him. Jingjing took the string of knots from his breast, told one knot, and handed it to Lingbao; Lingbao took it, told another, and handed it back. When the edge of the sky turned grey the two of them went into the scripture room. Jingjing opened the psalms and turned to *I will lift up mine eyes unto the hills*; in the spring of the year Azarel died, the first time he had stood in the middle of the courtyard, it was this psalm he had turned to as well. He wiped the character *home* off the board, handed Lingbao a stick of charcoal, and said, "Today you write."
