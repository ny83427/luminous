# Volume Nine　The Translation Hall

## Chapter 53　Timothy

> When the virtue of Zhou declined, the blue chariot ascended into the west; now that the Way of the great Tang shines, the Luminous wind blows east.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

The year Jingfu was nine, he broke wind at the morning office.

It was the autumn of the twentieth year of Zhenyuan, just after daybreak; the lamps were lit in the hall, and the people of the monastery knelt in three rows singing the office. Halfway through, a long chant had just ended and the next had not yet begun, and for a moment the hall was quiet.

In that moment there came a *pff*.

The sound was very loud, very long, took a turn, and had a little tail to it.

The children in the front row were the first to crack, their shoulders heaving. The old believers at the back bowed their heads lower; one could not hold it, and let a snort escape, and hastily covered his mouth with his sleeve. Siyuan, kneeling beside Jingfu, looked straight ahead with no expression at all on his face, but his ears went red.

Jingjing stood at the front, his back to them all. He did not turn round. He paused, raised the tune of the next chant, and sang on.

The others sang after him. The singing was a little ragged; it was ragged a few lines before it came together.

When the morning office ended, the children rushed out of the hall in a swarm. One bigger boy ran at the back, made faces at Jingfu, held his nose and said: "Iron Belly's sprung a leak."

Jingfu's face flushed bright red all at once.

"It wasn't me," he said. "It was Brother Siyuan."

Siyuan was just coming out of the hall, heard this, and his feet paused. He looked at Jingfu once, said nothing, bowed his head and went.

---

That noon, Jingjing called Jingfu to his room.

When Jingfu came in at the door his face was still red. He stood by the door, his two hands behind his back, his eyes on the floor.

Jingjing did not tell him to sit, nor scold him. He took two bowls from the cupboard—his own, and the one Jingfu used every day—set them on the table, and poured a little hot water into each.

"Sit," he said.

Jingfu sat.

"Let me tell you something," said Jingjing. "Something from when I was little."

Jingfu raised his eyes to him once, and lowered them again.

"The year I was ten, in Lingzhou," said Jingjing, "that second month a guest came to the house, and I sat with my pa to eat with him. A pot of mutton soup, a dish of bread, a dish of ginger. In the middle of eating, I broke wind."

Jingfu's eyes came up again. This time they did not go down.

"The night before I'd drunk cold water," said Jingjing. "My stomach was bad. That one was louder than yours today."

"The Master breaks wind too?"

"I do," said Jingjing. "More than you."

The corner of Jingfu's mouth twitched, and he pressed it shut quickly.

"That guest was an old soldier," said Jingjing, "sent by Lord Guo to fetch my pa, sitting right across from me, not two feet away. He heard it perfectly clearly. My face was red then too, as red as yours now. I said to my pa: it's shameful."

"What did your pa say?"

"First he told me to eat some ginger."

Jingfu could not help it, and laughed once.

"Then he said," said Jingjing, "what shames a man is a lie, not wind."

Jingfu's laugh stopped.

The room was silent a while. The kettle on the stove went *gudu*, *gudu*; outside the window a sparrow landed on the sill, hopped twice, and flew off.

Jingjing said no more. He picked up his bowl of hot water and slowly drank a mouthful.

Jingfu sat there with his head down, looking at the hot water in his bowl. Looked a long time.

Then he stood up and went to the door. At the door he turned back.

"Master," he said, "that old soldier—did he laugh at you afterwards?"

Jingjing thought.

"No," he said. "He told a camp joke right there and then, and made me laugh too. And all his life after he never once mentioned it."

Jingfu nodded and ran out.

Jingjing sat in the room and heard him running in the courtyard, running over towards the scripture room, and stopping. Through the wall he heard Jingfu say loudly: "Brother Siyuan, it was me."

He did not hear what Siyuan said. After a while he heard Jingfu run again, faster than before.

---

In the first month of the next year, Emperor Dezong died.

Chang'an hung white, and the ward gates were shut for some days. The new Son of Heaven came to the throne, and within a few months passed it on to the crown prince. People in the city said: this year emperors are changing faster than era names.

That autumn, Mi Jifen died too.

To the day of his death he never said for certain how old he was. His family reckoned it up and said he was past ninety. The Lord's Day before he died, his servants still held him up and brought him to the monastery, and he sat in the last row by the door and dozed through half the service. Jingfu sat beside him, gripping one of his fingers.

Siyuan led the funeral.

Jingjing stood to one side and watched Siyuan recite the prayers. Siyuan recited very steadily, without one word wrong, and at the end his voice did not shake. When he had finished, he patted flat the earth on his father's coffin, knelt and knocked his head three times. When he stood up, Jingjing saw two patches of wet mud on his knees.

Later the Mi family had an epitaph carved for Mi Jifen, saying what country he was from, what offices he had held, how long he lived, how many sons he had. Jingjing did not go to look at the epitaph. He heard that when it came to the younger son Siyuan, it said in passing: *dwelling at the Daqin monastery*.

In the whole epitaph, the words *Daqin monastery* appeared that once.

Jingjing thought: once is once.

---

The Patriarchate's letter arrived in the spring of the third year of Yuanhe.

It came with a Uighur caravan. The caravan had come round by the north, not by the Hexi road. That Hexi road had been cut more than twenty years before. The Tibetans had taken Liangzhou, taken Ganzhou, taken Shazhou; the Tang post stations were abandoned one by one, the Tang beacon towers fell in one by one. Now, to go west from Chang'an, you had first to go north to the Uighur lands, then circle west, round the Tianshan, before you could join the road to the Two Rivers.

So this letter had been nearly two years on the road.

It was written on parchment, folded into a long strip, tied with fine cord, and the knot sealed with black wax. On the wax was pressed the Patriarch's seal. The look of the seal Jingjing knew. It was the same pattern as the seal he had seen as a boy under that lamp in Lingzhou; only the ring of letters round the edge was different.

He held the letter and sat in the scripture room a long time before he opened it.

The writer was Timothy.

This Patriarch had been on his throne nearly thirty years. Jingjing had heard of him from merchants who came from the west: that his learning was very great, that he had debated the Way before the Caliph, debated two days, and the Caliph had not said he was wrong. That his reach was very long too: he had sent metropolitans north to the Turks, and was making plans towards Tibet as well.

The letter was very courteous, and inside the courtesy was something very hard.

The letter first greeted the people of the monastery in Chang'an, and said the Patriarchate had had no sure report from the East for many years; of the words brought by merchants, the true were few and the false many. It said too that it had heard a stele had been raised in Chang'an, and the Patriarchate wished to know whether what was written on it was true.

Then it came to the business.

It said the Patriarchate meant to set the province of China in order anew. The metropolitan's seat had been empty many years, and the Patriarchate could not let it stay empty. Either someone would be sent east from the Patriarchate, or someone would be chosen in the East to come west, be ordained at the Patriarchate, and go back. Which was better, the Patriarchate wished first to hear the mind of the East.

"If the one who has charge of the monastery in Chang'an," the letter said, "could come to the Patriarchate and set it out in person, that would be best."

Jingjing read this sentence three times.

He read on. At the end of the letter Timothy had written one sentence that had nothing to do with the business.

"I hear that there was once an archdeacon at the Patriarchate who in his lifetime often asked those who came from the east: is the road still open? When I took office I went through his old papers, and found that in the margin of one sheet he had written a child's name. I think that child must be old by now too."

Jingjing put the letter down.

---

He wanted to go.

Once the thought came up, he could not press it down.

He was sixty-two. Twice in his life he had been to the Patriarchate. The first time at ten, on a camel's back, tied to the pack frame with rope by Xu Jing, half a year on the road. The second time at fifteen, with the elder Marcus, another half year. The second time, his uncle was still there, and his uncle asked him: is the road still open? He said: it's open. I've walked it once.

That was the last time he saw his uncle.

His uncle had died at the Patriarchate and been buried at Ctesiphon. Now the Patriarchate had moved to Baghdad, and that grave at Ctesiphon—whether anyone still went to pull the grass, he did not know.

He wanted to see that grave once. He wanted to stand before Timothy and tell him, one by one, the things of these forty years and more in the East. He wanted to hear someone ask him once more: is the road still open?

He went first to Li Su.

Li Su was past sixty that year; he had been an official at the Astronomical Bureau for decades and had risen very high, but he was still the same—thin, slow of speech, counting on his fingers whatever he reckoned. He kept a great many books at home, Tang, Persian, Indian, piled in one room so that only a crack was left to walk through.

Jingjing showed him the letter. When Li Su had read it he gave it back and asked: "You want to go?"

"I do."

Li Su said neither good nor bad. He got up, drew a roll of map from the pile of books, spread it on the floor, and held it down with two paperweights. On the map were mountains, rivers, city after city, their names noted beside them in both Tang characters and *hu* letters.

He pointed with his finger at the map, place by place, as he spoke. Hexi is the Tibetans' now; you know that. Beiting is the Tibetans' now too; a dozen years ago the Uighurs and the Tibetans fought several battles there, fought back and forth, and the city changed masters several times; who holds it now, the caravans say different things. Going west, you can only keep close to the Uighur lands, as far as Suyab, and then turn south.

"All along the way," said Li Su, "there are Uighurs, Qarluqs, Tibetans, Arabs. Which stretch belongs to whom is different this year from next."

Jingjing squatted beside the map and looked. He knew some of the place names on it. He had walked them at ten, and walked them again at fifteen. In those days all these places flew Tang banners.

"A year going," said Li Su again, "a year coming back. You're sixty-two."

As he said it his fingers were still counting, as if reckoning an account.

Jingjing said: "My pa died at fifty-nine."

Li Su glanced at him and rolled up the map.

"Then you've had three years more already," he said.

Neither of them laughed.

---

He went back to the monastery, called Siyuan, and told him.

When Siyuan had heard it, he said nothing a long while. Then he said: "Master, the Hexi road is impassable."

"We'll go by the north."

"The north takes a year."

"A year, then."

Again Siyuan said nothing. He looked at Jingjing, looked a while, and said: "Master's stomach—the water in the north is all cold."

Jingjing did not answer.

Jingfu heard from outside the door. He was thirteen that year, already as tall as Jingjing, standing at the door with one hand on the frame.

"I'll go with the Master," he said.

"You won't," said Jingjing.

"Why?"

"If you go, who'll light the lamp?"

Jingfu opened his mouth, and no words came.

---

Jingjing began to pack all the same.

He had Siyuan go to the West Market to find that Uighur caravan; the caravan was going back in the autumn and could take people. He laid the things he would take one by one on the bed: two robes to change into, a roll of scripture, a packet of ginger, a packet of *haritaki*, a pile of paper, a brush, a stick of ink.

Round his neck hung his grandfather's wooden cross, and in his robe was the elder Ahad's knotted cord. These two needed no packing; he always carried them.

He also wrote an answer to Timothy, meaning to hand it over in person. The letter ran to a dozen pages and more: how many were in the congregation of the monastery in Chang'an, what property the monastery had, how many scriptures, how things stood with the court. He wrote of Lord Guo, of his father, of the stele. When he came to that affair of the second year of Zhenyuan, he stopped his brush, thought a while, and wrote it in after all:

"There is a government document, which says your servant's meaning is obscure and his language thin."

When he had written this sentence he looked at it himself, and smiled.

---

The day the caravan left, Jingjing went out of the monastery gate before it was light.

Jingfu wanted to see him off, and he would not let him. He said: you stay at home and lead the morning office. Jingfu stood at the monastery gate and said nothing more. Siyuan led the camel for him, and saw him all the way to the Kaiyuan Gate.

The Kaiyuan Gate was the gate of Chang'an that faced west. The gate passage was very deep, and walking in it your footsteps hummed. Outside the gate was a great road going west, stretching away out of sight. By the gate stood a stone carved with a few words, saying how many *li* it was from here west to Anxi. The number was very large, so large it looked like a mistake.

The Uighur caravan was already waiting outside the gate. The camels knelt on the ground one by one, the loads not all on yet, and the merchants walked about among the camels, speaking a tongue Jingjing half understood.

Jingjing stood in the gate passage, and did not walk out.

He thought of his father.

That year his father had stood at the courtyard gate in Lingzhou watching him ride off on a camel. His father had not seen him past the mouth of the lane. Later he had asked his father why he had not seen him off that day. His father said: if I'd seen you off, I wouldn't have wanted to come back.

He thought of something else his father had said. It was the year he was ten, when he asked his father why he would not come with him to the Patriarchate. His father said: I follow Lord Guo. Lord Guo wants me to watch the road. If I go, there'll be no one to watch the road.

Then he had not understood. He had thought the road his father meant was the road from Chang'an to Ctesiphon.

He stood in the gate passage of the Kaiyuan Gate, stood a long time. The head of the caravan came over twice to say it was time to go. Siyuan stood beside him holding the camel, and did not make a sound.

Then he turned round, took from his robe that letter of a dozen pages and more, and handed it to the head of the caravan.

"This letter," he said, "take it to Baghdad and give it to the Patriarch."

The head of the caravan took it, weighed it in his hand, and said: "And the man?"

"The man isn't going."

The head of the caravan gave him a look, probably finding this old *hu* monk a little odd, asked no more, and stuffed the letter into a pack.

Jingjing took the camel's lead from Siyuan, untied that small bundle from the pack, and carried it himself.

When he got back to the monastery that day it was noon. He opened the bundle and put the things back in their places one by one. The robes back in the cupboard, the scripture back in the scripture room, the paper and brush back on the table. Last of all was that packet of ginger.

He took the packet of ginger to the stove, and set it on the stove.

---

Afterwards he added another letter to that one, sent by the next caravan. The added one was very short, only a few lines.

"The one who has charge of the monastery in Chang'an cannot come. The province of China, let the Patriarchate arrange as it sees fit. The affairs of the East are set out in full in the earlier letter.

"The Patriarch asks: is the road still open? The road is still open; only you have to go round.

"Jingjing, at Chang'an"
