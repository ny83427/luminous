# Volume Five　Chang'an

## Chapter 23　The Polo Mallet

> Though the dragon's beard is far away, the bow and sword may still be grasped; the brow like the sun spreads its light, and the heavenly face is a foot away.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

In the autumn of the first year of Qianyuan, Guo Ziyi and Governor Li came to court together.

They came by two roads, Guo Ziyi from Hedong, Governor Li from Taiyuan. The day they entered the city, they were less than half a day apart; the men and horses in front had only just passed through the Chunming Gate when the banners behind showed their heads outside the walls. The people of the city stood along the streets to watch, and when they had seen one, waited to see the other. Some said the two had arranged to come in one after the other; some said it was chance.

The men in the camps knew it was not chance, and not arranged either. The two governors spoke to each other now, but did not speak much, and had no need to arrange anything.

The Son of Heaven gave a banquet in the palace, bestowed offices, bestowed gold and silk. These things the men in the camps only heard of, and did not see. What they saw was something else: the Son of Heaven wanted to watch a ball game.

---

The game was polo.

Polo was not like football. Football was played with the feet; polo was played on horseback with a mallet in your hand, its head curved like a crescent moon, so it was also called the moon-stick. The ball was wooden, painted in colours, the size of a fist, and struck, it flew fast and hard. If it hit a man, it broke bone; if it hit a horse, the horse bolted.

The people of Chang'an loved polo, from the palace down to the wards. The old soldiers in camp said that when the Retired Emperor was young, still Prince of Linzi, he had played a match against envoys from Tibet. Tibet put up ten men and the Prince of Linzi's side only four—four against ten—and they won. Emperor Zhongzong watched from the tower above and was so pleased he slapped the balustrade.

Some said too that the Retired Emperor had taken a fall at polo later on, fallen from his horse, and not lightly. When he was better, he played again.

Whether these tales were true no one went to find out. The Retired Emperor now lived in the Xingqing Palace, his hair all white, and it was said he seldom went out.

The polo ground was in the north of the city, beside the imperial park, a piece of ground built up perfectly level. The ground was first rammed with earth, and when it was rammed hard, oil was poured over it, poured and dried in the sun, dried and poured again, until in the end the surface was level and hard and shone so you could see your reflection in it. Horses galloping on it went *da-da* under their hooves and raised not a grain of dust.

Each side put up six riders. Guo Ziyi on one side, Governor Li on the other.

---

On Guo Ziyi's side, Hou Siyuan led.

Hou Siyuan had filled out since Changshan, his legs still long and thin, and on horseback it was as if his legs had grown into the horse. In the camp he was a famous hand at polo: football was his game, polo was his life.

Cheng Chongyi came too. That ankle of his, twisted once at Changshan and twisted again later, now made him limp a little when he walked. But once on a horse, you could not tell.

The other four were picked from the camp. Yisi was one.

It was Hou Siyuan who picked him. Hou Siyuan said: "Master Yi, that pass of yours last time—I didn't take it. This time it's not feet, it's the mallet. I'll take it."

Yisi said: "I can't play polo."

Hou Siyuan said: "You can ride, and you can read men. Those two, and you can play half of it."

"And the other half?"

"The other half," said Hou Siyuan, "you just watch me."

Yisi rode his chestnut gelding. Guo Ziyi had given him the horse; it had been with him three years, had been to Changshan, been to Xiangji Temple, and had an arrow scar on its haunch from the second scouting run east. The horse was not young, and did not run as fast as those fine horses from the Western Regions, but it was steady and obedient; whichever way Yisi leaned, it went.

Old Duan, at the edge of the ground, watched Yisi lead the horse over and said: "That horse is like you. Been shot, not much to look at, and tough."

Yisi patted the horse's neck and said nothing.

---

On Governor Li's side there were a few familiar faces too.

Of the men who had played against Yisi in that match at Changshan, there had been four or five. Some had died in the rearguard fight, some had died later, and of those left, three had come today. The three sat their horses and from a distance cupped their fists to Yisi. Yisi cupped his fist too.

Of the three, one was missing.

Yisi dismounted and took something out of the saddlebag behind his saddle. It was a coarse earthenware wine bowl with a chip in its rim and a pinch of earth in it.

He carried the bowl in both hands to the edge of the ground on Governor Li's side.

At the edge there was a row of folding stools for the players to rest on. Six riders, six stools, and one more, empty, kept for a substitute. Yisi went up to that empty stool, bent down, and set the bowl on it.

As he bent, that old wound in his back pulled. He paused, set the bowl steady, and only then straightened up.

The three on Governor Li's side saw it.

They said nothing. One dismounted first, then the other two dismounted, came over, and stood before the stool looking at the bowl.

One of them squatted down, reached out, and touched the chip in the rim.

"It's his," the man said.

Another said: "When he drank, he always drank from the chipped side. Said the chipped side was cooler on the mouth."

All three laughed. Laughed once, and stopped.

---

On Governor Li's side there was a young one who did not know the bowl.

His name was Xue Rang, nineteen, taken into Governor Li's camp only that year, a Hedong man, tall, with something of a child still in his face. He had not been one of the six; the day before, someone had fallen from his horse and hurt his arm, and he had been put in to fill the place. He sat his horse watching those three old soldiers squatting before the stool, watching that broken bowl with earth in it, and did not understand.

He asked the man beside him: "Whose is that?"

The man beside him told him. Du De, of Duling in the capital district, a soldier at sixteen, held the walls of Changshan more than forty days, scored a fine goal in the football match outside Jiumen, and when they pulled out of Hebei held the rear for the time it takes to drink a cup of tea.

Xue Rang heard it out and said nothing. He looked at the bowl a while, then at Yisi, and gripped the mallet in his hand tighter.

---

The ball was thrown up.

It was tossed in the middle of the ground, and twelve horses charged at it together. The hooves on the oiled ground drummed like a sudden downpour. The mallets swung up, their heads flashing in the sun.

Hou Siyuan got the ball first. He bent low, almost flat along his horse's neck, hooked with the mallet head, and the ball rolled away skimming the ground, his horse running after it, running faster than the ball.

Two of Governor Li's riders closed in on him from either side.

Hou Siyuan flicked the ball aside.

To Yisi.

Yisi put out his mallet, did not strike, only blocked. The ball struck the mallet head and stopped. He did not look towards the goal; first he looked to either side. On his left was Cheng Chongyi, with a man on his tail; on his right Hou Siyuan, who had just shaken off those two riders and was cutting forward.

He pushed the ball to Hou Siyuan.

Not hard, not soft; the ball rolled half a step in front of Hou Siyuan's horse, Hou Siyuan swung, and the ball flew up and into the goal.

One point. Hou Siyuan wheeled his horse round, and passing Yisi grinned and said: "Saw it this time."

The crowd at the edge shouted. Old Duan shouted loudest, and when he had shouted turned to Xu Jing beside him and said: "See that? He still won't hit it."

Xu Jing said: "He can't hit it."

Old Duan said: "He can. He just won't."

---

Polo was a dangerous game.

Lose at football and at worst you twisted an ankle. Polo was different: twelve horses turning on a patch of ground no bigger than your palm, and when they turned hard, horse ran into horse, man into man, a mallet caught a man in the face, the ball caught a horse in the eye—all of it happened all the time. In Chang'an every year someone was killed at polo, princes and young lords, and the strong young men of the army too.

That day was dangerous too.

The second point went to Governor Li's side. The man who scored was an old soldier, and having scored he could not pull up his horse, crashed into the wooden rail at the edge, and was thrown off and rolled a long way across the oiled ground. He got up with his face all blood, and still laughing.

The third point was Guo Ziyi's side again. Yisi intercepted a ball from the back, did not send it forward, but passed it back to Cheng Chongyi. Cheng Chongyi took it and lofted it forward, and Hou Siyuan put it in with a stroke in front of goal.

The fourth point, Governor Li's side.

Two all.

---

On the last ball, something happened.

The ball was in the middle of the ground, the horses of both sides jammed together in a knot. Xue Rang was charging at the very front. He had been charging since the first throw-in, charging as if he did not care whether he lived, going after every ball, going after it even when he could not reach it. Some said he wanted to show himself off in front of the governors; the three old soldiers on Governor Li's side knew it was because he had looked at that bowl.

Someone struck the ball and it came flying out slantwise. Xue Rang stretched out his mallet to reach it, leaned too far, the horse swerved, and he slid off its back.

One foot was still caught in the stirrup.

The horse took fright and bolted, dragging him along. Three or four horses behind could not pull up, and came charging straight at him.

Everyone at the edge stood up.

Yisi was nearest. He did not go to pull Xue Rang free, nor to rein in the bolting horse. He pressed his horse's flanks, and the chestnut cut in slantwise, between Xue Rang and the horses behind.

The leading horse crashed into the chestnut's shoulder.

Both horses staggered. Yisi swayed in the saddle, and the wound in his back felt as if someone had drawn a knife along it again. He clenched his teeth and did not let go of the reins. The chestnut snorted, its four hooves slid two steps on the oiled ground, and it stood.

The horses behind parted on either side and went round.

Ahead, Hou Siyuan had caught up with the bolting horse and seized it by the bit. Xue Rang's foot came out of the stirrup; he lay on the ground a while, then sat up, his face covered in oil, one sleeve torn open, a great patch of skin scraped off his arm.

He looked up at Yisi.

Yisi looked down at him and said: "Still playing?"

Xue Rang got up and said: "Still playing."

---

The ball was thrown up again.

This time it fell on Guo Ziyi's side.

Hou Siyuan got it and passed to Yisi. Yisi took it and carried it a few steps forward. Ahead was open; Governor Li's men were all still in the back of the ground and had not come back. The goal was just ahead, twenty paces.

The edge of the ground went quiet.

Old Duan shouted from the edge: "Yisi! Your stick won't slip this time, eh?"

Yisi did not look round. He pushed the ball lightly to the left.

Hou Siyuan cut in from the left. One stroke.

Three to two.

The edge of the ground exploded. The men of Guo Ziyi's camp poured onto the ground, dragged Hou Siyuan off his horse and threw him into the air. Hou Siyuan shouted in mid-air, and what he shouted no one could make out.

Yisi sat on his horse and did not go over. He saw Xue Rang ride slowly to the edge of the ground on Governor Li's side and stop before the stool where the bowl was. Xue Rang did not dismount; he bent his head in the saddle and looked at the bowl a while, then turned his horse and rode off.

---

That evening there was another banquet.

As at Changshan, the governors were in the main hall and the players in the side hall. In the side hall again two long tables were pushed together, Guo Ziyi's men on one side, Governor Li's on the other. Wine jars, mutton, steamed buns, flatbreads—more than at Changshan, and better too.

Yisi had brought the bowl.

He set it on Governor Li's side, at the end of the long table, in front of an empty place. No one sat in that place. All evening no one went to sit there.

After the third round of wine, the three old soldiers took turns carrying their wine to the empty place, touching their bowls to the rim of that bowl, then drinking. They did not speak as they touched it. When they had done it, they went back and sat down and went on arguing with Guo Ziyi's men about which point was a foul and which stroke had hit a horse's leg.

Xue Rang, his arm bound in cloth, came over carrying wine too. He stood before the empty place a while, not knowing how to touch it. He looked at the three old soldiers and, copying them, touched his bowl lightly against the rim of that bowl. *Ding.*

Then he came over to Yisi.

"*Hu* master," he said. "Today—thank you."

Yisi said: "You played well."

Xue Rang said: "I lost."

Yisi said: "He lost too, once."

Xue Rang was taken aback, looked back once at the bowl, and understood whom Yisi meant. He said nothing more, made Yisi a bow, and went back.

Cheng Chongyi had heard it from beside them, and leaned over and said under his breath: "Master Yi, at Changshan you gave him one goal. Today's better still—three goals, all given to Hou Two."

Yisi said: "Hou Two plays well."

"So do you."

Yisi bent his head over his broth and said nothing.

---

When the banquet was nearly over, a guard came over from the main hall and found Yisi.

The guard was one of Governor Li's own men, not tall, his face set hard, like his master's. He handed Yisi a folded slip of paper and said: "From the Governor."

Yisi took it and opened it.

On the paper were only two characters, in very thick ink, written very hard, every stroke as if carved with a knife.

安歸.

Yisi looked at the two characters, looked a long time.

He knew the two characters. 安, *an*, was the *an* of peace; 歸, *gui*, was the *gui* of going back. He did not know what Governor Li meant to say. He looked up for the guard, and the guard had already gone.

Jingjing was sitting by the door of the side hall; he had come with Old Duan, sat there all evening, and eaten a bellyful of mutton. He saw his father looking at a slip of paper, came over, stood behind him, and looked.

"Pa," said Jingjing under his breath, "these two characters can be read two ways."

Yisi said: "Mm."

"One is: go home in peace," said Jingjing. "The other is: go back—where?"

Yisi said nothing.

He folded the slip, folded it into a very narrow strip. Then he went to the end of the long table, to the empty place, and laid the strip in that bowl, gently, on top of the pinch of earth.
