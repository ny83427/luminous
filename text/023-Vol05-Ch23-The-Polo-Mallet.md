# Volume Five　Chang'an

## Chapter 23　The Polo Mallet

> Though the dragon's beard be far, the bow and sword may yet be grasped; the sun-crowned brow sheds its light, and the heavenly face is but a foot away.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

In the eighth month of the first year of Qianyuan, Guo Ziyi came to court.

He came from the river. That spring and summer he had fought An Qingxu's men along the river several times and captured the rebel general An Shouzhong, whom he brought with him to the capital to present as a captive. The Son of Heaven issued an edict that the hundred officials should go out of the city to the Changle post station to meet him; the Son of Heaven himself climbed the Wangchun Tower at the east end of the Forbidden Park and waited there.

The Changle station stood below the Changle slope east of the city, on the west bank of the River Chan. That day the officials' horses were lined up from outside the Tonghua Gate all the way to the station gate, purple robes, scarlet robes, green robes, patch after patch of them in the autumn sun.

Yisi had gone out of the Tonghua Gate early that morning. A few of the attendants Guo Ziyi's household had left in the capital had come to the Yining Ward the day before to tell him, and to have him come and meet him with them. He stood in the attendants' line outside the station gate and watched from afar as Guo Ziyi dismounted. Guo Ziyi returned the salutes one by one, and by the end could no longer bend at the waist, and only cupped his hands.

A few days later, Li Guangbi came too, from Taiyuan.

The two of them entered the capital one after the other and went up to the hall one after the other. Guo Ziyi was raised to Director of the Secretariat; Li Guangbi too was given higher office. The Son of Heaven gave them a banquet in the palace, gave them gold and silk, gave them mansions. All this, the people of the city knew only by hearsay.

Two days after that, someone came from Guo Ziyi's household and said: the day after tomorrow there's polo in the Forbidden Park, the Sage is to watch, and Lord Guo wants you to go with him.

---

The game was horse polo.

Polo was not like football. Football was played with the feet; polo was played on horseback, with a mallet in the hand whose head was bent like a crescent moon, so that it was also called the moon-mallet, or the ball-mallet. The ball was of wood, painted in colours, the size of a fist, and when it was struck it flew fast and hard. If it hit a man, it broke bones; if it hit a horse, the horse bolted.

Chang'an loved polo, from the palace down to the wards. The old soldiers in the camp said that when the Retired Emperor was young, still the Prince of Linzi, he had once played a match against envoys come from Tibet. Tibet put up ten men, and the Prince of Linzi's side only four—four against ten. The Prince of Linzi charged east and west across the field like a gust of wind, like a flash of lightning, so that the Tibetans could not bring any of their skill to bear, and he won. The Emperor Zhongzong, watching from the tower, gave a reward of several hundred lengths of silk. Later, when he had become Son of Heaven, he played before the halls, and once the Prince of Rong fell from his horse and lay senseless half a day; and the player Huang Fanchuo admonished him, saying that His Majesty was no longer young, and heavy in body, and if a horse's strength should fail and he took a fall, whom would the empire look to then? From that time on the Retired Emperor seldom took the field himself, and only watched from the tower.

The Retired Emperor now lived in the Xingqing Palace. His hair had gone all white, and it was said he seldom went out.

The polo ground was in the Forbidden Park, a piece of ground made perfectly level. First it was rammed with earth, and when the earth was rammed hard, oil was poured over it, poured and sunned, sunned and poured again, until at last the surface was flat and hard and so glossy it would show your reflection. Horses running on it clattered under their hooves and raised not a speck of dust. At either end of the ground stood a goal, and on the north side stood a viewing tower, two storeys high, hung with curtains on its upper floor, where the Son of Heaven sat.

Each side put up eight horsemen, Guo Ziyi's on one side, Li Guangbi's on the other. By the old rule of the palace, the first to three tallies won.

---

On Guo Ziyi's side, Hou Siyuan led.

Hou Siyuan was stockier than he had been at Changshan, and his legs were still long and thin, so that on horseback he looked as if he had grown out of the horse. In the camp he was famous at polo.

Cheng Chongyi had come too. That ankle of his, which he had turned once outside the walls of Jiumen, he had turned again since, and now he walked with a slight limp. But once he was on a horse it could not be seen.

Early that day they were readying their horses by the stables at the south end of the ground. Hou Siyuan saw Yisi, led his horse over and said: "Master Yi, are you playing too?"

Yisi said: "No. Lord Guo wants me along."

Hou Siyuan looked at his back and said no more. The arrow at the Xiangji Temple had gone in under the shield; the wound had healed, but now when Yisi rode for long his back tightened in spasms. Hou Siyuan knew it.

Yisi took something from his coat, wrapped in a cloth. He opened the cloth: it was a coarse earthenware wine-bowl, with a chip in its rim and a pinch of earth in it.

"Put it over on their side," said Yisi.

Hou Siyuan took the bowl, looked at it once, and asked nothing. He led his horse to the stables on the west side of the ground, Li Guangbi's side. At the edge of the ground there stood a row of folding stools for the players to rest on, eight for eight horsemen, and one more left empty, in case someone had to be replaced. Hou Siyuan put the bowl on the empty stool.

On Li Guangbi's side, three men were tightening their horses' girths. All three had played football against Yisi outside the walls of Jiumen; they had not died in that rearguard fight, nor afterwards, and today they had come. They saw the bowl.

One of them came over, squatted, and reached out and touched the chip in the rim.

"It's his," the man said.

Another said: "When he drank, he always drank from the chipped side. Said it was cooler on the mouth."

None of the three said anything more.

---

The Son of Heaven arrived at the hour of *chen*.

Yisi stood with Guo Ziyi's attendants below the viewing tower, behind the barrier outside the curtains. He could not see the upper floor; he could see only two rows of the Golden Guard at the foot of the stairs, and a few men in scarlet robes, beardless, walking back and forth, making no sound underfoot.

After a while a young eunuch came down from the tower, fifteen or sixteen, came to the barrier and asked: "Which is the *hu* monk under Director Guo's command?"

Yisi stepped out of the line.

The young eunuch said: "Come with me. The Sage says you're to come up."

At the foot of the stairs, the Golden Guard put out a hand and stopped him. Yisi unbuckled the short mace and the curved blade at his waist and handed them over. The guard felt him all over before letting him up. The stairs were wooden, newly lacquered, and creaked step by step.

Only afterwards did Yisi hear what had been said on the tower before.

That day Guo Ziyi had arrived first, and waited on the tower for the Emperor. When the Son of Heaven was seated, he asked a few questions about the fighting on the river, and asked who each side was putting up for today's polo. Guo Ziyi gave the names one by one. When he had done, he said: there is one more man under your servant's command, who is not playing today; your servant would beg Your Majesty a favour, that he be allowed to sit behind your servant and watch.

The Son of Heaven asked: "What man, that he must sit and watch?"

Guo Ziyi said he was a *hu*, a monk of the Daqin monastery, who served at his headquarters and found the way for him. In the battle at the Xiangji Temple last ninth month, when the rebels had laid several thousand horse in ambush in the third channel to the east, it was he who saw them first. Of the two who went with him, one died in the second channel; the other came back with the report, led the Uighur troops back, and died at the sluice-gate at the north end of the third channel. He himself had taken three arrows that day, and from the one in his back he still could not stand for long.

Li Guangbi sat to one side and said nothing.

When he had heard it, after a while the Son of Heaven said: "The Daqin monastery. When We were at Lingwu, We heard of it."

And he said: "Have him come up. Give him a seat."

---

Yisi came up onto the tower, knelt at the edge of the curtains, and knocked his head to the floor.

He did not dare raise his head. He heard someone above say: "Rise." The voice was not loud, and a little hoarse.

The young eunuch led him behind Guo Ziyi's seat. A small stool had already been put there, low, covered with a blue cloth. Yisi gave thanks for the favour and sat down.

Where he sat, Guo Ziyi's back was less than three feet away. Guo Ziyi's seat was on the left hand of the imperial throne; beyond it was the balustrade, and beyond the balustrade, the polo ground. On the right hand of the throne was Li Guangbi's seat.

Only now did he dare lift his eyes a little.

The first thing he saw was a pair of hands. They rested on the arms of the throne, very thin, the veins standing out one by one on their backs, the nails trimmed very even. Then an ochre-yellow robe, and beneath the robe narrow shoulders, sloping forward a little. Above that, a face.

The face was younger than Yisi had thought, and older than Yisi had thought. He could not have been much past forty, but there were two bluish patches beneath his eyes, his cheeks were sunken, his lips very pale. Every so often as he sat there, the Son of Heaven covered his mouth with his sleeve and coughed softly, twice.

Yisi looked only once, and lowered his head. He thought of what the old soldiers in the camp had said, of the Prince of Linzi who played four against ten and was like the wind and like lightning. The man sitting on the tower was the Prince of Linzi's son.

---

A little behind the throne stood a eunuch, thirty or so, with a smooth pale face and long narrow eyes, in a purple robe. Beside him a young eunuch held a vermilion-lacquered tally box with a bundle of bamboo tallies stuck in it.

The Son of Heaven said: "Chao'en, you call today's tallies."

The eunuch answered: "Your slave obeys."

Only then did Yisi learn that this man was called Yu Chao'en.

The Son of Heaven turned his face, looked first to his left, then to his right, and said: "Today the troops of you two ministers play a match before Us. We would ask one thing: whose troops are the stronger?"

The tower went still a moment.

Guo Ziyi leaned forward a little and said: "Your servant's troops are not as good as Guangbi's."

The Son of Heaven smiled, and looked at Li Guangbi.

Li Guangbi sat very straight, his eyelids half lowered, and only after a while did he speak. He said: "At holding a city, your servant's troops are the stronger. At polo, Ziyi's men and horses are good, and your servant's are not their match."

The Son of Heaven asked no more. He had the prize brought: two horses from the imperial stables, one grey, one white, with new saddles and trappings, led down below the tower and tethered at the edge of the ground. The Son of Heaven said that whichever side won, the horses would go to that side.

---

The drum sounded three rolls, and play began.

The ball was thrown up from the middle of the ground, and sixteen horses charged at it together. The hooves on the oiled ground rang out all at once, like a sudden downpour. The moon-mallets swung up, their heads flashing in the sun.

Yisi sat on the little stool and looked down past Guo Ziyi's shoulder. It was the first time he had watched a game from so high. Outside Jiumen he had been on the field, and had had eyes only for the few men before and behind and on either side of him; now, from the tower, he could see all sixteen horses at a glance, who was going where and who was waiting for whom.

He saw Hou Siyuan reach the ball first. Hou Siyuan bent low, almost flat along his horse's neck, hooked with the head of his mallet, and the ball rolled out skimming the ground, his horse running after it, running faster than the ball.

Two of Li Guangbi's riders closed in on either side.

Hou Siyuan did not look at them, nor at the goal. He flicked the ball away to the right, to Cheng Chongyi. Cheng Chongyi's horse had been waiting there all along; the ball rolled half a step ahead of the horse, he brought his mallet down once, and the ball flew into the goal.

Yu Chao'en called out: "Shuofang, one tally!" Below, the drum beat a roll.

Hou Siyuan wheeled his horse back, and as he passed below the viewing tower he looked up at the tower once, grinned, and rode off.

---

The second tally went to Li Guangbi's side.

The man who scored was one of those three old soldiers. The ball came flying at a slant from behind him; he had no time to turn his horse, leaned right back and struck backhand, and the ball grazed the goalpost and went in. He could not hold himself, turned head over heels off his horse, and rolled a long way across the oiled ground. When he got up, his face was all blood.

Yu Chao'en called: "Hedong, one tally!"

Li Guangbi did not move. Yisi saw the hand that rested on his knee; one finger moved.

The Son of Heaven said from the throne: "Good." Then he turned his face, and looked once at Guo Ziyi, and once at Li Guangbi.

Guo Ziyi said: "That stroke—no one on your servant's side could have made it."

Li Guangbi said nothing. He looked down at the old soldier with the bloodied face, looked a while, and lowered his eyelids again.

---

Polo was a dangerous game. Sixteen horses wheeling in one field, wheeling hard: horse struck horse, man struck man, mallets hit men in the face, balls hit horses in the eye—all of it happened often. In Chang'an every year there were those who died at polo, princes' sons and the army's best alike.

That day was dangerous too. When the third tally went to Guo Ziyi's side, four horses from the two sides crowded before the goal, one horse's foreleg caught another horse's hind leg, and men and horses went rolling together in a heap. On the tower the curtains were thrown into confusion; several eunuchs rushed to the balustrade to look. The Son of Heaven half rose from the throne, and sat back again.

The men got up, and the horses got up too. A soldier of Hedong had broken his arm; holding it up with his other hand, he walked off the field by himself.

The Son of Heaven had medicine sent down.

Yu Chao'en bent and said to the Son of Heaven, smiling: "Sage, this isn't polo, it's war."

The Son of Heaven did not take him up, only coughed twice.

The fourth tally, again, was Li Guangbi's side.

Two to two. One more tally would decide it.

---

When the sun had passed into the west, the ball was thrown up again.

The horses of the two sides stood in two rows in the middle of the field, the horses snorting, the men gripping their mallets, no one speaking. On the tower, too, no one spoke. The Son of Heaven took his sleeve from his mouth and laid it on the arm of the throne, and did not cough again.

Yisi watched from the tower. The ball came down first on Li Guangbi's side. Two of those three old soldiers, one on the left and one on the right, held the ball between them and moved forward. They moved very steadily, never snatching a step; when Guo Ziyi's men charged them, they drew the ball back, let the men charge past, and moved forward again. Watching them, Yisi thought of the crossbowmen on the wall at Changshan, loosing shot by shot, unhurried.

Hou Siyuan did not go after the ball. He reined in his horse and stopped at the half-field, waiting.

The ball reached the goal. The old soldier guarding it raised his mallet.

At that moment Cheng Chongyi cut across from the side. His lame leg could not grip the horse; his body hung slantwise in the saddle, his mallet stretched out to its full length, the head of it reached the ball, and with a flick the ball rolled out from under the old soldier's mallet and away to the half-field.

That one stroke was what Hou Siyuan had been waiting for.

He pressed his horse, met the ball, and struck once. The ball flew from the half-field all the way into the goal.

Yu Chao'en called: "Shuofang—one tally!"

Below, the drums broke out all together.

Three to two.

---

The eight horsemen of Guo Ziyi's side dismounted in the middle of the field and bowed down together toward the viewing tower. The eight of Li Guangbi's side dismounted too, and bowed down too. When Cheng Chongyi knelt, his lame leg would not bend, and Hou Siyuan held him up beside him.

On the tower, Guo Ziyi rose and bowed toward the throne. Li Guangbi rose too, and bowed.

The Son of Heaven sat, looking at the sixteen men kneeling below, looked a good while.

"The horses go to Shuofang," said the Son of Heaven.

Guo Ziyi gave thanks for the favour.

The Son of Heaven said again: "The one from Hedong who fell, and the one who broke his arm—reward them both." He thought, and had two more horses led out, the same as the first two, one grey, one white, with new saddles and trappings too, and tethered at the edge of the ground on Li Guangbi's side.

Li Guangbi gave thanks for the favour, and his face showed nothing.

Yu Chao'en bent and said: "Yes," and had a young eunuch go down and pass on the word.

Yisi sat on the little stool with his head lowered, looking at his two hands resting on his knees.

---

When the gathering broke up, the Son of Heaven left first. The Golden Guard cleared the way ahead, the eunuchs followed behind, and the stairs creaked step by step a long time before they were quiet.

The two military governors went down from the tower to the edge of the field to see the players.

At the foot of the stairs Yisi collected his mace and blade, and following behind Guo Ziyi's attendants he came to the edge of the field too. He saw Li Guangbi walk across from the west side of the field to the row of stools; the three old soldiers and the soldier with the broken arm all stood up. Li Guangbi looked them over one by one, came to the empty stool, and saw the bowl.

He checked his stride.

Only checked it, once. He did not bend his head to look, nor ask. Then he walked on, came to the old soldier whose face had been cut open, and said something; Yisi was too far off to hear. The old soldier grinned.

Hou Siyuan came over to take the bowl back. One of the three old soldiers stopped him, bent down himself, took up the bowl, wiped its rim with his sleeve, and handed it to Yisi in both hands.

Yisi took it, wrapped it in the cloth, and tucked it into his coat.

---

When he got back to the Yining Ward it was nearly dark. The monastery was at supper; Jingjing was sitting on the threshold of the side room with his bowl, and when he saw Yisi come in at the gate he stood up.

"Pa," said Jingjing, "did you see the Sage?"

Yisi said: "I did."

Jingjing was going to ask more. Yisi went into the side room, took the cloth from his coat, opened it, took out the bowl, and set it on the table.

Jingjing followed him in with his own bowl, stood a while, and set his bowl down, next to that one.
