# Appendix 9　Luminous Terms and Modern Christian Terms

When the Luminous clergy of the Tang translated their scriptures, they had to say in Chinese a whole set of things for which Chinese had no words. They borrowed a great many ready-made ones: Buddhist terms like "Dharma king," "great virtue," "monastery"; Daoist ones like "non-striving," "the true and constant"; Confucian ones like "filial piety" and "rites"; and sometimes they simply transliterated the Syriac. Most of these renderings were later lost. When Catholicism came to China at the end of the Ming it started afresh; in the nineteenth century the Protestant missionaries started afresh again, and the Mandarin Union Version of 1919 laid down most of the vocabulary of Chinese Christianity today.

So the same idea was often said quite differently by the people of the Tang and by the church today. The stele writes 三一妙身無元真主阿羅訶, "the Triune Wonder, the beginningless true Lord Alaha"; to a modern Chinese reader it reads like a book from heaven, though in Union Version Chinese it would simply be "the one true God in three persons."

**A note on the English edition.** The Chinese edition quotes scripture throughout in the Chinese Union Version, for readers' convenience. This English edition quotes it in the King James Version, whose language is close enough to that of the Union Version's day, and far enough from ours, to keep the same distance. The characters of the book, reading and preaching in the Tang, of course read neither.

This appendix has two parts. The first sets out the main terms in a table. The second lists, passage by passage, the scriptures quoted in the book; where a Tang Luminous text has a corresponding rendering, the Luminous original is given; where it has none, the classical Chinese translations of the late Qing (the Wenli Union Version and the Delegates' Version) are given instead, so that readers can get a rough sense of how these scriptures sounded when read in literary Chinese. The Chinese is given as it stands, with an English gloss where it helps.

## 1. The Luminous texts

The Chinese Luminous texts used in this appendix are chiefly these four.

The first is the *Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom*, raised in the second year of Jianzhong (781), composed by Jingjing and written by Lü Xiuyan. The first half of the inscription expounds doctrine and is the most concentrated, and most reliable, source of Tang Luminous terminology. On the stele itself, see Appendix 1.

The second is the *Xuting mishisuo jing* (the *Jesus-Messiah Sutra*), a Dunhuang manuscript formerly owned by Takakusu Junjirō in Japan. *Xuting mishi* in the title is generally taken as a transliteration of "Jesus Messiah." The text speaks of the Heavenly Honoured One (God) and of ten "vows" (corresponding to the Ten Commandments), and of the Messiah's birth, baptism and Passion; its language is clumsy and full of Buddhist terms, and it is generally thought to be an early translation from the first years of the Luminous Religion in China.

The third is the *Da Qin jingjiao sanwei mengdu zan* (*Hymn of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in Praise of the Three Majesties*), a Dunhuang manuscript taken to France by Pelliot, now in the Bibliothèque nationale (P.3847). It is a hymn, and scholars agree that it shares its origin with the Syriac morning hymn of the Church of the East (corresponding to the Western *Gloria in excelsis*).

The fourth is the *Zunjing* (*Book of the Honoured*), copied on the same scroll as the *Sanwei mengdu zan*. Its first half venerates the Trinity and the "Dharma kings" (saints and authors of scripture); its second half lists the titles of thirty-five scriptures.

It should be said that since the late twentieth century some scholars (such as Lin Wushu and Rong Xinjiang) have questioned the provenance and authenticity of the manuscripts of the *Xuting mishisuo jing* and of another text, the *Yishen lun* (*Discourse on the One God*, formerly owned by Tomioka Kenzō), suggesting they may be modern forgeries based on the stele and other materials. The question is still open. This appendix quotes these two texts in the ordinary way, but readers should know of the doubt. The reliability of the stele, the *Sanwei mengdu zan* and the *Zunjing* is not in dispute.

## 3. The scriptures in this book

The list below follows the chapters, giving scriptures quoted or echoed in the text. Each entry gives first the King James Version (as used in the English edition), then the corresponding text in the Luminous documents; where the Luminous documents have none, the Wenli Union Version (1919) is given, with the Delegates' Version (1854) where useful. ### Chapter 28, *Ba'aluo*

There are two passages in this chapter. One is Ba'aluo citing the Ten Commandments:

KJV (Exod. 20:4–5):

> Thou shalt not make unto thee any graven image … Thou shalt not bow down thyself to them …

Luminous parallel: the *Xuting mishisuo jing* sets out ten "vows," corresponding broadly to the second half of the Ten Commandments (do not kill, do not commit adultery, do not steal, do not bear false witness, and so on):

> 第五願者。眾生自莫殺生。亦莫諫他殺。……第六願者。莫姦他人妻子……第七願者。莫作賊。
>
> (The fifth vow: let living beings not kill, nor urge others to kill… The sixth vow: do not lie with another man's wife… The seventh vow: do not be a thief.)

The passage on idols comes before the ten vows:

> 眾生自被誑惑。乃將金造象。銀神像及銅像并□神像及木神像。……
>
> (Living beings, being deceived, make images of gold, idols of silver and of bronze and of □, and idols of wood…)

That is, people are led astray and make idols of gold, silver, bronze and wood to worship. It speaks of the folly of idolatry, but does not render it as a commandment like "thou shalt not make any graven image."

Wenli Union (Exod. 20:4–5):

> 勿雕偶像、勿造上天下地、地下水中、百物之像、勿拜跪、勿崇奉、

Delegates':

> 毋雕偶像、天上地下水中百物、勿作像象之、毋拜跪、無崇奉、

The other passage is Ba'aluo saying that Elijah killed the four hundred and fifty prophets of Baal (1 Kings 18:22, 40).

KJV (1 Kings 18:40):

> And Elijah said unto them, Take the prophets of Baal; let not one of them escape. And they took them: and Elijah brought them down to the brook Kishon, and slew them there.

Luminous parallel: none. The *Book of Yiliye* listed in the *Zunjing* may have to do with Elijah, but the text does not survive.

Wenli Union:

### Chapters 32, 55, 59, 62: "In the highest heavens, deep reverence and praise"

This is the Luminous scripture that appears most often in the book: the hymn Jingjing translated is being sung. This line does survive in the Luminous original; it is the opening of the *Sanwei mengdu zan*:

> 無上諸天深敬歎　大地重念普安和　人元真性蒙依止　三才慈父阿羅訶
>
> (In the highest heavens, deep reverence and praise; on the great earth, peace and harmony everywhere; man's first true nature finds its refuge; Alaha, merciful Father of the three realms.)

It corresponds to the opening of the Syriac morning hymn—the source of the Western *Gloria in excelsis*—taken from the angels' words at the Nativity.

KJV (Luke 2:14):

> Glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace, good will toward men.

Set side by side, "in the highest heavens, deep reverence and praise" is "Glory to God in the highest," and "on the great earth, peace and harmony everywhere" is "on earth peace." The Luminous translator made it into seven-character verse.

### Throughout the book: "Lord, have mercy"

The "Lord, have mercy" that appears many times in the text is the commonest short prayer in the liturgy and popular devotion of the Church of the East, corresponding to Greek *Kyrie eleison*; in Syriac, *Māran ethraḥam ʿlayn* (Lord, have mercy on us). The old woman's "Moran… ethrahem" in the Epilogue is this prayer in Syriac, carried onto the steppe, its sounds gone astray.

No rendering of this short prayer survives in the Chinese Luminous texts. In the KJV a close equivalent is the publican's prayer in Luke 18:13, "God be merciful to me a sinner."

## 4. Some notes

1. **The book's strategy with terms.** The narration uses today's words (Jesus, baptise, pray, patriarch, bishop); when the characters speak—especially before Tang people, and in scenes of translation—they use Luminous terms (Alaha, the Messiah, the Pure Wind, the Heavenly Honoured One, Great Virtue, head of the monastery). This is so that readers can follow, and also feel how people of that time spoke. The correspondence between the two sets of terms is the table in section 2 of this appendix. The English edition follows the same strategy: modern English in the narration, the Luminous terms in English rendering (with the original word where it matters) in the characters' mouths.
2. **Why Luminous renderings borrow so much from Buddhism and Daoism.** The religious vocabulary of Tang Chang'an was almost wholly taken up by Buddhism and Daoism; to be understood, the Luminous clergy could only borrow. Borrowed words carried their old meanings with them and inevitably caused misunderstanding, and later criticism that the Luminous Religion was "Buddhicised" or "Daoicised" mostly comes from this. Jingjing, the author of the stele, is recorded in the *Zhenyuan xinding shijiao mulu* as having even translated a Buddhist sutra with the Indian monk Prajñā.
3. **Most Chinese Luminous scriptures are lost.** The *Zunjing* lists thirty-five titles, of which only a handful survive. That is why most entries in section 3 of this appendix can only give late-Qing literary translations instead.
