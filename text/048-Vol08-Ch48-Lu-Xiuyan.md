# Volume Eight　The Daqin Monastery

## Chapter 48　Lü Xiuyan

> That every thought should meet its answer and every feeling come from a true heart.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

At the end of the sixth month Official Pei came into the Yining Ward in a sedan chair, bringing the petition returned from the Bureau of Sacrifices: granted. The petition bore only a few lines, stamped with the Bureau's seal; Samaan took it, read it twice from beginning to end, and slipped it into his account book, at the page recording the price of the stone. That evening a few of them sat again in the small room in the back courtyard, and Samaan, the account book spread on his knees, read out the accounts: Luo Fifteen's money from Luoyang had arrived, but only half; his letter said the Xiushan Ward's rents were low this year, and the other half would wait till after autumn; Old Master Jiao's nephew at Lingzhou wrote back that the monastery roof was leaking again, the rain pouring into the hall from the east end and spoiling two rolls of scripture; money he could not find; at the end of the letter he asked the Chang'an elders to pray for them. All that could be moved had been moved over; the stone, the carting, the stonemason, the base, the dragons on the head—all were provided for; what was still short was ten-odd bolts of silk for the calligrapher's fee. When Samaan had finished reading, he folded his hands back into his sleeves and said: in the fifth month the Great Virtue Yi said that if the stele was set up a year late, the stone wouldn't run away; he'd said then it was the safe way, and he said it was the safe way still.

Jingjing said: no waiting, and no more moving either; the gruel kitchen's rice not touched, the medicine chest not touched, Narsai's money for reed mats not touched. He said from tomorrow on, the monastery's people should come every day at the hour of *wu* into the hall to pray for this stele; all who could come, come; those who couldn't, pray wherever they were. Azarel asked him what they were to ask for. Jingjing said: not for money. He said the stele text was settled, the stone was settled, Li Su had settled the day; what was lacking was a man willing to write this stele; find the right writer, and when the time came the money would sort itself out; find the wrong one, and even with the money made up it would be no use. Marya said there was already one of the seven daily praises at the hour of *wu*; they'd add a passage to it. Samaan said neither good nor not good, but wrote a line in the account book: "From the first of the seventh month, prayer for the stele daily at the hour of *wu*." When he had written it he said that in all his life he had never seen a prayer that could stand in for an account; but the elders had decided, and he'd record it by the rule. The next day at the hour of *wu*, the first to kneel in the hall was he.

---

As to the writer, Official Pei had already made inquiries once. He said that of the famous hands in the city of Chang'an who could write a great stele like this, the first was Lord Xu Hao, the second Lord Yan Zhenqing; the calligraphy of both was known to the whole realm, and their going rates were not what the monastery could pay; and with Lord Xu there was one more layer: the Great Xingshan Temple had already engaged him to write the stele for the late Tripitaka Master Amoghavajra, with the text by Lord Yan Ying; it was to be set up next year, he heard.

When Yisi heard the name Amoghavajra, some old things came back to him. In the ninth month of the first year of Yongtai he had kept watch over Pugu Huaien at Mingsha, watched the man pass blood day after day, till at the last he pushed away a bowl of plum-and-rice water a foot off and would not drink, whatever was said, and died just before dawn. When he came back to Chang'an in the eleventh month, the whole city was saying that Pugu Huaien had been preached to death by the *Benevolent Kings Sutra* at the Zisheng and Ximing temples: in the ninth month the palace had brought out the newly translated sutra, with banners, canopies, incense and flowers, escorted it all the way into the two temples, and set up a hundred high seats; the new translation was from the hand of the Tripitaka Master Amoghavajra; and the Tibetans and Uighurs had withdrawn in the tenth month too; of Lord Guo taking off his armour before the Uighurs outside Jingyang, few in the wards spoke; they said only that the sutra had driven them off. Yisi heard it at the monastery and went to the West Market every day to give out gruel as usual. That year the Tripitaka Master Amoghavajra was given the rank of Specially Advanced and acting Chief Minister of the Court of State Ceremonial, with the added title Great Wise and Broad Tripitaka; Yisi too was raised to Grand Master of Splendid Happiness with Gold and Purple, the patent of appointment brought to the monastery by Lord Guo's administrative officer; he received it kneeling and put it in that box at the bottom of the scripture cupboard. Then one year, on the Emperor Daizong's birthday, the palace as usual granted heavenly incense and imperial food, brought to the monastery by a palace commissioner, and Yisi went with the commissioner into the palace to give thanks, and waited under the gallery of a hall; the Tripitaka Master Amoghavajra came out of the hall in purple, a man past sixty with his back still straight, two disciples behind him. He had grown up in his youth in the region of Liangzhou and Samarkand, and spoke Sogdian; passing Yisi, he stopped a moment and asked him in Sogdian: "That man at Mingsha—how did he die?" Yisi said: "The flux." The Tripitaka Master Amoghavajra said: "I know." And having said it, went on. Yisi thought about it afterwards for several years and never worked out what it was he knew.

These things Yisi had never told anyone, and he did not tell them that day in the small room either; he said only that as for Lord Xu, the monastery would not go. Samaan said he thought the same: in the first year of Shangyuan, when the censor memorialized against the monastery, the paper attached at the end of the memorial was written by monks of the Ximing Temple; the disciples of the Tripitaka Master Amoghavajra, the Ximing Temple and the Great Xingshan Temple were all one household. Yisi said Lord Xu might not necessarily refuse; but he was writing a stele for his own master just now, and if we went with a stele for Alaha and begged him, it would put him in a difficult place; to agree would look bad, and not to agree would look bad; why do it? Official Pei nodded and said: then that leaves only Lord Yan.

---

Lord Yan's calligraphy Yisi had seen. The year Lord Guo's father's family shrine was set up, the stele was composed and written by Lord Yan; Yisi had gone with Lord Guo once into the family shrine; Lord Guo stood before the stele and looked at it character by character, and when he had looked said to those beside him: Yan Qingchen's calligraphy is like the man; it stands firm. Yisi could not read all the characters, but he could see how they stood on the stone, pressed down stroke by stroke, pressed till the stone itself seemed heavier. Official Pei said Lord Yan was writing a stele for his own family shrine this year, to be carved full on all four faces, with the names of more than ten generations of the Yan family on it. Yisi said: a stele for Alaha—if such a hand could be asked to write it, that would be good.

Lord Yan had been Minister of Personnel, and this year had been changed to Junior Preceptor of the Heir Apparent; the monastery had no way to him. Yisi thought of asking Lord Guo. When Lord Guo did not call him he had never gone; this was the first time he went uncalled. The gate of the Guo mansion in the Qinren Ward stood open as always, people going in and out without end; but these two years Lord Guo had hardly seen his old followers; Yisi found Hou Siyuan to pass word in, waited a good half hour by the mansion gate, and Hou Siyuan came out holding a card, signed by the mansion's secretary, saying: the monk Yisi of the Daqin monastery has business and begs an audience with the Junior Preceptor Yan; Lord Guo's seal was not on it, and there was not one word more. Hou Siyuan handed him the card and said Lord Guo had told him to pass on one sentence: "Whether he sees you is Lord Yan's affair." Yisi tucked the card inside his coat.

---

Lord Yan saw them. In mid-seventh month Yisi took Jingjing, the draft of the stele text inside Jingjing's coat. Lord Yan was seventy-two, tall, long-faced, his beard all white; seated, he still held his back straight; he did not speak fast, and his voice was hoarse. He first asked Yisi about Changshan, asked whether, when Li Guangbi took Changshan, anyone in the city still remembered Yan of Changshan. Yisi said that he was in the city twenty-odd days then, and had only heard an old soldier of the garrison speak of it, saying Governor Yan cursed the rebels till he had no tongue left; beyond that he did not know. Lord Yan heard it and said only one word, "Good," and asked about that ditch east of the Xiangji Temple, asked about Lingwu, said that when he was in Hebei he had heard someone say there was a *hu* monk in the Shuofang army who saw the road for Lord Guo, and had never thought it would be the man before his eyes. Only when he had asked these things did he have Jingjing bring out the draft.

He read the draft more than an hour, reading a passage and asking about a passage, asking more finely even than Official Pei. He asked what "the wondrous Body of Three in One" was; Jingjing said it was one and also three, three Persons all one Lord. He asked who "Meshiha" was; Jingjing said the Lord's Son, born in Daqin, who later ascended to heaven. He asked about "at high noon he ascended to the Truth," asked about "he hung up the luminous sun to break open the dark realm"—where the dark realm was; Jingjing answered each, and when it came to how Meshiha died, he answered slowly, and Lord Yan did not press. When he came to Taizong, Gaozong, Xuanzong, Suzong, Daizong, reign by reign down to the present emperor, Lord Yan stopped and asked Jingjing: this stele joins the rise and fall of your religion with one Son of Heaven after another—when the Son of Heaven is good, you are well—is that the meaning? Jingjing said that was the meaning, and said too that it was the fact: without Taizong's edict there would be no monastery in the Yining Ward. Lord Yan nodded and read on, and when he came to Yisi's passage pressed his finger on the characters and moved it down line by line.

When he had read it, he shut the draft, pushed it back before Jingjing, and said something to Yisi. He said these lines, "The hungry came and were fed; the cold came and were clothed; the sick were healed and raised up; the dead were buried and laid to rest," he believed; in Hebei he had seen such men—monks, old village men, and *hu* too: men who in the upheaval were willing to share out the rice in their own bowls; he remembered a few. He said that of the Daoists' affairs and the Buddhists' affairs he had read books, asked people, written steles, and knew his own mind; of your house he had not so much as read a book; who Alaha was, who Meshiha was, what Three in One was, where the dark realm was, he did not know whether it was true or false; if his name went at the foot of the stele, those who looked at the stele would take it that Yan Zhenqing stood guarantor for these words, and he did not stand guarantor for what he did not know. He said he respected the Great Virtue, and respected this monastery too; the lines on the stele he could vouch for, he was willing to speak for the monastery to people. When he had said this he had paper and brush brought, wrote a name, folded it, and handed it to Yisi, saying there was in the city a Master Lü, named Lü Xiuyan, formerly Administrator of Granaries of Taizhou, now out of office, living in the Chongxian Ward; his calligraphy was clean and vigorous, not airy. "Master Lü's brush is equal to this stele. Whether he is willing or not, you gentlemen must ask him yourselves."

Yisi asked Lord Yan how he knew this man. Lord Yan said that when he was at Huzhou he gathered a company of men to compile a book, the *Mirror Source of the Sea of Rhymes*; Master Lü of Taizhou, at that time finished with his term and waiting for selection, idle at home, came to Huzhou to copy for them, and copied the better part of a year. Once a rich householder of Huzhou died, and his family came with five bolts of silk to ask Master Lü to write the epitaph; the text of the epitaph was already written by someone; Master Lü read it and said: the man written of in this epitaph he knew, and he was not such a man; and he sent the text back, and sent back the five bolts of silk too. Lord Yan said he had remembered this man ever since.

Coming out of the Dunhua Ward, Jingjing just walked on the whole way, and near the Yining Ward asked his Pa whether this trip had been in vain. Yisi said it had not. He said Lord Yan would not say what he did not know; that, your Pa understands.

---

Lü Xiuyan's home was in the southwest corner of the Chongxian Ward, very small; through the gate there was at once a great table, paper heaped on it, a dozen-odd brushes hanging on the wall, ranged from thick to fine, and a smell of ink through the room. He was fifty or so, thin, with not much flesh on his face, his fingers covered in ink; when he looked at a man he looked first at his hands. Lord Yan's name he knew; Yisi gave him that folded paper; he read it, smiled a little, and said Lord Yan, recommending a man, never agrees on his behalf. He read the stele text very fast, much faster than Lord Yan, and when he had read it, asked what Alaha was. Jingjing told him, told it at length, from before all things to Meshiha, and on to the monastery's offering once in seven days. Lü Xiuyan listened, now nodding, now shaking his head, and when he had listened asked whether they believed this. Jingjing said they did. Lü Xiuyan said: "You believe it; I don't." He squared the draft on the table and asked again: if the characters he wrote on this stele were written in a wrong sense, whose would it be? Jingjing said it would be his; the text was his composition. Lü Xiuyan said: once characters are written on a stele, they're the stele's business; you compose yours and I write mine; my name is at the foot of the stele; if they're wrong, I bear half too. When he had said this, he did not say he would write, nor that he would not; he said only: in a few days, would the two of them come once more. Of money he said not a word, and nor did Yisi.

Those few days the monastery prayed at the hour of *wu* as usual; Samaan came first every day and knelt near the door of the hall, muttering very fast, and when he had done got up and went back to his accounts. On the fifth day Yisi and Jingjing went again to the Chongxian Ward, and Lü Xiuyan said he would write.

Yisi thanked him, and went on to speak of the fee. He and Jingjing had discussed it on the road; the monastery's accounts were what they were, and they did not mean to hide them. By the going rate Official Pei had found out, writing such a great stele was thirty bolts of silk; the monastery could find only twenty just now; Yisi said so plainly, and asked whether Master Lü would allow some time for the rest. Lü Xiuyan heard it, smiled a little, and shook his head. Yisi and Jingjing exchanged a look, both taking it that it was too little, and Yisi said: then at the going rate, thirty bolts; the ten short he would make up himself when next year's stipend came down; he could write a bond first. Jingjing beside him did not stop him; this time he did not say Pa should not pay. Lü Xiuyan still shook his head and said: "Not one bolt."

Yisi asked why. Lü Xiuyan got up and shut the door, sat down again, and said that a few days ago he had gone once before light to the West Market: at the mouth of the West Market, that row of gruel pots, the one ladling was a man of thirty or so, ladling bowl after bowl very evenly; when he came to a woman holding a child, the ladle sank once more to the bottom of the pot; behind the pots a *hu* of fifty-odd was unloading rice from a cart, his back not able to bend, so he shouldered it. The next day he went out of the Kaiyuan Gate and north two or three *li*; on the waste ground by the road, row after row of wooden stakes, carved *old man*, *old woman*, *child*, *frozen*, *below the wall of such-and-such a ward*, carved very clumsily; an old *hu* pushing an empty cart came out of the burial ground, saw him, and nodded. He said only when he had seen these two places did he dare tell the Great Virtue something. From a box under the table he took out a piece of cloth and laid it on the table: the size of a palm, so old it had gone brown, and sewn on it in red thread a character, *Lü*, the thread faded, the stitches still to be seen. He asked Yisi whether he remembered the dead in that field north of the Xiangji Temple in the ninth month of the second year of Zhide.

The Lü family had lived in the Changle Ward, four brothers: the eldest died early; the second was a carpenter; he was third; and below him there was the Fourth. The year the rebel army entered Chang'an, the able-bodied men of the ward were rounded up household by household; the second brother was rounded up into the rebel army, and on the day he left their mother stayed up all night sewing him a blue cloth lined jacket, and inside the corner of its front sewed a character *Lü* in red thread, saying: in case—so someone will know him. He himself had passed the Classics examination the year before and was waiting for selection; his schooling had been paid for by his uncle; of that generation of the Lü family, he alone had got through his books; his uncle ran a silk shop in the West Market and had paid for him ten-odd years, hoping only that he would become an official and the family get a little of the light. When Chang'an fell, his uncle took him to hide at a farm outside the city; when Chang'an was recovered, the court was deliberating on what to do with those who had been trapped among the rebels, and everyone in the city was afraid of being touched by the rebel army. Word came into the city of the gathering of the dead south of the city; his mother wanted him to go; his uncle would not allow it; his uncle called in several of the clan's elders, sat in the hall, and said: if it's known a man of the family went over to the rebels, and those words get into the selection register, the Third's life is finished, and the ten-odd years the Lü family paid for him are paid for nothing; his uncle said the Second is dead, that's the Second's fate; the living still have to live. He knelt below the hall, and did not answer back by one word. His uncle allowed the Fourth to go; the Fourth was sixteen that year, had no rank, couldn't read, no one knew him; his uncle charged him only to look, and not to say his surname. Their mother told the Fourth about the blue cloth lined jacket and told him to turn back the corners of the clothes. The Fourth went for half a month, went every day, turned over dead men's faces one by one, turned back the corners of clothes, and did not find him.

Yisi remembered. On the fifth day, among the forty-odd who came, there was a young man of the Changle Ward who said his elder brother was in the rebel army, taken by force, and he didn't know where he'd died; he'd come to look. That young man came every day, looked for half a month, and then came no more. Yisi's back had not then healed; he could not bend; he had not talked with him much.

Lü Xiuyan said the Fourth couldn't find him because that lined jacket had long since not been on his second brother's body. He said that at the end of the Dali years, when he left office and came back to Chang'an, his uncle was dead, and his mother was dead too; on her deathbed his mother still spoke of that lined jacket. He went alone south of the city and found the old man south of the city who had buried people in that field that year; the old man was long gone, but the old man's son was still there; that land his family had farmed ever since, and those few rows on the ditch he had never ploughed; when the markers rotted he put in new ones by the old numbers. The old man's son took him into the house; under the roof beam hung several clay jars, and in the jars were the things taken from the dead that year: sashes, wooden combs, coins, scraps of cloth, each tied with hemp cord to a wooden tag, and on the tags the numbers carved. In one jar he turned up this piece of cloth; his mother's stitches he knew. The number was row three, seven; he went to the ditch, counted along the third row, the seventh marker. The old man's son told him that the one who hauled the people that year was a *hu* monk of the Daqin monastery of the Yining Ward; the clothes stripped off were washed and given to the poor at the monastery gate to wear; where there were characters on a corner of the clothing, they were cut off first and kept, he said, in case one day someone should come to claim them.

When Lü Xiuyan got this far he paused. He said that when he learned it was the Daqin monastery's people, he did not go to give thanks. That year he had not dared to claim his second brother; his second brother had been buried by someone else; what face had he to go first and claim the man who buried his second brother? He wanted to move his second brother back to the ancestral graves on the White Deer Plain, and wanted the man who buried his second brother to be there; but that man he had not yet seen clearly. He had heard it said that among the *hu* monks of the city there were those who made their fortunes treating the great, and those who hung on official titles and went about in the train of the great; if he saw wrongly, this thing would rot in his belly, and he'd hire men himself to move his second brother. He said now he had seen; this money, he would not take.

Yisi sat, his hands on his knees, looking at that piece of cloth on the table. He said those markers he had had Old Duan put in; the corners of the clothes Old Duan had cut; he knew few Tang characters; the character on this cloth he had not recognized at the time, not one; he had only had them kept. He said that character *Lü* was not recognized by him; it was sewn on by Master Lü's mother, and found by Master Lü himself. Lü Xiuyan folded the cloth and put it back in the box, and said he meant to move the grave at the beginning of the eighth month; his uncle was gone; in the clan now his word was law; but he was a scholar; digging up a grave and raising the bones he did not know how to do; to hire men—he did not want strangers touching his second brother. Yisi said the monastery had men who knew how. That evening when they got back, Samaan heard Yisi out, spread open the account book, turned to the line for the calligrapher's fee, and below it wrote four characters: "Master Lü declines."

---

On the third of the eighth month, seven went from the monastery. Narsai pushed that small cart of his, on it two spades, a new reed mat, and a clay urn; he had gathered the dead four years, and those rows of stakes outside the Kaiyuan Gate he had carved one by one. Sargis's son brought two guard brothers carrying spades; Ishaq carried a bucket of water and a packet of clean white cloth; Yisi and Jingjing walked in front. Lü Xiuyan was waiting outside the Mingde Gate; beside him stood a man of forty or so, short, dark, his hands covered in calluses: the Fourth, who now carried bales of silk for people in the West Market. The Fourth saw Yisi, looked once, looked again, and said: "You're that *hu* monk." Yisi said he was. The Fourth said he remembered: that year that *hu* monk had a cloth bound round his waist and blood seeping through his back, dragged people with a reed mat, dragged a stretch and rested, and asked him what he'd come for, and he said he'd come to look.

The marker at row three, seven had been newly put in by the old man's son; the wood was still white. Narsai squatted down, first brushed aside a layer of loose earth beside the marker with his hand, looked at the colour of the soil, pointed to a spot, and had the guard brothers dig there, shallow, peeling it away spade by spade. When they had dug nearly chest-deep, Narsai had them come up, and went down himself, knelt at the bottom of the ditch, and worked it bit by bit with his hands. The bones showed first, lying there face up, both hands at the sides, as the old man south of the city had laid them that year; a foot of earth away on either side lay others. Narsai lifted them out piece by piece and passed them to Ishaq; Ishaq washed them in clean water, dried them with white cloth, and laid them in the clay urn in order from head to foot. Lü Xiuyan stood at the edge of the ditch, the Fourth behind him. Halfway through the raising Lü Xiuyan told Narsai he would come down too; Narsai glanced at Yisi; Yisi nodded; Narsai reached up and helped him down. Lü Xiuyan knelt at the bottom of the ditch, picked up one of the bones of his second brother's hand, held it in his palm, held it until Ishaq reached out to take it, and only then passed it up.

When all the bones were raised, Yisi asked Lü Xiuyan whether he might say a few words of their scripture. Lü Xiuyan said: "Say yours." So Yisi stood at the edge of the ditch and said it in Syriac, very slowly, and Jingjing beside him followed it line by line in Tang; what it said was: Lord, we beg thee remember this man; he died in the upheaval; when he died there was no one beside him; he has lain here twenty-three years and no one came to claim him; today his brothers have come; we beg thee remember him, and remember his brothers. The Fourth behind wept aloud; Lü Xiuyan did not weep; he tied the mouth of the urn shut with white cloth, carried it himself, and lifted it onto Narsai's small cart. The guard brothers filled the earth back in and levelled it, and Narsai put that new marker back in its place.

That day they pushed the cart east, round the Qujiang, across the Chan river, to the foot of the White Deer Plain, and it was dark; they lodged in the thatched hut of the family who kept the Lü ancestral graves. The keeper was an old man; he boiled a pot of millet gruel, and a few *hu* and two Tang sat round one pot drinking; Ishaq drank two bowls and said the gruel was well made. The urn was set in a corner of the hut, and Lü Xiuyan sat beside it all night. Early on the fourth they went up onto the plain; the Lü ancestral graves were under a stand of cypresses, his father's grave and his mother's side by side, and beside his mother's grave a pit newly dug, which the Fourth had come and dug a few days before. The urn was lowered, the earth filled in; the Fourth set out a bowl of wine and a plate of steamed buns before the grave; Lü Xiuyan knelt and knocked his head on the ground three times; Yisi and the others waited under the cypresses. When he had done, Lü Xiuyan rose, stood a moment before his mother's grave, and said to Yisi: when his mother sewed that character, she probably never thought it would be a *hu* monk who kept it for her.

On the road back into the city, across the Chan river, Yisi said once more: the monastery has the offering once in seven days; if Master Lü has leisure, please come and sit with us at the monastery. Lü Xiuyan shook his head and said: "Worship I won't come to. Your kind thought, I accept." Jingjing was going to ask again, and he said further: "The stele stands in the monastery with my name on it; count that as my having been."

---

They got into the city before the closing drum finished. On the fifth at the hour of *wu* the hall was full of kneelers as usual; when the prayer was over Samaan did not leave, but waited for Yisi to rise and asked him whether this counted as what the elders called a prayer granted; he'd thought it this way and that and couldn't make it out: the praying began on the first of last month, and that account was run up twenty-three years ago, when no one in the monastery had prayed for this stele. Yisi said those cartloads of people—he had asked Lord Guo for two carts to haul them; while he hauled, his back hurt, and he thought only of getting it done sooner; he never thought of today. Jingjing, sitting to one side, said: the Lord went ahead of us by twenty-odd years—had Pa go first and haul that cartload of people, and had Master Lü's mother sew a character into the corner of a jacket. Azarel kept his eyes closed; Marya said: this is a prayer granted. Samaan went back to the small room, turned the account book to the line "Master Lü declines," and added below it a line: "Prayer at the hour of *wu* continues as before; what was asked is changed to what is thanked."

---

Note: The end of the stele is signed "Written by Lü Xiuyan, Gentleman for Court Discussion, formerly Administrator of Granaries of Taizhou"; nothing else of Lü Xiuyan's life is known, and his family, relatives, copying at Huzhou, returning of an epitaph, and moving of a grave in this chapter are all the novel's invention. Xu Hao wrote the *Stele of the Monk Amoghavajra*, with text by Yan Ying, set up in 781 (second year of Jianzhong) at the Great Xingshan Temple. Amoghavajra in 765 (first year of Yongtai) was given the rank of Specially Advanced and acting Chief Minister of the Court of State Ceremonial, with the added title Great Wise and Broad Tripitaka; in the ninth month of that year a hundred high seats were set up at the Zisheng and Ximing temples for lectures on the newly translated *Benevolent Kings Sutra*, at the time Pugu Huaien led the Tibetans and Uighurs in invasion; the exchange between Amoghavajra and Yisi under the gallery is invented. The stele: "The Emperor Daizong the Cultured and Martial... on every birthday granted heavenly incense to announce success, and bestowed imperial food to honour the Luminous congregation." Yan Zhenqing composed and wrote the *Stele of the Guo Family Shrine* in 764 (second year of Guangde), presided over the compiling of the *Mirror Source of the Sea of Rhymes* at Huzhou in the Dali years, was Minister of Personnel at the end of Dali, was changed in 780 (first year of Jianzhong) to Junior Preceptor of the Heir Apparent, and in the same year composed and wrote the *Stele of the Yan Family Shrine*, set up in the Dunhua Ward; Yan Gaoqing held Changshan in the fifteenth year of Tianbao and, when the city fell, died cursing the rebels. The card from the Guo mansion, Yan Zhenqing's reading of the draft and courteous refusal, and his recommendation of Lü Xiuyan are the novel's arrangement. After the recovery of the two capitals in the second year of Zhide, the court graded the guilt of officials trapped among the rebels (month and day to be checked). The sums of the fees are invented.
