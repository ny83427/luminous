# Volume Six　The Shuofang Army

## Chapter 28　Baaluo

> Some pointed to things and made them the object of worship; some made emptiness and being the two into which all things sink; some prayed and sacrificed to win blessings; some boasted of their goodness to put themselves above others.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

After the defeat at Xiangzhou, Guo Ziyi did not go back to the army.

In the seventh month of the second year of Qianyuan the court summoned him back to the capital and gave the Shuofang troops to Li Guangbi. The men in camp all said in private that it was Yu Chao'en who had spoken before the Son of Heaven, saying the blame for the defeat at Xiangzhou lay with Guo Ziyi. Guo Ziyi went back to Chang'an and moved into his mansion in the Qinren Ward. Every morning, as before, he ate millet gruel, pickles and a steamed bun, and when he had eaten walked slowly in the courtyard, looking about him wherever he went, no different from when he was in camp; only the carts and horses at the gate of his mansion grew fewer day by day. In the ninth month of the first year of Shangyuan the court issued another edict making him Commander-in-Chief of the Armies of All Circuits, to lead troops out of Shuofang and take Fanyang; the edict had not been down many days before someone had it stopped, and the matter came to nothing in the end. These were all court affairs; Yisi was far from them and knew little, only that Lord Guo was idle now.

He himself had no idleness. Work on the main hall in the Yining Ward had begun in the spring of the second year of Qianyuan. The fifteen bolts of silk in Sabrisho's hands had long been spent; afterwards, once the four monasteries had kept the Fast together, money and goods came in little by little from Luoyang and Lingzhou, and the few households of old believers left at the old Persian monastery in the Liquan Ward put some together too, and only so did the pillars go up one by one. By the autumn of the first year of Shangyuan the pillars were all up, and in the ninth month the roof beam was to be raised. Since that one Fast together, the Liquan Ward households often came to the Yining Ward to worship; the two wards were two streets apart, less than half an hour's walk.

It was also that autumn that a band of young people in white appeared at the monastery in the Yining Ward.

---

The one who led them was called Baaluo. He was the son of a leather-worker in the West Market, had scraped hides with his father since he was small, and his hands smelled of saltpetre and would not wash clean. He could not read, and had never had a day's schooling; he knew the shopfronts of those few streets of the West Market, knew which shop bought raw hides and which bought cured. In the spring famine of the first year of Qianyuan his father starved to death at home; he could not carry him, and kept watch in the house nine days, and in the end it was people from the ward who helped carry him out and bury him. On the ninth of the third month he sat on the grass west of the Qujiang Pool and received a *dou* of rice. When he had it, he shouldered the sack and started home, and halfway there turned back and asked the old soldier watching his block whose idea this rice was, making people sit to get it. The old soldier pointed toward the rice carts and said it was that *hu* monk with the scar on his face.

A few days later he came to the Yining Ward. He did not say he wanted anything; he rolled up his sleeves and helped put wood on the fire under the pot, helped ladle gruel for those in line. He was strong and quick, and did the work of two. At first Granny He was afraid he had come to eat for nothing; later, seeing he came every day and worked when he came and when he had done drank only one bowl himself, she said nothing more. On the day the foundation was laid, when the courtyard full of old people was weeping as one, he looked at the new stone and was the first to grin; in those days he could not yet say a single line of scripture.

In the summer of the second year of Qianyuan, under the jujube tree, Gabriel once came in his teaching to the Dharma King Baolu. This man had at first made it his business to seize those who believed in the Lord, and seized a good many; then, walking on the road, he was struck to the ground by a light, and was blind three days, and when he opened his eyes again he became the most zealous preacher in the world, and walked many, many roads, all the way to the edge of the sky. The leather-worker's son heard it and sat under the jujube tree until dark. The next day he went to Gabriel and said he wanted to be baptized, and to be called by that name. Gabriel said the name was too big; you can't carry it. He said he would grow slowly, grow till he could carry it. In the monastery's Tang scriptures this Dharma King was written *Baolu*, but when Gabriel baptized him he said it in Syriac, and the leather-worker's son, saying it after him, could not get his tongue round it, and said it as *Baaluo*. Others laughed at him, and he would not change it, and from then on he was called Baaluo.

---

Baaluo was the most zealous in the monastery. It was not any one person who said so; everyone in the monastery said it.

Babai taught him characters. He learned slowly; when he had learned one, he wrote it a hundred times on the ground with a stick, till the ground was covered with his characters. But his memory was very good. A story Gabriel had told, he could tell from beginning to end after hearing it once, without a line wrong; only when he told it, the people in the story were all a little fiercer and a little more urgent than when Gabriel told it. He had two robes, and in winter gave one to a blind man in the ward. He ate only half of each meal, and wrapped up the other half for the begging children at the monastery gate. When anyone in the monastery fell sick, he sat up with them, and sitting up did not sleep, but knelt all night by the sick man's bed, and the next day, his knees black and blue, went off to scrape his hides just the same. When work began on the main hall, he came before light to carry timber and mix mud, and Sargis said that of the twenty-four guard brothers there were few stronger than he, and not one willing to work like that.

The elders all thought highly of him at first. Ishaq said the boy's heart was warm; once behind his back he praised him, saying that if Chang'an had a few more Baaluos, the monastery would never want for someone to do the cooking. Narsai seldom praised anyone, but he looked on Baaluo with particular favour, called him to his own room at night and taught him chapter by chapter—the Law, the prophets; how Elijah contended with the prophets of Baal on Mount Carmel; how Phinehas thrust through those two who had broken the commandment with one spear. What Narsai taught was all the very words of scripture, not one word added, not one word taken away. When Baaluo had heard it, he would often sit alone on the foundations of the hall until midnight.

Only Sabrisho, once, watching Baaluo's back as he knelt before a sick man's bed, said: too hot. Ishaq asked him what was wrong with hot. Sabrisho did not go on.

---

Those who followed Baaluo grew in number slowly; it took about a year, all told.

In the winter of the second year of Qianyuan there were three or four, all of them men who had received rice at the Qujiang together and helped at the pots at the monastery gate together; after spring opened, seven or eight; by the summer of the first year of Shangyuan, eleven. The eldest was twenty-four or -five, the youngest only seventeen. There were Tang among them, and Sogdians, and one the son a Uighur horse-dealer had left in Chang'an, whose Tang was better than his Uighur. The youngest was the son of a butcher in the East Market, who the winter before last had learned characters from Jingjing in the felt tent in the back courtyard; that character *keep* that Sargis could not write well, he wrote straight and true.

They sewed themselves white clothes, of hemp, undyed, washed till they were stiff, so that they rustled when they walked. Every day before light they went to the well behind the monastery, drew a bucket of well water and poured it over their heads; one done, they drew another and poured it over the next; and when all were done, they knelt dripping on the well platform and chanted scripture until dawn. Sabrisho told them that the rule of the Patriarchate had no such thing; baptism was once, there was no need to wash every day. Baaluo heard him out with his head bowed and said they were not keeping any rule; only their hearts were not clean, and washing every day was the only way their hearts could be at peace. The people of the ward who rose early to fetch water got a great fright the first few times they came on them; later they grew used to it, and walked past them with their buckets saying: "At their washing again." That year Jingjing was fourteen. One morning he got up and watched once, stood by the well watching them pour bucket after bucket over their heads, and before he had finished watching, his own stomach had begun to ache.

They called themselves the Pure Ones. Later, they would not eat at the same table as those who were not baptized. Later still, they said the monastery's elders were not pure enough either: the elders bowed when they met officials, and bowed to Buddhist monks too; they took benefactors' money and recited scriptures for the benefactors' safety; the old believers of the ward had the Stove God set up at home and door-gods pasted up, and when the elders went to eat there and saw them, they pretended not to. They said: how was that any different from the people of the ward who worshipped the Stove God and the fox spirits?

That spring and summer the elders went one after another to talk to them. Gabriel did most of the talking: urged them to eat sitting with everyone else, urged them not to quarrel with the Buddhist monks at the monastery gate. When Ishaq heard they said the elders were not pure enough, he did not take offence, but took two dry flatbreads in his coat and went to find them and pushed one on each of two of them. Baaluo took the bread, broke it in two, gave half to the man beside him and set the other half on the ground, saying: Elder, today we are fasting. Ishaq looked at the half loaf on the ground, stood a while, stooped and picked it up, blew the dust off it, and ate it himself. Months of talking, and Baaluo was respectful to the elders all along; whatever the elders said he heard with his head bowed, and when he had heard it, went to the well before light and poured the water just the same.

---

At the beginning of the ninth month, something happened.

There was an old believer in the ward, a Tang old woman, past seventy, whose husband had been dead twenty years. On her stove there had always stood a clay Stove God, the size of a palm, smoked pitch-black. Her husband had shaped it when he was alive, and after she was baptized she had never taken it down—not because she did not believe, but because she could not bear to.

The Pure Ones went to her house. They did not abuse her. Baaluo knelt before her, took her hands, and told her how Moushi came down from the mountain, saw the people worshipping a golden calf, ground the golden calf to powder, strewed it on the water, and made them drink it. He told it very well, told it till his own tears were falling. When he had finished, he stood up, went to the stove, took up the Stove God, and threw it on the ground. A thing of clay; it smashed at one throw. The old woman did not cry aloud. She squatted down and picked the pieces up one by one, and held them in her palm. The Pure Ones left, and she was still squatting there picking them up.

That day Jingjing went to take her gruel and saw it as he came in at the door. He set the bowl of gruel on the stove, squatted down to help her pick them up, and when they had picked them all up, went back to the monastery and told his Pa.

---

That evening Yisi called Baaluo into the side room.

Baaluo stood at the door, his white clothes wet, water still dripping from his hair. Yisi told him to sit; he would not sit, but spoke first, saying Master Yi wanted to speak about the Stove God. Yisi said yes. Baaluo said the scripture was perfectly plain: thou shalt not make unto thee any graven image, thou shalt not bow down to them; the Stove God was an idol, and he did not know where he had done wrong.

Yisi thought a while before he said: "That's what the scripture says. But that Stove God was made by her husband when he was alive. You can talk to her, talk to her a hundred times, talk till she's willing to take it down herself. You can't smash it for her."

Baaluo bowed his head, and after a while raised it again and said: "Master Yi, Elijah on Mount Carmel killed four hundred and fifty of the prophets of Baal. He didn't talk to them a hundred times."

Yisi asked him who had told him that. Baaluo said Elder Narsai. Yisi asked no more.

Baaluo stood there as if there were something he had kept in his heart a long time, and now at last he brought it out. He did not raise his voice, nor did he speak sharply; he said it word by word: "Master Yi has killed men. I heard from men back from the camps that Master Yi killed men at Changshan, and killed men at the Xiangji Temple. When Master Yi did that—was it for the Lord he killed them?"

All at once the side room was still. Outside the window someone was drawing water at the well, the windlass creaking.

It was a long time before Yisi answered him. He said: "When I killed men, I wasn't thinking of the Lord. What I was thinking of was whether the men beside me would live. In that, don't learn from me."

Baaluo opened his mouth and did not get any words out. Yisi said again: "Pouring cold water over yourselves, I let be; fasting, not eating at one table with others, I let be too. Going into people's houses and smashing their things, that I won't let be. Tomorrow you go to the old woman's house and pay her for the Stove God." Baaluo said the thing wasn't worth any money. Yisi said: worth nothing, you pay all the same. Baaluo stood a while, bowed to him, turned and went, and walking across the courtyard held his back bolt upright. The next day he really did go and pay, and the old woman would not take it.

---

That night the elders discussed Baaluo in the side room. It was the first time anyone had spoken of the matter openly before them all.

Sabrisho was the bluntest. He said the boy would not listen to counsel, and had led a whole band of people into this state; if it went on like this, sooner or later something terrible would come of it. By the rule of the Patriarchate, he should be barred from the Offering of the Bread and not allowed through the monastery gate; when he knew his fault, they would see.

Ishaq was the first to refuse. He said: how many sick men Baaluo had sat up with, how many beams he had carried; most of the wood under that pot at the monastery gate was his; push a man like that out of the gate, and the ten following him would all go out with him, and once out of the gate there'd be no one at all to hold them. Gabriel too said it would not do: the boy was baptized by him, and even his name came from a story he told; it was he who had not taught him well, and if there was to be punishment, punish him first.

Narsai said nothing at all until everyone had spoken, and only then said that Baaluo smashing the Stove God was smashing in the wrong place, but what he was keeping in his heart was the Law; if one who kept the Law were driven out of the gate by the monastery, who would dare keep it after this? Sabrisho looked at him once and asked whether, when he told Baaluo about Elijah, he had ever said to him that it was a thing done by a prophet at the Lord's command. Narsai did not answer.

Haddad said that by the rule of the Patriarchate, to cut a man off from the church had to be decided by a bishop, and Chang'an had no bishop now. At this they all glanced at Yonan, sitting where the lamplight did not reach. Yonan had once been bishop in Chang'an; everyone in the monastery knew it. But Yonan only kept his head bowed, his two hands folded, and said not a word. Babai, head down, was writing and drawing something on a wooden board.

They discussed it till midnight; cutting him off could not be agreed, and not cutting him off did not persuade Sabrisho. Yisi said: then let it rest at this—Gabriel would keep a closer eye on him, and they would have them go to work on the hall with the guard brothers in the daytime, and not leave them idle. Sabrisho did not argue further, only said he had said his piece. And they broke up.

---

On the twentieth of the ninth month, the fire-temple in the Liquan Ward held its festival for the god.

The fire-worshippers of Chang'an were mostly Sogdian merchants. Their shrines were in the Liquan Ward, the Buzheng Ward, and a few other places; ordinarily the doors were shut, and inside was kept a basin of fire that never went out day or night, with a man to tend it, called the keeper of the fire. On the festival day the shrine doors were thrown wide; the merchants slaughtered sheep and oxen, played the *pipa*, blew flutes, beat drums, drank and danced, and made merry until midnight. And the shrine by custom hired a *hu* to be the shrine-master, who before them all took a knife and drove it into his own belly so that it came out through his back; and when it had gone through, he drew the knife out, spat a mouthful of water, and there was not a mark on his belly. The people of the city all loved to go and watch, and when they had watched, said: the *hu* conjurers really are good.

The few households of old Luminous believers in the Liquan Ward had lived in the same ward as the fire-temple for decades. They did not worship fire, but every year at the festival they would stand at their doors to watch the fun, and when they met the old keeper of the fire they nodded to each other, and at the New Year they had even exchanged cakes.

That afternoon Yisi was at the building site of the main hall watching the beam go up, his hands covered in mud. When the sun had passed into the west, Xu Jing came running in, running till he streamed with sweat, waving that right hand short two fingers in the air, and waved a long while before he got his breath, and said the Pure Ones had gone to the Liquan Ward, and someone was dead.

---

When Yisi got there, several hundred people were crowded round the gate of the fire-temple: *hu* and Tang, the ward warden, the patrol, and children riding on grown-ups' shoulders to see. Every kind of thing was being shouted in the crowd; Yisi heard Sogdian and heard Tang; someone cursed "murdering *hu* religion," and someone said "let's see what their Daqin monastery says this time." The few households of old Luminous believers from the Liquan Ward were squeezed at the edge of the crowd; one old man recognized Yisi and wanted to come over, and did not dare, and stood where he was wringing his hands.

The shrine courtyard was wet, water all over the ground, and in the water lay overturned wooden buckets, carrying poles, trampled fruit, and one red dancing boot. In the middle of the courtyard was a stone platform, and on it a bronze basin, and in the basin the fire was still burning.

Only later did Yisi get the thing straight, and most of it was told him by those few old believers of the Liquan Ward. That afternoon the Pure Ones, each carrying two buckets of well water on a pole, had walked all the way from the Yining Ward to the Liquan Ward, singing psalms all the way, in Syriac, not quite in tune. When they reached the gate of the fire-temple, it was just as the shrine-master was stabbing his belly with the knife; everyone was looking at the knife and no one at them, and they carried their water straight into the courtyard. On the way Baaluo had told them that Elijah on Mount Carmel had men pour twelve barrels of water on the altar, and then called on the Lord, and fire came down from heaven and burned all the water up; today they too would pour water on the altar, and see whether the fire-worshippers' god sent fire. An old believer of the Liquan Ward recognised them at the shrine gate and reached out to pull back the last of them, and could not hold him.

The first bucket went on, and half the fire in the bronze basin went out with a great cloud of white smoke. The people in the courtyard were stunned at first, and then it exploded. To put out that basin of fire with water was, to fire-worshippers, worse than killing a man. The keeper of the fire was an old man past sixty, thin and short, white-bearded, in an old white robe, a white cloth over his mouth. He had kept the fire in this shrine forty years and never left it a single day. When the first bucket went on he was standing nearest, and was drenched. When the second of the Pure Ones came rushing in with his buckets, he did not get out of the way, but spread both arms and stood before the fire basin. The youngest, the butcher's son, had a carrying pole in his fist; he said afterwards he had only meant to push the old man aside, but the pole struck the old man on the temple. The old man pitched forward onto the edge of the stone platform, and his sleeve touched the half of the fire not yet out, and caught. The people of the shrine threw themselves on him, dragged him clear, and beat out the fire on his sleeve with their hands. By the time they dragged him clear, the old man had stopped moving.

The rest was all chaos. The *hu* threw themselves on the Pure Ones and beat them, and not one of the Pure Ones struck back. Afterwards the people of the ward all spoke of it: that those in white, while they were being beaten, every one knelt in the water with his arms over his head, still singing. The ward warden and the patrol at last got them apart, and all eleven of the Pure Ones were tied up, tied in a string, squatting by the shrine gate.

Yisi went up to them. Baaluo's face was covered in blood, one eye swollen so that it would not open; with the other eye he saw Yisi, and grinned, his teeth bloody too, and said: "Master Yi, the fire didn't go out."

Yisi did not answer him. The youngest boy was squatting at the very end, his trousers wet, whether with water or something else no one knew, his two hands tied in front of him, his eyes fixed on his hands all the while, muttering over and over that he hadn't meant to hit the old man's head. Baaluo turned his head and said to him: you did nothing wrong. The boy looked up, looked at Baaluo, and looked at Yisi.

Yisi turned and walked toward the middle of the courtyard.

---

The old man lay at the foot of the stone platform with a white cloth over him; one corner of the cloth had been soaked by the water and clung to his face. Yisi went over and squatted down and put out his hand to lift that wet corner of the cloth away and set the old man to rights.

Behind him someone shouted in Sogdian, "Don't touch him!", rushed up and knocked his hand away, saying the dead were not to be touched, they of the Daqin monastery knew nothing.

Yisi's hand stopped in the air. He remembered: fire-worshippers had their own rules; a dead man was unclean, and only men set apart for it might touch him, and those who touched him had to wash many times. He had known that in Balkh, and he had forgotten. He drew back his hand and knelt down on the wet ground, knelt three paces from the old man, and the water soaked into his knees at once. The people in the courtyard all looked at him. Someone cursed; someone threw a fruit rind at him, which hit his shoulder and rolled into the water. He did not move. He knelt on until dark, and before his eyes the people of the shrine twice added wood to the bronze basin.

---

Three days later, a memorial was sent in to the palace.

The man who sent it was a censor; Yisi did not know him. The memorial said: the *hu* monk Yisi, having received the imperial favour and been granted the purple, knew no gratitude, and let his followers carry weapons into a shrine and beat to death an old *hu* who kept it, shaking the capital; the *hu* religions were not the law of the Middle Kingdom to begin with; today they dared kill a man of a fire-temple; tomorrow what would they not dare? Let the acting office conferred on Yisi be revoked, and the purple robe granted him taken back; let the Daqin monastery in the Yining Ward be destroyed forthwith, and its monks sent back to their own countries. Appended to the memorial was a complaint from the monks of the Ximing Temple, saying that for half a year these *hu* religionists in white had repeatedly reviled the monks outside the temple gate, calling the Buddha an idol, and the monks slaves of an idol.

Official Pei came the next evening. He did not go into the side room, but stood at the monastery gate and said a few words to Yisi. He said the case belonged to the Metropolitan Prefecture to try; he served in the Metropolitan Prefecture, and the man who had baptized him was the monastery's own Elder Sabrisho; by the rules he had to stand aside, and had already gone to report it to his superiors himself. From tomorrow, until the case was closed, he could not come to the monastery again. When he had said it, he folded his hands in salute, turned and went. Yisi stood at the gate watching him go; he walked as he always did, a little lopsided.

That night the seven teachers again talked all night in the side room. Haddad said they must go to people—to Lord Guo, to anyone they could go to; Sabrisho said going to anyone was no use, the man had been killed by the monastery's people; Ishaq said: no use, and we must still go. Babai sat in the corner taking count of the monastery's scriptures scroll by scroll, and wrapping them again scroll by scroll, as if the monastery were to be sealed tomorrow. Gabriel said not a word. Narsai sat furthest from the lamp, and he too said nothing. Yonan asked Yisi what Lord Guo said. Yisi said he had not gone to ask.

He had not gone to ask, and did not mean to. Guo Ziyi was idle at home this year, and there were more eyes at court on him than on the rebels; if he said one word for a *hu* monk, in other men's mouths it would be more than one word.

---

On the fourth morning a minor clerk in a blue robe came to the monastery gate and delivered a document, very short: tomorrow at the hour of *chen*, present yourself at the Ministry of Rites in the Department of State Affairs, to be questioned.

Yisi took the document, read it once, folded it, and tucked it into his coat. That evening he ate with Jingjing: a pot of broth, a plate of flatbread, a dish of ginger; father and son said little. When they had eaten, Jingjing went to wash the bowls, and Yisi sat at the table a while, stood up, went to the wall, unbuckled the mace at his waist, and hung it back on the wooden peg on the wall.
