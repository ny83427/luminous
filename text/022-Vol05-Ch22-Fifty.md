# Volume Five　Chang'an

## Chapter 22　Fifty

> Every year he gathered the monks of four monasteries, and served them reverently and provided for them carefully for fifty days.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

In the second month of the first year of Qianyuan, the willows outside the Kaiyuan Gate put out buds.

That year, in the second month, the era name was changed. The old one had been Zhide, the new one was Qianyuan, and even the word for *year* was changed back from *zai* to *nian*. When people in the city spoke of it they often still said the third *zai* of Zhide, and only corrected themselves afterwards, and having corrected themselves stumbled over it again and said the first *zai* of Qianyuan. The edict changing the era was posted in the gate passage, and stayed posted half a month until the rain had washed half of it out, and no one replaced it.

From the first month on, Old Duan went out to the Kaiyuan Gate every few days.

The Kaiyuan Gate was the northernmost of the gates on the west side of Chang'an, and every road going west left from it. Outside the gate stood a milepost inscribed *To Anxi in the west, nine thousand nine hundred* li. Old Duan could not read many characters, but these he knew; Yisi had taught him. Yisi said it should by rights have said ten thousand *li*, and it said nine thousand nine hundred for luck, so that the traveller would feel he had not yet come to the end.

Old Duan would stand by the milepost and look west. The road was a dirt road, stretching to the edge of the sky. On the road there were caravans, post horses, men with carrying poles, men driving sheep. He looked them over one by one, and when he had finished, went back into the city.

In the first month, the monastery kept the Great Fast. By the rule of their church, before the day of the Lord's rising they fasted fifty days, eating one meal a day, after sunset. Yisi was now the monastery's only teacher of the Law, and those keeping the fast were he and a few old believers, Granny He and Old Mi among them. The gruel at the monastery gate was still boiled from morning till noon; Yisi stood by the pot ladling it out to others, and did not take a mouthful himself.

Xu Jing had sent a letter by a caravan coming from the west, saying they would set out around the winter solstice. Babai had written the letter, in very proper characters, and at the end there was a crooked line that Jingjing had added himself: "Pa, I don't drink cold water."

Yisi read that line, read it a long time, folded the letter and put it inside his coat.

Old Duan asked him: "What does the letter say?"

Yisi said: "He's been drinking cold water."

---

They arrived on the nineteenth of the second month.

That day Old Duan was standing by the milepost as usual. Near noon a line of dust rose on the road to the west, and under the dust was a long string of camels, and before the camels were horses, and before the horses was one man on a thin horse, his right hand tucked in his sleeve.

Old Duan narrowed his eyes and looked a while, then turned and ran back into the city.

When he got to the Yining Ward, Yisi was minding the pot at the monastery gate. The gruel had just been cooked and was steaming, and several dozen people were lined up at the gate.

"They're here," said Old Duan.

The ladle in Yisi's hand paused, and moved on again. He ladled that spoonful of gruel into an old woman's bowl, filled it, handed the ladle to Old Mi beside him, and said: "You serve. One ladle each."

Old Mi took the ladle.

Yisi untied the cloth round his waist, folded it, and set it on the stove platform. He went to the well, drew half a bucket of water and washed his hands, washed them very carefully, under the nails too. When he had washed them, he washed them again. Then he went back to the front courtyard, took down the old leather arm-guards hanging on the wall, looked at them, and hung them back up. In his grey robe, he went out of the gate.

Old Duan followed him a few steps and could not help saying: "Get a move on, will you."

Yisi said: "I am."

He did not walk fast at all. At the ward gate he stopped and asked Old Duan: "You saw clearly? There's a boy?"

"I saw."

"On a horse or a camel?"

"A horse. A little one."

Yisi nodded and walked on. After a stretch he asked again: "Was he sitting up straight?"

Old Duan looked at him once and said: "Straight."

---

The caravan had halted just inside the Kaiyuan Gate.

Xu Jing was first off his horse. He was thinner and darker, the skin of his face flaking white from the wind, and when he saw Old Duan he broke into a grin that creased his whole face. He drew that right hand with two missing fingers out of his sleeve and clasped Old Duan's hand with it, clasped it very tight; neither spoke, and they held on a long while before Xu Jing said: "Old Duan, you said you'd wait in Chang'an, and you really did."

Old Duan said: "I keep my word."

Behind Xu Jing came the twenty-four who guarded the monastery: four squads, six to a squad, in black, small round shields on their backs, leaning on shoulder-high staves shod with iron. The Tang people who saw them called them monk-soldiers; they did not call themselves that. Their leader Sargis sat his horse, tall and strong, a head above the men beside him. He looked at the city gate, looked at the street inside it, looked at the half-fallen ward walls on either side of the street, and his face showed nothing.

Behind them came the seven teachers in grey robes. Ishaq's camel was the most conspicuous, because Ishaq was the fattest.

Further back was a boy.

The boy was on a small horse, a good deal taller than when he had left; his legs had grown longer, his neck longer, his face burned a dark red. He wore an old lined robe whose sleeves had grown short, showing a length of wrist. Round his neck hung a cord, and on the cord a small wooden cross.

He saw Yisi.

Yisi stood at the side of the street and did not come forward.

Jingjing got down from his horse. He got down very fast; his feet touched the ground, he ran two steps forward, and stopped. He stood there, still three or four paces from Yisi.

The two of them looked at each other.

There was wind in the gate passage, and it set the hem of Jingjing's robe fluttering.

Jingjing saw the scar on his father's face. From a little left of the middle of his brow it ran up onto his forehead; it was still red, with the marks of the stitches along its edges. Half his left eyebrow was missing.

Yisi saw Jingjing's wrists. They were very thin—a little thicker than when he left, but still thin. On the backs of his hands were the marks left by healed chilblains.

Jingjing took another step forward.

Yisi took a step forward too.

Between them there was still one step.

"On the road," said Yisi. "Did you drink cold water?"

Jingjing's mouth moved.

"Once," he said.

"Did you get the runs?"

"I did."

"How many days?"

"Two. Elder Ishaq boiled me some kind of herb. Bitter."

"Did you eat ginger?"

"I did."

Jingjing nodded. His eyes had gone red; he blinked hard, once, and did not let it fall.

Yisi raised his hand. It rose halfway, paused, and did not come down on Jingjing's head. He reached past and took the reins of Jingjing's little horse, and held them in his fist.

"Come on," he said. "There's gruel in the pot."

He led the horse into the city. Jingjing walked beside him, at the same pace as him, not fast, not slow.

Old Duan stood behind watching a while, then turned his face away and said to Xu Jing: "That's how he is."

Xu Jing said: "The boy's the same. In Xizhou he looked east every day. Ask him what he was looking at and he'd say the wind."

---

That evening a great many people came to the monastery.

The seven teachers, the twenty-four guard brothers, Xu Jing, Old Duan, and a few old believers, Granny He, Old Mi and Widow Kang among them. The main hall had not been repaired yet, and the hole in its roof was covered with straw mats, so everyone crowded into the side rooms, and those who could not fit stood in the courtyard. It was still the fast; after sunset, each had a bowl of gruel.

Yisi greeted them one by one.

Narsai made him a bow, very correctly, and not one word more. Ishaq hugged him, hugged him so that the wound in his back went tight, and he made no sound. Babai took his hand and said all those letters he had written for the boy; the boy's characters were better than his, only he would not write more. Gabriel smiled at him, the left side of his face wrinkled, the right side whole, so that when he smiled half of it was a smile and half was something else. Sabrisho asked him who was keeping the monastery's rule these days. Haddad asked him the price of grain in the city.

Yonan was last.

Yonan stood at the door and did not come in. His face was still as young as ever, beardless, but his hands were an old man's hands, folded before him.

Yisi went over.

The two had known each other before. In those days one had been the bishop, the other a priest. More than ten years ago.

Yonan looked at him, at the scar on his face, looked a while and said: "He's grown up."

Yisi said: "Mm."

Yonan said again: "He teaches Tang speech well. I learned slowly, and he was never impatient."

Yisi did not answer. He looked at Yonan once, and then once into the room. Jingjing was sitting in the corner hugging his knees, watching the two of them.

Yonan bowed his head slightly, turned and went out. He went to the back courtyard; Yisi heard water being drawn at the well, one bucket, and another.

---

At night, when everyone had gone to sleep, Yisi called Jingjing into the side room and lit the lamp.

"Hold out your hand."

Jingjing held out his hand. Yisi pressed three fingers to his wrist, pressed a long time; changed to the other hand, and pressed a long time again. Then he had Jingjing open his mouth and looked at his tongue; had him pull up his lined robe, and pressed his belly, pressed on the left, pressed on the right, asked whether it hurt.

Jingjing said it didn't.

"Those two days you had the runs—what were they like?"

Jingjing went red, but he told him, item by item.

Yisi heard him out, and nodded.

"You've got thin," he said. "Nothing else."

"Pa," said Jingjing, "you're doing it like a doctor."

Yisi nudged the lamp wick, and said: "I can doctor."

Jingjing thought, and said: "I remember. When I was little the monastery had a row of cabinets, lots of little drawers. Granny He says you saw to her old man's leg."

"She told you?"

"She did. She cried when she told it."

Yisi said nothing.

Jingjing lay down, lay a while, and said again: "Pa, Elder Yonan says he's seen me asleep when I was little."

Yisi said: "Go to sleep."

Jingjing said no more. He lay on his side, one hand clutching the wooden cross round his neck, and was soon fast asleep.

Yisi could not sleep. He lay watching that hand. In the small hours he heard water being drawn at the well in the back courtyard again, very softly, a pull at a time. He looked out through a crack in the window. Yonan was squatting by the well, washing his hands in the moonlight. When he had washed them he sat on the rim of the well, not moving, looking east.

---

The next day was the day of the Lord's rising, and the fast was broken.

The meal that broke the fast was mutton broth, stewed most of the day. Yisi could not stew; Widow Kang had done it.

The seat at the head of the table was empty. The elder Ahad had stayed in Balkh; that had been known since Old Duan came back last fifth month. As Yisi carried the broth in, he glanced that way once.

Before they ate, Yisi set two bowls beside himself.

One was Jingjing's. One was of coarse earthenware, with a chip in its rim and a pinch of earth in it.

Jingjing looked at that bowl.

"Du De's," said Yisi.

Jingjing had heard the name. Pa's letters had mentioned Changshan, mentioned an old soldier who changed his dressings. He did not know what happened afterwards.

He did not ask. He moved his own bowl over beside that one, moved it until the two bowls touched, and only then picked up his chopsticks.

At table they spoke Tang. The seven teachers' Tang, after most of a year's study in Xizhou, was good in some and bad in others. Ishaq wanted more broth and said "*Mo pa*, another bowl," and the whole room laughed. Ishaq laughed too, and when he had finished laughing he said: "I know I said it wrong. I just like the word."

Yonan sat at the far end of the table. He reached his ladle across the table and gave Jingjing another spoonful of broth.

Yisi saw. He lowered his head and drank from his own bowl, drank twice, and raised his head again. Yonan was already talking to Babai, and Jingjing was blowing on his bowl.

Halfway through the meal, Haddad told of something he had heard in the West Market. Since Luoyang had been recovered, a Sogdian merchant had come from there, bringing word from that old deacon in the Xiushan Ward: the monastery had burned, but the people were still there, ten or so of them, worshipping in a believer's house.

Yisi asked: "From Luoyang to here—how many days?"

Haddad said: "Going fast, half a month."

Yisi asked no more. He thought: next year, at the Fast, have them all come.

---

After the start of spring, Chang'an was short of grain.

The city had been recovered, but the fields had not been sown. Last autumn most of the crops of Guanzhong had rotted in the fields with no one to bring them in. When the rebels left, they had plundered once more. By the second and third months the price of rice in the city had risen to several hundred cash a *dou*, and in the wards some families began pulling down their door panels to trade for rice.

The government opened its granaries.

The place where the granary was opened was outside the East Market. At the end of the second month, on the day people went to draw rice, the line stretched from the gate of the East Market all the way out of the Chunming Gate. The official in charge stood at the granary gate, gave the word, and the gates were opened.

What came after, no one could say clearly.

Some said that the people at the back heard there was not enough rice and pushed forward; some said someone at the front drew his rice and would not move, and those behind shoved him; others said no one pushed at all, there were simply too many, and they fell of themselves. However it was, all at once there was chaos at the granary gate. The people surged forward like water bursting a dike. The official in charge stood on the steps and tried to shout and could not, and was squeezed off the steps.

When the crowd had gone, a dozen and more people lay on the ground. Old people, children, and that official.

The official was a revenue clerk of the Metropolitan Prefecture; what his surname was, afterwards no one remembered. When they lifted him up his official cap was gone, one shoe was gone, and he still had a roll of registers clenched in his hand.

Yisi was there that day too. He had gone to see whether he could draw a little rice for the monastery. When the chaos broke out at the granary gate he was far back, and the crowd pushed him backward, his feet unable to stop, until he fetched up against the foot of a wall and stood. He saw the people surge forward swathe by swathe, and fall back swathe by swathe, and of those who went down, not one got up.

He thought of the Qingqu. That day at the Qingqu, he too had been pushed along like this, and those pushing him had all been his own side.

That evening, he did not eat.

---

The man in charge of relief was an official named Pei.

Official Pei was about forty, not tall, with very high brow ridges and deep-set eyes, so that when he looked at you it was as if he were looking out from behind a mountain ridge. He was the owner of that roll of registers. He had had the registers drawn up, drawn up over half a month, ward by ward, and when they were done gave them to the revenue clerk to issue the rice.

After the clerk died, Official Pei did not leave his house for three days.

On the fourth day he went to the Yining Ward.

He had heard people say it. People said there was a *hu* monastery in the Yining Ward that gave out gruel at its gate every day, had done so all winter, and not one person had been crushed to death. He did not quite believe it, and went to see for himself.

When he arrived it was morning. More than a hundred people were lined up at the monastery gate, a long line, but a very orderly one. There were two pots at the gate; by one stood a *hu*, by the other a scar-faced old soldier. The people in line came up to the pot, held out a bowl, got one ladle, and went off. Old women stood in the line, and whoever pushed forward, they sent to the back.

Official Pei stood across the street and watched all morning.

Towards noon the gruel ran out. The *hu* scraped the last of it from the pot and gave it to a child, then straightened up and looked once across the street.

Official Pei went over.

"Are you the Great Virtue Yi?"

Yisi folded his hands in salute and said: "This humble monk is Yisi."

Official Pei said: "A hundred-odd people here. How do you line them up?"

"There aren't many, and the old folk of the ward help keep watch," said Yisi. "Anyone who pushes is sent to the back."

Official Pei nodded, and said: "The Ministry of Revenue has allotted rice. Spring famine—I've registered the households in the city short of grain, a little over six thousand souls. The rice is in the granary, and it can't be given out. Last time it was given out, a dozen and more died."

When he came to the word *died*, the eyes under those brow ridges moved.

Yisi said: "This humble monk saw it."

"You were there?"

"I was."

Official Pei said nothing. After a while he said: "Here, all winter, not one died."

"Eleven died," said Yisi. "Of sickness."

Official Pei looked at him.

"A hundred-odd people, this humble monk can watch over," said Yisi. "Six thousand, he cannot. Your honour should ask someone else."

Official Pei did not answer. He stood there looking at those two empty pots, looked a long time. Then he cupped his hands to Yisi and said: "On the ninth of the third month the rice leaves the granary. The Ministry's rice leaves the granary this once only, and is to be given out in one day. Whether you come or not, I'll be there."

He went. Yisi watched him go. The man walked a little lopsided, as if the sole of one shoe had worn thin.

---

That night Yisi lay in the side room listening to the snoring of the guard brothers in the courtyard. Twenty-four men in two tents, snoring now here, now there, like two herds of oxen fighting. Jingjing slept beside him, slept very deep.

He thought about that crowd at the granary gate. He thought of the Qingqu: an old soldier running ahead of him with an arrow in his back, still running, running until he pitched face-down on the ground, and those behind trod over him.

Near dawn, he got up. He thought: when the praises are done, I'll go to the Metropolitan Prefecture and give Official Pei his answer: this humble monk will not come.

---

The monastery's morning praises were said under the jujube tree in the back courtyard. The main hall had not been repaired, its roof let in the wind, and Gabriel said under the jujube tree was just as good; the Lord did not mind.

That morning it was Gabriel's turn to expound the scripture.

He sat on a stone under the jujube tree with the scroll spread on his knees; the scroll was in Syriac, and he read it and expounded it in Tang as he went. His Tang was the best of the seven.

Yisi sat at the very back. Jingjing sat beside him.

Gabriel came to that day when the Lord was in a desert place, and five thousand were following him. It was late, and the disciples said: send them away, let them go into the villages and buy themselves food. The Lord said: give ye them to eat.

The disciples said: we have only five loaves and two fishes.

When Gabriel had read this far he paused, raised his head and looked at the people sitting before him. The guard brothers, the teachers, Granny He and the few old believers, Jingjing, and Old Duan, standing at the very back leaning against the wall. Old Duan did not believe, but he came every morning, to hear the fun, he said.

Gabriel bent his head again and read on.

"And the Messiah commanded them to make all sit down by companies upon the green grass. And they sat down in ranks, by hundreds, and by fifties."

He read on: how the Lord took the five loaves and the two fishes, looked up to heaven and blessed, and brake the loaves and gave them to the disciples to set before them.

Yisi listened, and what he was thinking of was still the Metropolitan Prefecture, and how to put it when he saw Official Pei.

Jingjing stirred beside him, leaned over, and asked under his breath: "Pa, why does he make people sit down first?"

Yisi had not understood. "What?"

"There's no bread yet," said Jingjing, "and he makes five thousand people sit down first. If they sit down and then get up to fetch it, isn't that slower?"

Yisi looked at him.

Jingjing said: "In Xizhou, when the monastery north of the city gave out gruel, people stood to get it. Once, the pot got knocked over in the crush."

When he had said it, he went back to listening to Gabriel.

Gabriel was still reading. Yisi did not take in a word.

He thought of that day at the granary gate, when he had been pushed backward and his feet could not stop. He thought: a man sitting on the ground who wants to get up has first to put his hand down, first to bend his legs.

He had never once thought about sitting down.

Gabriel finished reading, closed the scroll, raised his head, and saw Yisi looking at him.

"Yisi," said Gabriel. "What are you thinking about?"

Yisi said: "Sitting down."

Gabriel did not understand, and looked at Jingjing. Jingjing was looking at his Pa too.

---

That morning at the hour of *si*, Yisi went to the Metropolitan Prefecture. The man at the gate went in to announce him, and it was a long while before he was led in.

Official Pei was in a side hall, with registers piled on his table. Yisi saluted him and told him what had happened that morning. He told it very slowly, and however he put it, it came to only one thing: first have people sit down, then give out the rice.

"Whether it will work," he said, "this humble monk does not know."

When he had heard it, for a long time Official Pei made no sound.

"Sit down," he said. "Six thousand people all sitting down—how big a piece of ground would that take?"

Yisi could not answer.

Official Pei drew a map from the table and spread it out, searched the south of the city a while, and his finger stopped west of the Qujiang Pool. "There used to be a garden here. The owner fled, the walls fell, it's waste ground," he said. "Whether it's big enough, I don't know."

He patted the pile of registers on the table. "The registers are drawn up by ward. Have each ward's warden bring the ward's people, one ward's people sitting in one place, and the warden knows them. Neighbours all know who's who; anyone trying to claim twice won't get a seat."

Yisi said: "Good."

"Six thousand people sitting in one great expanse," said Official Pei. "Can you watch them on your own?"

Yisi could not answer.

Official Pei rolled up the map and said: "Go back and think. Come again before the ninth."

---

Back at the monastery, Yisi called the teachers, Xu Jing, Old Duan and Sargis to the jujube tree in the back courtyard and told them.

Sabrisho spoke first: "Six thousand people—why give it out in one day? Give it out separately. One ward a day, a few hundred people a ward, give it out over a dozen days or so."

"The rice leaves the granary only once," said Yisi. "Official Pei said so. In one day."

Sabrisho said no more.

Xu Jing said: "Then seat them separately. One great expanse, no one can watch."

"In how many?" asked Haddad.

No one answered.

Old Duan was squatting on the ground stirring the dirt with a twig. He stirred a long time, and said: "Fifty. In the camp a company is fifty men, and one company leader can watch them. Any more, and the ones at the back can't hear him shout."

Gabriel said beside him: "In the scripture it's fifty too. Some by fifties."

Yisi looked at Old Duan, and then at Gabriel, and said: "Then fifty."

Babai drew a few lines on the ground and said: "Six thousand souls, fifty to a block—a hundred and twenty blocks."

"Where would you get a hundred and twenty men?" asked Xu Jing.

Yisi counted them one by one. Seven teachers, twenty-four guard brothers—that made thirty-one. Old believers from the ward who could stand steady and speak clearly, about twenty. Still seventy short.

Old Duan said: "The camp has old soldiers."

Xu Jing said: "Soldiers in charge of famished people? One wrong word and they'll lay hands on them."

"Pick old ones," said Old Duan. "The older ones have been beaten and have beaten men, and know what it's like when it comes to blows. They won't be the first to lay a hand on anyone."

Xu Jing thought, and did not argue further. He said that then the rules had to be fixed hard first, and once fixed, not a word changed. He took the twig from Old Duan and wrote on the ground, writing a rule and reading it out:

First sit down, then the rice is given. Anyone not sitting gets none.

One man watches fifty. The fifty sit in one block, sit in front of you.

The rice is brought forward by the runners from behind; no one comes to the front to take it.

No shouting. If anything's wrong, raise your hand.

When he had written them, Sargis asked: "And if someone won't sit?"

Yisi said: "Wait for him to sit."

"And if he never sits?"

Yisi thought a moment, and could not say.

Sargis said: "Then watch him. Don't push him, don't touch him. When we held city gates and someone tried to rush in, we didn't strike; we just stood. Stand without moving, and he can't get in."

He said a few words in Syriac to his brothers. Yisi heard; what he said was: hold, and there'll be no need to fight.

Narsai had said nothing all this while. Nor had Ishaq; he was leaning against the jujube tree with his eyes half shut, as if asleep.

When they broke up, Yonan came over and said he would watch a block too. He said too that his Tang was poor; could Jingjing go with him, to pass on what he said?

Yisi said: "Jingjing goes with me."

Yonan looked at him a while, nodded, and went.

Old Duan went to find Hou Siyuan and Cheng Chongyi. Hou Siyuan and Cheng Chongyi went round the camp and came back with more than fifty men, all past thirty—some short an ear, some lame in a leg, some short of nothing, only not fond of talking. Another dozen and more were found in the ward, and that made up a hundred and twenty.

---

The day before the rice was given out, at the hour of *shen*, Yisi went to the waste ground west of the Qujiang Pool. Jingjing went with him.

Grass had grown on the waste ground; it was spring, and the grass was green. He walked it from the north end to the south end, from the east end to the west, measuring it pace by pace. Where there was a pit, where there were the footings of a fallen wall, where there was the stump of a felled tree, he noted each in turn. Jingjing walked behind him counting the paces for him.

When he had measured it, he stood at the north end and looked at that great empty expanse. He looked a long time.

Jingjing took a flatbread from his coat, broke it, and held out half.

Yisi took it and bit into it. The bread was cold and hard; Widow Kang had baked it that morning.

The two of them stood at the north end of the waste ground and ate that one flatbread between them. The sun went down the west, and Yisi said: "Let's go back." They walked very fast, and got into the Yining Ward before the drums for the closing of the gates had finished.

---

The day the rice was given out was the ninth of the third month.

Before it was light the people came.

At first it was still chaos. People came from every side; the ward wardens led them at the front, and in the leading of them lost hold of them. People pushed forward, pushed towards the rice carts. The carts stood at the north end of the open ground, forty of them, the sacks of rice piled high on them. The runners of the Metropolitan Prefecture stood by the carts, waiting to shoulder the sacks.

The hundred and twenty men stood spread out, a dozen and more paces between each.

Yisi stood by the rice carts. As the people surged this way, he saw the faces at the front, every pair of eyes on the rice carts, exactly as on that day at the granary gate.

Old Duan stood in the very front row. He unbuckled his blade and handed it to Xu Jing, and stood empty-handed before the crowd.

Someone pushed up right in front of him.

Old Duan did not push him back. He sat down himself first, plumped down on the grass, crossed his legs, and looked up at the man.

"Sit," he said.

The man stood stunned.

"Sit down," said Old Duan. "Sit down, and the rice gets given out."

The man looked at him, looked at the rice carts behind him, looked left and right. A dozen paces to the left, a *hu* in black had sat down too, his staff laid across his knees. A dozen paces to the right, an old soldier missing an ear had sat down too. Further right, a fat *hu* in a grey robe had sat down too, and was smiling at him and saying something. It took the man a long time to make out that it was *mo pa*.

The man sat down.

The people behind him saw him sit, and sat too.

One sat, two sat, a whole swathe sat. The sound of their sitting down was very soft, like wind passing over a wheatfield.

Yisi stood by the rice carts, watching them sit down swathe by swathe. Only then did he find that his own two legs were shaking.

---

The sun rose high and baked the grass white.

In the block Ishaq was watching, an old man's lips were so dry they had peeled, and he kept licking them. Ishaq watched him a while, got up, went to the rice carts, asked the runners for a bucket, went himself to the edge of the Qujiang Pool and drew a bucket of water, brought it back, set it at the front of his block, and with a bowl handed it out bowl by bowl.

The people in the blocks round about all watched that bucket. After a while Xu Jing went and fetched a bucket too. By noon there was a bucket at the front of every block.

---

Near noon, something happened.

In one block to the south, the rice had not yet arrived. A man sitting in it suddenly stood up and yelled: "The rice up front's run out!"

Half that block was on its feet at once. In the blocks beside it, people half rose too.

Yisi saw from the rice carts, and stood without moving. He could think of nothing to say.

That block was watched by an old soldier lame in one leg. The old soldier did not get up. He sat there, took a handful of roasted beans from his coat, picked one out and put it in his mouth, chewed it slowly, and chewing, looked up at the man once and said: "There are twenty carts of rice left. Standing up, can you see them?"

The man looked north. Heads were in the way; he could not see.

"Can't, can you," said the old soldier. "Sit down and wait. The rice comes forward from behind, comes right to you. While you're standing, the men bringing it can't get through."

The man stood a while, and sat down. The half that had stood up sat down too, one by one.

Yisi watched that old soldier the whole time.

---

That day Jingjing stayed by Yisi, running back and forth with messages. Afterwards he wrote in his journal that he did not remember much of that day. He remembered only that, looking south from the rice carts, the ground was covered with sitting people, block by block, a man watching at the front of each block, like rows and rows of crops growing in a field. He remembered too that it was very quiet that day. Six thousand people, so quiet you could hear the sacks being unloaded from the carts.

He remembered an old woman too. She was sitting in the block Xu Jing was watching, and when the rice was brought to her she did not take it, but first wept. Xu Jing squatted down and asked her what was wrong. She said her man had died at the granary gate by the East Market.

Xu Jing laid that *dou* of rice in her lap, pressed it with the hand that was missing two fingers, and said: "Sit, don't hurry. There's enough for everyone."

They gave out rice from the hour of *mao* to the hour of *shen*.

A little over six thousand souls, one *dou* each.

Not one person fell.

---

Official Pei watched from beginning to end.

He stood by the rice carts from the hour of *mao* to the hour of *shen* and did not move. He held the registers in his hands, and the names in the registers were ticked off one by one, and all the while he watched the brush moving across the paper.

When it was done the people dispersed. On the grass they left patches of flattened grass, block by block, so that you could still see the shape where each fifty had sat.

Official Pei came over to Yisi.

He looked at Yisi a long time. The eyes under those brow ridges were not as they had been that morning.

"This method," he said. "Where did it come from?"

Yisi thought, and said: "A book."

"What book?"

"A book about a man who gave a meal to five thousand people."

"He could afford to feed five thousand?"

"He only had five loaves."

Official Pei said nothing.

Yisi looked over that way. Old Duan and Xu Jing were sitting side by side on the grass, one cursing the other, the other laughing. Hou Siyuan had lain down, his two hands under his head, looking at the sky.

"The first of it," said Yisi, "my son asked. He asked why people should be made to sit down first."

Official Pei looked over that way. Jingjing was squatting on the grass with Babai, counting the blocks of flattened grass one by one, counting very seriously, his lips moving.

The wind blew over from the Qujiang Pool, with a little moisture in it, a little smell of green grass.

Official Pei stood a while, rolled up his registers and tucked them under his arm.

Then he sat down on the grass.
