# Volume Eleven　Huichang

## Chapter 75　Burying the Stele

> He set out the cross to fix the four quarters; He stirred the primal wind and brought forth the two breaths.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

Jingfu took his hand from the stele and turned round. It was nearly dark. Everyone in the courtyard stood without moving—Joseph, Elijah, Salang, Rijin, the Shi brothers, He Siniang holding Lingbao's arm, Liu'er and Ayi hiding behind He Siniang—each of them looking at him.

"The three days' fast is done, and the dispatch has come," said Jingfu. "From tonight on there's plenty of work for our strength, and on empty stomachs there'll be no strength for it. Everyone go and eat first; when we've eaten our fill, we'll deliberate. The children eat first, Uncle Lingbao eats first, and nobody is to tell me he isn't hungry."

He Siniang went to the stove and boiled a pot of gruel and baked a stack of sesame cakes, and Mother Kang sat by the stove working the bellows. Rijin went once to the south end of the ward and carried back a sack of rice, which he said his ma had saved these years handful by handful. "Ma says she was saving it for her own funeral; today we'll eat it first, and she'll save for the funeral again later." Everyone sat in the gallery of the back courtyard holding bowls, drinking the gruel very slowly; after three days without food, their stomachs couldn't hold much. Liu'er ate a whole sesame cake; Ayi ate two, reached for another, gripped it uneaten, and tucked it into her breast.

Lingbao drank half a bowl and set it down. Jingfu sat down beside him and asked quietly, "Uncle Lingbao, just now when I'd finished reading the dispatch you said something under your breath, and I didn't catch it."

Lingbao's face was toward the courtyard darkening beyond the gallery. After a while he said, "I said, the golden sceptre has been held out."

Jingfu asked no more. He Siniang pushed the half bowl of gruel toward Lingbao's hand, and Lingbao found the rim and drank two more mouthfuls.

---

The deliberation was held before the stele. Two lamps were lit, one set at the foot of the stele, one hung in the jujube tree. Lingbao sat on the low stool under the jujube tree; the others stood or crouched round the stele.

Jingfu spoke first. "What the dispatch says, you've all heard: *the stele the Director is to dispose of as he judges fit.* These three days I've thought it over and over. The stele can't be hauled away; a stone that size, the few of us couldn't even get it out of the ward gate. It can't be hidden either, and it can't be held; Daoist Lu will come with the county's document and men, and we can't stop them. The disposal I've thought of is burial. Here, in the monastery, in the place it has stood sixty-four years, dig a pit, lay it down, put it in, cover it with earth, level it, and leave nothing on top. What Daoist Lu means to haul off is a standing stele. If there's no stele, what will he haul?"

Lingbao had been listening all along under the jujube tree. At this he spoke, his voice not loud, but very hard. "No. Fu'er, do you remember that year Daoist Lu came to measure the ground, and you wanted to cover the stele with a cloth, and what I said? I said, cover it, and the monastery has shown itself guilty. Today you want to bury it, and burying is more thorough than covering. Covering is hanging a cloth; burying is taking the stele away from under heaven. The characters Pa carved, the characters Brother wrote—from now on no one will see them."

He drew a breath and went on: "If Daoist Lu hauls the stele off to bed a canal bank, it'll be trodden on and spoiled. I know that. But trodden on and spoiled, it's still under heaven. One day someone walking by the Yong'an Canal will step on that stone, turn it over, see the characters, and know that Chang'an once had such a monastery. Bury it, and there's nothing."

Jingfu did not argue with him. He went over, crouched before Lingbao, put his two hands on Lingbao's knees, and said, "Uncle Lingbao, you're right. Covering is guilt, and burying is guilt too; that I own. But the two guilts aren't the same fear. That year I wanted to cover it for fear Daoist Lu would take that line on the stele and denounce us to the palace—I was afraid the monastery's people would be punished; what I wanted to protect was people. Today the people can't be protected. On the eighteenth the monastery will be inventoried, and all of us—Tang men back to our native places, foreigners sent back to their own countries—not one can stay. The people can't be protected; what I want to protect is the stele. Bedded under a canal bank, soaked by water, smeared with mud, ground by ox-cart after ox-cart—grind it a few years, and the characters are gone. Buried in the monastery, sand below, felt above, the characters stay whole. Whether anyone will dig it up one day, I don't know. I only know: bury it, and the characters remain; let it be hauled off, and the characters are gone."

Lingbao's hands rested on his knees, his fingers tapping, tap by tap. He tapped a good while, and stopped.

"I can't see this stele any more, but I've felt it all my life," he said. "When I was small Brother held me up to feel the characters Pa carved, and later I felt them myself, felt them until I was old. After this I won't be able to feel them. Fu'er, the reasons I gave just now are all reasons, but they're also the reasons of what I can't bear to let go. What you say is right. Bury it. When you bury it, let me sit beside it, and I'll recite it to it."

Salang had been standing by the stele all along. Now he asked where it would be buried.

"Behind the stele," said Jingfu. "The stele faces the monastery gate. Dig the pit in that patch behind it—the ground's level, no tree roots. Let the stele fall backward, and when it's down the characters face up; let it fall forward, and the characters are face down underneath. The pit must be longer than the stele and wider than the stele, deep enough that once the stele's in there are still three feet of earth above it. Sand at the bottom, the stele lying on the sand, felt over it, then earth; stone in earth doesn't rot."

Joseph asked, "And the base?" The stele stood on a single slab of bluestone, its foot set in a groove in the base. The south-east corner of the base was missing a piece, struck off with a hammer in the Yuanhe years by that lame old soldier from the south of the city. That broken piece Jingjing had not thrown away; he had kept it on the bottom shelf of the scripture cabinet, thirty-odd years now.

"The base too," said Jingfu. "Bury it with the stele, at the stele's foot. The missing corner—I'll take that broken stone from the scripture cabinet and fit it back."

Rijin reckoned up hands. "The stele's nearly ten feet tall, thousands of *jin*; the base is over a thousand too. Those who can put their strength to it—Brother Salang, the Shi brothers, me, the Director—five or six; Brother Joseph and Brother Elijah are past fifty and count only half. Not enough."

"If it's not enough, we'll find more," said Jingfu. "But the people of the ward—if the inspectorate hears, if the county hears, they'll bear the blame. I won't go and call them. Those willing to come, come; those who don't, none of us is to blame them."

---

That night no one went to call them, and people came of themselves.

First Mother Kang went back once to the south end of the ward and told those few old believing households. At the second watch four young men came from the south end, all sons and grandsons of those households, with spades and picks on their shoulders, one with a coil of hemp rope as well. The one at their head said as he came in, "Director, my granny says three generations of our family have drunk gruel at the monastery. If we don't come tonight, she'll have no face to meet Master Jingjing hereafter."

After them came two helpers the younger Shi brother had had when pushing the cart for the dead, from the east end of the ward, not believers, and they came too. One said, "We've pushed the cart with the Shi Second ten-odd years. Of those rows of stakes outside the Kaiyuan Gate, half we buried. The monastery's given us gruel ten-odd years; tonight we've come to pay back a few spadefuls of earth. Don't thank us either; if you thank us we'll be embarrassed."

At the third watch a man dropped over the back-courtyard wall. He fell as he landed, scrambled up, and it was Qushen. On his back he carried a coil of thick rope, twice as thick as the one from the south end. "This is what the temple used to hoist the beams when it repaired the hall. I took it from the storeroom," he said, untying it as he spoke, very fast. "To lower the stele, hemp rope won't hold; it has to be this. I have to be back before daylight. When you've used it, don't return it; I'll say it was lost. One coil of rope lost—Daoist Lu won't look into it. I can't stay. If I stay here, your business becomes the temple's business."

At the fourth watch someone knocked softly at the monastery gate. Salang went to open it, and outside stood Lü Shao. He had changed into a short coat and carried a lantern. He said, "Today at the Secretariat I heard someone say the inspectorate has sent the Daqin Monastery a dispatch deferring it three days, with the stele left to the Director's disposal. I guessed at once what you'd be doing. Director, I can't dig, but I can write. I've come to hold you a light and watch. If ever anyone asks about tonight, there has to be one person who remembers it clearly."

---

The earth for the pit was broken that night. Lingbao sat under the jujube tree, He Siniang putting a lined robe round him. He couldn't see, but he could hear the spades going into the earth, the picks striking stones, the men's breathing; someone swore under his breath, and someone else laughed once. He listened to a few spadefuls, and began to recite, first in Syriac—the Syriac lines at the foot of the stele—and when he had recited them, in Tang, from the first sentence of the stele's text: "*Behold: the Eternal, true and still, before all firsts and Himself without beginning; the Unfathomable, spirit and void, after all lasts and wondrously enduring…*"

Joseph stopped his spade to listen a few lines and said, "The Great Worthy is reciting the stele."

"It's the stele," said Lingbao. "The day it was set up I was fourteen, standing beside Pa. Brother stood before the stele and recited—the hymn at the end; the whole text he didn't recite; by the time he'd finished, the sun would have been overhead. All these years the stele has stood here, people coming and going, some reading, some touching, but not one of them has ever recited it to its face from beginning to end without missing a character. Today I'll recite it for Brother. You dig your digging and I'll recite my reciting. By the time I've finished, you should be nearly done digging."

So he recited all night, very slowly, line by line, reciting a line, pausing, reciting the next. When he reached *The true and constant Way is wondrous and hard to name; its works shine so plainly that we are forced to call it the Luminous Teaching*, the pit was dug waist-deep. When he reached Taizong, reached Alopen, reached *The office concerned at once built a Daqin monastery in the Yining Ward of the capital and ordained twenty-one monks*, he paused a moment and said, "That line—these years we've been counting it every day." When he reached *The great benefactor, the monk Yisi—Grand Master of the Golden Seal and Purple Ribbon, Associate Deputy Military Governor of Shuofang, Acting Director of the Palace Administration, granted the purple robe*, his voice went hoarse for a moment. When he reached *among the white-robed Luminous scholars, now we see the man*, he stopped, took the mouthful of water He Siniang handed him, drank it, and recited on.

Toward dawn the pit had been dug a man's height deep. Qushen went back over the wall to the temple, and the coil of thick rope stayed at the edge of the pit.

---

The next day the monastery gate stayed shut, the inspectorate's notice still posted on it: *not to be moved without leave, nor sold privately.* Inside the gate the people were digging the pit. Those of the ward passing the gate heard spades inside; some stopped and peered through the crack, some didn't, and hurried past.

Headman Liu passed the gate twice. The second time he knocked. Salang opened a crack, and Headman Liu looked in, saw the pit, saw the earth heaped beside it, saw the stele still standing. He lowered his voice and said, "Let me tell you plainly. If people in the ward ask, I'll say the monastery's putting its things in order; the inspectorate's dispatch tells the Director to dispose of them, and what I've seen is disposing, and that's what I'll say. But if the inspectorate's people come and ask me what the monastery dug, I'll tell them as it is; I won't hide it for you. Your business, you bear it yourselves." With that he drew the gate shut for Salang and left.

They went on digging through the day, and when the sun slanted west the pit was finished: a foot longer than the stele, a foot wider than the stele, so deep that a man standing on the bottom with his arm stretched up could not touch the edge. The younger Shi brother jumped down and trod the bottom level; Ayi and Liu'er drew water from the well in buckets and poured it into the pit; the water soaked in, and he trod it again until it was firm. Rijin pushed the cart for the dead and brought three cartloads of sand from the sand heap at the south end of the ward, and tipped them into the pit, half a foot thick.

Then the raising of the stele. Its foot was set in the groove in the base, and the groove had been filled with lime mortar; after sixty-four years it was as hard as stone. Salang and Rijin, with chisels, chipped along the seam of the groove bit by bit, chipped more than an hour, and loosened it. In front of the stele, on the side toward the gate, they drove two thick wooden stakes; the coil of thick rope Qushen had left was tied at one end to the stakes and at the other passed round the head of the stele and caught under the cross. The young men from the south end hauled on one rope, the Shi brothers on the other. Salang and Rijin each took a wooden bar and stood before the stele with the ends of the bars against the upper half of its shaft. Jingfu stood at the side of the stele, where he could see both the ropes in front and the pit behind.

"Brother Salang, Rijin—push it back. You on the ropes—when I call, ease off a handful," said Jingfu. "Ease slowly; better slow, never fast. Anyone who feels he can't hold it, call out; don't strain past it. Nobody is to stand on the side the stele falls."

Salang and Rijin pushed, and the stele tilted back a little, and the ropes went taut. Jingfu called, the ropes eased a handful, the stele tilted back a little more; he called again, they eased again, and the foot of the stele shifted out of the groove inch by inch. When the foot rested on the edge of the base and the shaft lay slanting, most of its weight hung on the ropes, seven or eight men hauling, their faces red with strain. Jingfu knelt at the edge of the pit, his eyes on the head of the stele, calling, call after call. The head of the stele reached down into the pit bit by bit and came to rest on the sand without knocking the cross. The men above levered the foot with the bars, prised it off the edge of the base, and the foot slid down the side of the pit and dropped onto the sand with a soft thud; the sand sprayed up, sprayed over the edge, sprayed Jingfu's face.

The stele lay down, characters upward, its head to the north and its foot to the south. Everyone in the courtyard came to stand at the edge of the pit and look down. Lying at the bottom of the pit the stele seemed smaller than standing, greyish white, its lines of characters lit by the daylight, the strokes visible, the two dragons at its head visible, the row of Syriac letters at its foot visible—carved by Master Yisi.

Only then did Jingfu climb down into the pit by the ladder and walk once round the stele, bent over, looking from head to foot; not one character was damaged. He climbed out, his face covered in sand, and said, "There's still the base." The base was harder than the stele. It was one solid block, its underside bedded in rammed earth, and the bars could not get under it; they had first to dig out the earth beneath it, lever it with the bars, lift it a little, push in a roller, lever again, push in another—they levered an hour before the base moved. Then several men pushed together, pushed it to the edge of the pit; the base turned over once, slid down the side of the pit, and landed at the stele's foot with a heavy thump, and the sand on the bottom of the pit jumped. As the base came down, the broken south-east corner faced upward.

Jingfu went back to the scripture room, took the cloth bundle from the bottom shelf of the scripture cabinet, and opened it. Inside was that broken stone, the size of a fist, flat on one face, jagged on the other, the white mark of the hammer still visible on the jagged face. He carried it down into the pit to the base and pressed it into the broken place, jagged face to jagged face. He pressed once; the stone held; it was fitted back, as if it had never fallen off.

Joseph, standing at the edge of the pit, watched and said, "Director, Master Jingjing said in his day: put the broken stone in the scripture cabinet; don't mend it."

"The Master didn't mend it then because the stele was still standing," Jingfu answered, his face turned up. "With the stele standing and a corner missing, people saw it and knew this stele had been struck, and was struck, and still stood. That missing corner was missing to some purpose. Now the stele's lying down to be buried. Buried in the earth, no one will see it again. The missing corner—who would it be for? Mend it, and the stele is whole."

---

The felt was laid by Ayi and Liu'er. He Siniang brought two felts from her house at the south end of the ward, the ones she had spread the year she married Lingbao—white when they were new, grey now after fifteen years. The two children carried one each down the ladder Jingfu had set up into the pit, and spread them from the stele's head to its foot, Liu'er the upper half and Ayi the lower, crouching on the stele, smoothing the felt bit by bit.

When Ayi reached the stele's foot she saw that row of Syriac letters, reached out and touched them, and asked Jingfu, "Director, what writing is this? It looks a bit like Uighur writing to me."

Jingfu said, "It's the Patriarchate's writing. If it looks alike to you, that's no wonder. It and Uighur writing came down from one old ancestor; many generations apart, they don't look much alike any more, but you can still tell they're one family."

Ayi touched it once more, and covered it with the felt.

Lingbao was the last to go down into the pit. He had He Siniang and Rijin hold him and went down the ladder rung by rung, reached the bottom, stood on the sand, reached out, and felt the felt, and felt the stele under the felt. He lifted a corner of the felt at the stele's foot, slipped his hand in, felt the face of the stele, felt that row of Syriac letters, one by one, and at one place stopped. It was Pa's name, Yazdbozid, carved by Pa's own hand. His hand rested on those letters a good while. Then he let the felt down, smoothed it, and said to Rijin, "Help me up."

---

The earth was filled in from dusk until midnight. A dozen-odd men, a dozen-odd spades, spadeful after spadeful into the pit—first along the two sides of the stele, then over it. The earth fell on the felt with a soft patter, pattered for some dozens of spadefuls, and then stopped; the felt was covered. Lü Shao stood at the edge of the pit holding the lantern to light them. When someone called for him to light this side, he lit this side; when someone called for that side, that side.

Halfway through the filling, the young man at the head of those from the south end stopped his spade and asked Jingfu, "Director, will it be dug out again one day?"

"I don't know," said Jingfu.

"And if it's never dug out?"

Jingfu leaned on his spade and thought, and said, "Then it'll stay here always. What's written on the stele is the story of this monastery, of Chang'an, of things granted and written by several reigns of Sons of Heaven. The characters are carved in stone, the stone is buried in earth, and the characters can't run away. Whatever year, whatever day someone digs it up and reads it and knows that Chang'an once had such a monastery and such people—that's enough. Who digs it up, what times those will be, isn't ours to manage. We only manage to bury it well tonight."

The young man thought, asked no more, and went on filling.

It was filled, filled level, filled as high as the ground beside it. Salang and Rijin rammed it firm with a wooden rammer, blow by blow. The Shi brothers brought a few old bricks from the back courtyard and laid them on top, scattered a layer of loose earth, and swept it level. The place where the stele had stood was empty now, with only a square mark left; the earth round the mark was darker. Jingfu had someone sweep it several times with a broom until it was even.

At midnight the wind rose. It came from the west and blew harder and harder, tossing the branches of the jujube tree, blowing out the lanterns in the gallery one by one, blowing up the loose earth of the courtyard into people's eyes. Jingfu told everyone to go indoors and went himself to look at the lamp at the gate.

The lamp at the gate Rijin had lit for him at dusk; he had been in the pit then and couldn't see to it. He reached the gate, and Salang was already crouched before the stone block. The paper cover over the lamp had long since been blown away and had rolled out into the ward street; the flame was leaning to one side, leaning until it nearly touched the rim of the dish, and Salang, with both hands cupped, was shielding it on the windward side. Jingfu went over and shielded it with him. The wind blew a while and rested a while; when it blew the flame bent down, bent nearly out; when the wind rested the flame stood up again. The two of them crouched before the stone block, four hands round that lamp, until nearly dawn, when the wind dropped.

---

At daybreak it rained. That day was the seventeenth of the eighth month. The rain was not heavy—fine and thin, falling on the courtyard, on the place where the stele had stood, on the newly swept loose earth. The loose earth grew wet and changed colour, the same colour as the ground beside it. The footprints, spade marks and rammer marks of all those people the night before were soaked soft by the rain one by one, and smoothed away.

The morning was stood in the rain. Jingfu stood in the middle of the courtyard, before the place where the stele had stood, and when the sky greyed looked back and counted: nineteen, not one fewer. Lingbao stood under the jujube tree. He had not gone back to the south end of the ward that night; he had lain down two hours in the scripture room and come out again before light.

---

Lu Shouchong came that afternoon. With him came a commandant of Chang'an County and four or five county clerks, and a dozen-odd men of the Tongzhen Temple carrying ropes, picks, spades and rollers, driving a big cart with two oxen in harness. He stood at the monastery gate and called for it to be opened. Salang opened it. Lu Shouchong walked in first, reached the front courtyard, and stopped.

He saw the empty ground. He looked back at the commandant, and the commandant was looking at the empty ground too.

"Where's the stele?" asked Lu Shouchong.

Jingfu stood before him and said, "Buried."

"Buried?" Lu Shouchong's voice rose at once. "First thing this morning I heard from the county that the Commissioner of Merit's office had deferred you three days with the stele left to the Director's disposal, and I knew it would go wrong. Three days' deferral is time for you to move the stele! I went to the county that same day to fetch the Shaofu, and I still didn't expect you to get in first. The inspectorate's notice is posted on the gate: *the articles in the monastery are not to be moved without leave.* You've buried the stele—that's theft of government property." He turned to the commandant. "Shaofu, this is defiance of orders."

The commandant was forty or so, in a blue robe. He did not take up what Lu said, but looked at Jingfu. Jingfu took the dispatch from his breast and held it out in both hands. "Shaofu, this is the Commissioner of Merit's dispatch. It came the evening before last. It says: *The scriptures, images, articles and stele of the monastery the Director is to dispose of as he judges fit, not allowing them to be destroyed, not allowing them to be sold privately.* I am the Director. I've judged, and I've disposed. The stele isn't destroyed, and it isn't sold. It's buried."

The commandant took the dispatch and read it through from beginning to end; when he reached the seal of the Commissioner's office at the end, and the mark beside the seal, he read it again, and only then gave it back to Jingfu. "Daoist, the county's document is the county's, and the Commissioner's dispatch is the Commissioner's. The monastery's articles and stele belong to the Commissioner of Merit's office. The Commissioner's dispatch came first; the Director has disposed of it; the county's document can't reach it here."

"Can't reach it?" Lu Shouchong gave a cold laugh. "Shaofu, I'll remember those words for you." He flung his sleeve, took two steps toward the middle of the courtyard, and turned round, facing the commandant and the whole courtyard of people. "The Shaofu perhaps doesn't know yet: this stele has been named in the palace. The Alchemist Zhao said in the Three Halls, before the Sage himself: there is a *hu* stele in Chang'an on which it is written that Lord Lao went west and the central lands had no Way—an insult to our Sage Ancestor. The Sage heard it and said nothing. The Sage saying nothing means he has made a note of it. If this stele isn't raised today, and tomorrow the palace asks where the stele is, how does the Shaofu mean to answer? That the *hu* monks buried it and the county watched and didn't dig?"

The commandant's face changed. He was a county commandant; of the affairs above he understood nothing, but the words *Alchemist Zhao* he had heard—all Chang'an had heard them. He stood a while, coughed once, and said, "Since the Daoist puts it so… then dig it open and have a look. Once it's been looked at, the county will have something to answer with."

Jingfu's hands clenched in his sleeves. Salang stepped forward a pace and struck the ground once with the foot of his staff. Joseph and Elijah, in the gallery, were saying something low in Syriac. Liu'er shrank behind He Siniang, and Ayi held Liu'er's hand, held it tight.

"Director," said the commandant, turning to Jingfu, "where in the monastery is the stele buried?"

Jingfu stood before him, and those behind could all hear his voice. "This I won't say. The Shaofu asks where the stele is, and I've said: buried. The Shaofu asks where it's buried, and I've said: in the monastery. If the Shaofu asks further under which piece of ground, I won't say. We of the monastery don't lie, but neither do we say everything. If the Shaofu wants to take me, I'll go with the Shaofu."

"If you won't say, I'll find it myself," said Lu Shouchong.

He paced back and forth at the edge of the empty ground, a step and a look at the earth, a step and another look. The loose earth of the courtyard had been soaked once by the rain and dried to a skin, the same colour everywhere. He went round twice, stopped, and pointed at the ground, explaining it to the commandant as if expounding a scripture. "Shaofu, look. The stele weighs thousands of *jin*; these few couldn't carry it far. To carry it to the back courtyard or out of the ward gate would leave traces; they could only bury it close by. The stele stood here, facing the monastery gate. To have the characters face up, it could only be let fall backward; let fall forward, the characters would be face down. Fallen backward, where would the head land?" He walked a few steps north from the empty ground, behind where the stele had stood, and tapped the ground with the toe of his shoe. "Right here. Ten feet or so long, three or four feet wide, right under my feet."

The ground he tapped was the very place where the stele was buried.

Jingfu's face went white at once. Lu Shouchong was not looking at the ground; he was looking at the people. Salang's knuckles stood out on his staff; the younger Shi brother's mouth hung open, and Rijin had gripped his arm; Joseph in the gallery had stopped talking, and Elijah's lips were still moving, making no sound. Lu Shouchong looked them over one by one, and the corners of his mouth lifted.

Lu Shouchong turned and called to the temple's men: "Picks in."

Two of the temple's men came over with picks on their shoulders, stood on the ground he had tapped, and raised their picks.

"The Daoist judges well."

The voice came from under the jujube tree—not loud, slow. Lingbao sat on that low stool, his two hands resting on his knees, his face toward the front courtyard. He could not see, but there was no haste at all in his face. "The Daoist judges well; then dig there. Dig slowly, no hurry; the ground's been rammed, it's hard. Siniang, it's hot; pour two bowls of water for the Daoist and the Shaofu."

He Siniang stood stock still a moment, then turned and went to the stove to ladle water.

Everyone in the courtyard looked at Lingbao. The two men with raised picks stopped too, and looked back at Lu Shouchong.

"We keep to the law," said Lingbao. "The Commissioner's dispatch told the Director to dispose of it, and the Director has disposed of it. If the county wants to dig, we won't stop it, wouldn't dare to; a royal command is a royal command. Only there's one thing I must trouble the Shaofu with." He turned his face toward where the commandant stood. "Tomorrow, when the inspectorate comes to inventory, there'll be a pit in the courtyard, and Assistant Yang will ask who ordered it dug. The Shaofu has brought his register; I trouble him to write it down clearly today: which piece of ground, at what hour the picks went in, how deep they dug."

He Siniang came out carrying two bowls of water. The commandant did not take one, and Lu Shouchong did not take one.

When the commandant heard the words *Assistant Yang*, his eyelid jumped. He knew what office the Commissioner of Merit's was: it was a eunuchs' office, and the assistants of the inspectorate were mostly men from eunuch households. The Alchemist Zhao had a voice in the palace, and the eunuchs had a voice in the palace too; in affairs between those two houses, the one in the middle who got the beating was always the county. He took the register from the county clerk's hands, and handed it back again, and said, "Daoist, the county has come to watch. Whether to dig, the Daoist decides himself. If he digs, the register will record the Daoist's name."

Lu Shouchong stood on the ground he had tapped, and said nothing.

He looked at the blind old *hu* monk under the jujube tree. Just now, when he tapped the ground, he had seen the face of every man in the courtyard one by one; the stele was nine chances in ten under his feet. But once it was dug up—then what? It was a thing the Commissioner's dispatch left to the Director's disposal. If he raised it and hauled it off with his name in the county register, and tomorrow the inspectorate came to inventory and Assistant Yang asked about it, would the Alchemist Zhao offend the eunuchs' office over a *hu* stele? Those words of the Alchemist Zhao's he himself had only heard secondhand from the abbot. He thought too of that Assistant Yang—an old eunuch past sixty, they said, who had not said a word for anyone in forty or fifty years, and yet had deferred this *hu* monastery three days. What tie lay behind it, he had not been able to find out.

He stood a good while, and sweat came out on his forehead. The two men with raised picks held them up all the while until their arms ached, not daring to lower them, watching him.

"Put them down," said Lu Shouchong at last. He came down off that ground, and passing in front of Lingbao he stopped a moment and said, "Old monk, you people of this monastery don't lie with your mouths, but you've plenty of cunning in your hearts."

Lingbao said, "Daoist, we haven't much cunning. We just don't lie. If the Daoist doesn't believe it, there's still time to dig now."

Lu Shouchong did not answer him. He waved his hand at the temple's men and went out of the gate. His men followed him driving the empty cart, the oxen's hooves striking the mud of the ward street, beat by beat. The stack of rollers piled at the foot of the wall they did not take; it stayed where it was.

The commandant had a county clerk take out the register and a brush, and said, "Record: *One stone stele of the Daqin Monastery, Yining Ward, disposed of by the Director Jingfu according to the Commissioner of Merit's dispatch: buried.*" The clerk recorded it, and he said, "Add three words after: *as judged fit.*" The clerk wrote: *buried, as judged fit*. The commandant made his mark, put away the register, and left too. At the gate he turned back and cupped his hands toward the jujube tree.

As soon as the gate was shut, Jingfu's legs gave way, and only by holding a gallery pillar did he stay standing. Only now did he feel that the clothes on his back were soaked through, clinging to him, cold. He went under the jujube tree, crouched before Lingbao, and his voice was hoarse. "Uncle Lingbao, weren't you afraid he'd really dig?"

"Afraid," said Lingbao. "My hands are still shaking. Feel them." He held out his hand to Jingfu, and Jingfu took it. The hand was cold and shaking badly. "But of what I said, not one sentence was false. The ground he tapped himself, the water Siniang poured, Assistant Yang's mark is on the dispatch—you can feel it. If he had really dug, that would have been the Lord allowing it, and tomorrow we'd have stood the morning all the same."

Jingfu held that hand, and for a long while could not speak.

At dusk he stood alone in the middle of the courtyard looking at the empty ground. The earth on it had dried another layer, the same as everywhere else. He stood a while, went to the gate, and shut the monastery gate.

Note: *The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom* went into the ground around the time of the Huichang persecution and came out of it around the third or fifth year of Tianqi in the Ming (1623 or 1625) west of the city of Chang'an (accounts of the place and year differ); when unearthed its characters were whole. Under the stele today is a tortoise base; whether it is the original base is not known. The shaft with its dragon head is about 279 cm tall and is now in the Beilin Museum in Xi'an. *Shaofu* was the Tang term of address for a county commandant. Zhao Guizhen's favour with Wuzong: see the note to Chapter 69; the Commissioners of Merit of the Left and Right Streets were mostly palace eunuchs. The Alchemist Zhao's words in the hall are as Lu Shouchong claims them, the novel's invention. Uighur script derives from Sogdian, which with Syriac derives from the Aramaic alphabet. The burial of the stele as told here is the novel's invention.
