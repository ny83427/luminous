# Volume Eight　The Daqin Monastery

## Chapter 51　Yisi

> Among the pure and upright tarsa, such goodness was never heard of; among the white-robed Luminous scholars, now we see the man.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

On the third of the tenth month of the fourth year of Jianzhong, Chang'an fell into disorder once more. The troops of Jingyuan, ordered east to attack Huaixi, marched through the rain to outside the walls of Chang'an; the court rewarded the army, and what was sent out was coarse rice and vegetables; the soldiers held their bowls and cursed, and cursing, rebelled; they fought their way into the city from the east and plundered all the way to the palace gates. The Son of Heaven, with the crown prince, the princes, consorts and princesses, and a hundred or so eunuchs, went out by the north gate of the park and fled west to Fengtian. The rioting troops bore Zhu Ci into the palace—a man who held the empty title of Grand Commandant and sat idle at home—and within a few days Zhu Ci was seated in the Xuanzheng Hall, calling himself emperor; the name of his dynasty was Qin, and he changed the era to Yingtian. The news reached the Yining Ward when the ward headman knocked in the middle of the night to tell it; when he had told it he stood at the monastery gate, looked up once at the characters over the gate—Daqin Monastery—and having looked, went off shaking his head. That night Samaan entered a line in his account book: The false dynasty is named Qin. When he had written it he put down his brush, took it up again, and added two characters beside it: the same name.

From that day the monastery gate was shut, shut by day and shut by night. Rioting soldiers came twice. The first time seven or eight came and battered the gate with their sword hilts, demanding money and rice. Yisi opened the gate and stood in the gateway, and behind him stood Sargis's son and the nine guard brothers left, holding wooden staves shoulder-high, iron-shod at both ends; Yisi himself held nothing. He was fifty-eight that year, his hair all white, his back still straight, the scar over his left brow plain to see in the lamplight. He said a few words to the soldiers; what, the monastery's people afterwards each told differently: some said he said the monastery's rice was only enough for gruel; some said he said: I know your commander; Sargis's son said he said nothing at all, only stood there looking at them as if looking at a road. The soldiers looked at him, looked at the staves behind him, looked at the stele in the front courtyard, and left. The second time more came, and this time Yisi did not open the gate; the soldiers cursed outside half the night, threw a bucket of dung over the gate, and left too. The lamp on the stone block by the gate could not be set outside in those days; Jingjing lit it every evening at dark, carried it inside the gate, and set it under the crack of the door, so that a thread of light leaked out through the crack, and those outside, not taking notice, could not see it.

With the gate shut, the monastery's people went about their work as usual. Gruel was boiled as usual, once a day and thin, and passed out by the side gate to the old and weak of the ward. The wounded grew more day by day: people of the ward, cut down by the rioting soldiers, trampled by plunderers, carried to the monastery gate; and rioting soldiers too, who had fought with people in the streets and taken a blade, carried on comrades' shoulders, knocking at the side gate, saying they'd heard there was a *hu* monk here who could treat wounds. Samaan stood inside the side gate and blocked it, saying the monastery's medicines belonged to the common folk of the ward, bought with money saved at the edges of the gruel kitchen, and were not for rebel soldiers. Yisi came up from behind and looked at the man outside the gate: a young man of Jingyuan, twenty or so, a gash in his thigh, the flesh turned back, a whole trouser leg run with blood, and the soldier who carried him wore a face of fear too. Yisi said: carry him in. Samaan said: Superintendent, he's a rebel. Yisi said he knew; he said he knew wounds: below the walls of Changshan, by the ditch at the Xiangji Temple, in the tent at Mingsha, how many wounds he had seen; a wound looks the same on whoever's body it grows; he could only treat wounds; he could not tell which side a wound was on; that distinction was the government's business; when the government came back, let the government make it. He had Jingjing write down: what day, what sort of man taken in, wounded where, when he left. Samaan folded his hands back into his sleeves and made an entry in his account book too; what he wrote was: The Superintendent took in and treated one rebel soldier; Samaan held it improper.

In the tenth month word came through the city that Zhu Ci had summoned the former ministers left in the city to the palace to deliberate, and Duan Xiushi went too; Duan Xiushi was an old general who had been Military Governor of Jingyuan and had served many years at court with Lord Guo; in the hall he snatched the ivory tablet from Yuan Xiu's hand, struck Zhu Ci on the forehead with it, drew blood, and cursed him for a mad rebel, and was killed on the spot by Zhu Ci's men. This Hou Siyuan came on his crutch from the Qinren Ward to tell; he said the old soldiers of the city of Chang'an, hearing it, all shut their doors and drank wine, and when they had drunk, wept. That night Yisi lit a lamp in the hall and recited a passage of scripture for Duan Xiushi; Duan Xiushi was not of the faith; he recited it all the same; and when he had recited it sat before the lamp till the second watch.

In the eleventh month the false court's people came to the door. The one who came was an official in a new scarlet robe with four soldiers, saying he was from the new court's Court of State Ceremonial, a card held in his hands. He stood before the hall, all smiles, and said: the new dynasty is named Qin, and your monastery is named Daqin; this is the will of heaven; the emperor has heard of it and is greatly pleased; he wishes to invite the monastery's Superintendent, the Great Virtue Yi, into the palace and confer an office on him; the Great Virtue Yi is an old general of Shuofang, a man of Lord Guo's command; of the Shuofang troops and *hu* troops outside the city, many know the Great Virtue Yi; if the Great Virtue Yi comes into the palace and writes a few letters out, it will be a great service; and the monastery must also present a memorial of congratulation, congratulating the new dynasty on heaven's favour. Those few days Yisi's right shoulder hurt badly: the sword wound left on the river beach by the Mianman hurt whenever it rained, and there was much rain that winter; he could not raise the arm, and even his robe Jingjing had to help him into. He sat on a folding chair in the hall, his right arm in a sling across his chest, heard the official out, and said: this humble monk is ill; his arm can't be raised; he can't write letters, nor walk either; those old Shuofang soldiers who know this humble monk are all old now and sit at home; one letter from this humble monk wouldn't stir one soldier; this humble monk is a monk who can treat a few kinds of wounds; once in the palace he'd only lie there. The official said: plenty of the former ministers in the city are ill; the Chief Minister of the Court of Judicial Review Jiang Yan said he was ill too, refused food, got out of the city, and was he not chased and brought back? The office was conferred on him all the same; however ill the Great Virtue Yi is, he can be carried in. Yisi said: then carry me. The official's face darkened; he put the card down on the table, said: the card stays; let the Great Virtue Yi think it over well; and left with his soldiers.

As soon as the official had gone, Jingjing took up the card, opened it, and copied it entire from beginning to end, and when it was copied had Samaan record it plainly in the account book: On such a day at such an hour, so-and-so of the false Court of State Ceremonial came bearing a card summoning the Superintendent; the Superintendent declined on grounds of illness and did not accept; the card returned with its seal unbroken. The next day he had Sargis's son go with him and delivered the original card to the gate of the false Court of State Ceremonial, handed it in and left; and when he came back he had the ward headman make his mark below Samaan's line. Yisi asked him what he had copied it for; Jingjing said: this business, some day it will have to be told to people, and on that day empty words won't be believed. Yisi heard it, nodded, and went back to the side courtyard.

That official did not come again. At the end of the eleventh month word came in from outside the city that Li Huaiguang, Military Governor of Shuofang, had brought troops back from Hebei and defeated Zhu Ci's troops at Liquan; Zhu Ci had lifted the siege of Fengtian and fallen back into the city; Hun Zhen was at Fengtian too, had held the city more than a month, the men on the walls so hungry they ate horse fodder, and Hun Zhen covered with wounds and still on the walls. When Yisi heard Hun Zhen's name he thought of Hun Shizhi going down in the gateway at Lingzhou, thought of Hun Zhen, when he had read that letter, shooting sixty arrows; and that night he knelt in the hall till the lamp oil dried before he rose. But when spring opened, in the second month of the first year of Xingyuan, word came in again that Li Huaiguang had rebelled: that he hated Lu Qi, hated the Son of Heaven for not seeing him, held his troops at Xianyang and did not move, corresponded secretly with Zhu Ci; and the Son of Heaven had left Fengtian again and gone south to Liangzhou. The Shuofang army had rebelled. Jingjing went into the ward to hear the news, and coming back went first to find his Pa; his Pa was weighing medicines at the medicine chest; he stood there and told what he had heard. The medicine steelyard in Yisi's hand stopped, the weight still swinging; he waited till the weight was still, and went on weighing. On his head there still hung a title, Assistant Vice-Military Governor of Shuofang, petitioned for by Lord Guo in those days, when Lord Guo was Military Governor of Shuofang; that was Lord Guo's Shuofang; now the Military Governor of Shuofang was Li Huaiguang, and Li Huaiguang had rebelled, and this title had become overnight a title in Li Huaiguang's command. He wrapped the medicine, handed it to the one waiting, and took up the next prescription.

At the end of the fifth month Li Sheng's troops fought their way in from the northeast of the city, and Zhu Ci fled northwest and was killed on the road by his own men. Li Sheng's troops were strictly disciplined; the order was given: entering the city, no one to go in at anyone's door, no one to take so much as a blade of grass; whoever did, to be beheaded. The monastery gate opened. Yisi took a broom and scraped off the dung half a year dry on the gate, drew several buckets of well water and sluiced it three times, and when it was sluiced Jingjing carried out that lamp and set it back on the stone block to the left of the gate. In the seventh month, the Son of Heaven came back from Liangzhou.

With the Son of Heaven back, the city began investigating people: investigating who had held false office, who had presented memorials to the false court, whose threshold the false court's men had crossed. At the beginning of the eighth month a dispatch came from the Censorate, ordering Yisi, Superintendent of the Daqin monastery, to come to the Censorate to be questioned. The dispatch listed three points: one, the monastery's name was the same as the false dynasty's, and men of the false Court of State Ceremonial had gone in and out of the monastery; two, the monastery had taken in and treated rebel soldiers; three, the Superintendent Yisi held the title of Assistant Vice-Military Governor of Shuofang, the Military Governor of Shuofang Li Huaiguang had plotted rebellion, and Yisi's dealings with the old Shuofang command were unclear. Samaan read it, folded the dispatch, and pressed it under his account book. The charity and the errands to the government Yisi had handed to Jingjing nearly twenty years before; this time Jingjing went with him too, carrying three things: the copy of the false card, Samaan's account book, and a rubbing of the stele.

The one who questioned them was a young investigating censor, a little over thirty, white-faced, who asked very closely. Yisi answered as it was: the false court's men had come; he had declined and not gone; rebel soldiers he had treated—treated seven; common folk of the ward he had treated, a hundred and more; in the days Li Sheng's troops entered the city the monastery had treated government troops too, ten-odd; as for Shuofang, while Lord Guo lived he had followed him; after he came back to the monastery in the first year of Yongtai he had never again gone in at a Shuofang camp gate; Li Huaiguang he had never once set eyes on. The censor asked on what grounds. He said on the grounds that he was a monk, and a monk does not lie. The censor gave a laugh and said: of all those in the city who held false office, every one who comes into the Censorate says he hasn't lied. Jingjing stepped forward then and handed up the copy of the false card, and opened Samaan's account book and pointed to that line, the ward headman's mark below it, and said the original card had been returned that same day to the false Court of State Ceremonial; the files of the false Court of State Ceremonial Minister Li had sealed when he entered the city; the Censorate had only to look. He unrolled the rubbing too and pointed to the inscription beneath the cross on the stele's head, and said the monastery's name, Daqin, was changed by the edict of the Emperor Xuanzong in the fourth year of Tianbao, and the Bureau of Sacrifices had the record; this stele was set up in the first month of the second year of Jianzhong, and Zhu Ci took his title in the tenth month of the fourth year of Jianzhong; what the stele wrote reign by reign was Taizong, Gaozong, Xuanzong, Suzong, Daizong, the present emperor, and not one character of the false court; the wounded taken in, the monastery had recorded entry by entry: rebel soldiers seven, government troops ten-odd, common folk of the ward a hundred and more. The censor took the rubbing and looked at it from the stele's head all the way to its foot, then looked at the account book, and when he came to Samaan's "Samaan held it improper," raised his head and looked at Samaan once. He said these things he would copy and report, and told them to go back and wait.

On the way back to the monastery Yisi walked very slowly. Jingjing thought it was his shoulder hurting and reached out to support him; Yisi would not let him. At the ward gate of the Yining Ward he stopped and said to Jingjing: today in the Censorate he could only tell his own affairs: he was a monk; he had not gone; he had not lied; of all he said, that censor did not believe one sentence; what the censor believed was those few sheets of paper in your hands. He said all his life he had followed Lord Guo and seen the road: the road on the battlefield, the road in the fog, the road at the bottom of the ditch, he could see clearly; the road at court, from that year he said a few words for Lord Guo in the hall until today, he had never once seen clearly; when Lord Guo told him to go back to the monastery he went back to the monastery; when Lord Guo did not call him he did not go; when Lord Guo had him go out by the back door he went out by the back door; he never knew how those roads turned. He said that night in the Qinren Ward, eating cold gruel, Lord Guo told him: in the court, to say a word for me is sometimes to harm me; it took him twenty years of hearing it before he understood it; you never heard those words, and yet you know it all. He said he had made up his mind: he would go back and have someone write a memorial and give up all these titles—Grand Master of Splendid Happiness with Gold and Purple, Assistant Vice-Military Governor of Shuofang, acting Director of the Palace Administration—give them all back to the court, and be a monk, boil gruel at the monastery and treat the sick; those two things were all he knew how to do.

Back at the monastery, he said this again in the small council room. The elders would none of them agree. Samaan was the first to say no; he said in these years the monastery had stood half by the titles on the Superintendent's head: when men of the Bureau of Sacrifices saw the Superintendent, they saw a Grand Master of Splendid Happiness with Gold and Purple; when a censor saw the Superintendent, he saw an old follower of Lord Guo's command; give up the titles, and the Superintendent was only a *hu* monk; the next time someone came to investigate, what would the monastery have to hold them off with? Marya said the Patriarchate too would ask: the titles were given by the Son of Heaven; to give them up yourself, the Patriarchate would think Chang'an had done something wrong. Ishaq said the Son of Heaven had only just come back and was investigating people; to resign office at such a time, people would think there was something on your conscience. Jingjing too said he should not resign; he said Shuofang had changed, but the title was still the title; the title was written on the stele; written on the stele, and given up, that passage on the stele would become empty. Yisi sat by the door and heard them out one by one, and said he knew these titles were of use; but on his head hung the name of Shuofang, and Shuofang had rebelled, and he could not sleep at night.

Two days later Official Pei came. He was sixty-six that year, his legs bad; he came in a sedan chair; Jingjing had gone to ask him. He heard Jingjing tell the matter, then heard Yisi, and when they had done, stroked his beard and laughed, and said the Great Virtue Yi all his life at court had this one fault: too honest. He said this memorial should be presented, but not as the Great Virtue Yi meant it; as the Great Virtue Yi meant it, it was a true resignation, and a true resignation the court might well grant; this memorial must be written so that it is hard to grant. He said: this is called advancing by retreating; the Great Virtue Yi has seen troops retreat; a good retreat matters more than an advance. Yisi said he could not write such a memorial; Official Pei said the Great Virtue Yi need not be able to; have your son write it.

The memorial Jingjing wrote. He wrote it in the scripture room three nights: the first night he wrote the memorial; the second night a supplementary statement, setting out in it item by item the copy of the false card, the day the card was returned, the ward headman's mark, the number of wounded the monastery had treated; the third night he put the two together, made a fair copy, and took it to Official Pei to see. When Official Pei came to the line in the statement about treating seven rebel soldiers, he put a vermilion dot beside it and said: this item could be left out; to write it is to draw trouble onto yourself. Jingjing said this item had to be written; the Censorate's files already had it; if the court heard it first from someone else's mouth, it would be a crime; if the monastery wrote it itself, it would be fact; when Pa said in the Censorate that he had treated rebel soldiers, the censor believed nothing else, but this sentence he believed. Official Pei looked at him, put down the vermilion brush, and said: your Pa doesn't lie, and nor do you; then write it in. The memorial said:

> Your servant Yisi states: Your servant was by origin a monk of the Western Regions, with a rough knowledge of medicine. At the end of Tianbao he followed Guo Ziyi in Shuofang; the Emperor Suzong permitted your servant to go with him on campaign and granted your servant the purple robe; the Emperor Daizong further added the gold and purple; all at Guo Ziyi's petition, not by any merit of your servant's; the favour received exceeds his portion, and day and night he is not at ease. Now Guo Ziyi has passed away and Shuofang has had a change; your servant's name is still in the register as Assistant Vice-Governor of Shuofang, and every time he thinks of it, sweat runs down to his heels. The monastery where your servant dwells has since Zhenguan received the grace of six sage emperors; the stele stands before the monastery for all the realm to see. When the false envoy came to the monastery, your servant declined on grounds of illness and returned the card to his gate; the wounded treated in the monastery—government troops, common folk, rebel soldiers—each are given by number in a separate statement for Your Majesty's hearing. Prostrate, your servant begs Your Majesty to release him from what has been conferred on him and permit him to return to the monastery, to treat the sick and give out gruel to the end of his remaining years. With utmost earnestness, he respectfully presents this memorial of resignation for Your Majesty's hearing. Your servant Yisi, in sincere fear and trembling, knocks his head, knocks his head.

When Jingjing had written it he took it to the side courtyard and read it to his Pa line by line in the country speech of Balkh. Yisi listened, and in several places had him stop and read again; when he had heard it, he said: of what's in here, half is what he wanted to say, and half he'd never have thought of; the half he'd never have thought of is probably what the court wants to hear; you write it; what you've written is more than he wanted to say, and more right than what he wanted to say.

Jingjing delivered the memorial himself in mid-eighth month. He waited most of a day outside the Secretariat, and a drafter of the Secretariat came out, forty or so, holding the memorial and the statement, leafed through them once before his eyes, and asked him one question: your monastery is named Daqin, the same as the rebel's dynasty—why haven't you changed it? Jingjing said the monastery's name was changed by the Emperor Xuanzong's edict in the fourth year of Tianbao; a name granted by edict the monastery dared not change of itself; what the rebel stole was the Son of Heaven's character; what the monastery kept was the Son of Heaven's edict; the one who stole the character has run; the one who kept the edict is still here. The drafter glanced at him, put the memorial into his sleeve, and sent him back. At the beginning of the ninth month a palace commissioner came and proclaimed before the hall the imperial rescript: The memorial is read and understood. You are an old follower of Shuofang, long known for loyalty and diligence; when trapped among the rebels you kept your integrity unmoved; you should remain in your posts; the request is not granted. Yisi heard it kneeling and knocked his head on the ground. Jingjing, kneeling behind him, heard the words "kept your integrity unmoved," and thought of the line he had written in the memorial: returned the card to his gate. From the Censorate no one came again. Samaan put the copy of the rescript in the scripture cupboard and entered a line in his account book: Memorial presented; not granted; titles as before. When he had written it, he smiled, a rare thing, and said to Jingjing: this time the account he wrote with his heart in it.

---

That autumn many in the city were sick. After the better part of a year of war, the dead in and outside the city had been buried shallow, and when autumn came and the rain fell, fevers broke out, first round the West Market, then in the Yining and Jude wards too. Every day early Yisi went out with his medicine bag on his back: to the gruel kitchen in the west of the city; to the place outside the Kaiyuan Gate by that row of wooden stakes where Narsai gathered the dead; to the households of the ward that kept their doors shut and dared not come out; felt pulses, poured medicine, taught people to boil well water before drinking it, taught people to burn the clothes of the dead. Jingjing urged him to go out less; he said this was what he knew how to do; with the titles still hanging on him, he had at least to do one thing he knew how. Shiyiniang prepared him a handkerchief soaked in vinegar to cover his mouth and nose when he went into the sick rooms; he used it two days, found it stifling, and took it off again.

At the end of the ninth month he himself fell into a fever. First his right shoulder: the sword wound hurt so that he could not raise the arm; then his waist: the old wound left by that arrow at the Xiangji Temple, healed in a hard knot, swelled up now, red and hot, so that pressing it once brought a cold sweat; and after that the fever rose, and once risen did not fall. Shopkeeper Wang's son came to see him and wrote a prescription; the medicine went down, the fever broke for two days and rose again. Yisi lay in that room in the side courtyard; on the wall hung that small round shield, and under the shield two wooden pegs, one hung with the curved sword, one with the short mace; they had hung there these years and no one had touched them. Shiyiniang kept watch by day; at night Jingjing moved over and spread a felt on the floor beside his bed to sleep; when Yisi coughed once he woke; when Yisi turned over he woke too. Yisi told him to go back and sleep; he said he would, and still slept on the floor. Lingbao was seventeen that year; by day he copied scripture in the scripture room in his place, copied a page and ran over to look once, looked once and ran back.

One night at the beginning of the tenth month, Yisi woke and called Jingjing. Jingjing got up and lit the lamp; Yisi lay with his face burning red, but his eyes were bright; he had Jingjing sit closer and said in the country speech of Balkh: "There's one thing I've never told anyone in all my life. Your uncle knew; your grandfather knew; they're both gone; it's time you knew."

"The year I was seventeen, I fought someone in the market at Balkh: the son of an official's household of the Tazi. He cursed me with something—what, I can't remember now. I threw a punch at him; he went over backward; the back of his head struck the stone edge of a step, and he didn't get up. When the officials came to ask who had struck him, I said it wasn't me. That was the first lie of my life, and the last. I stood there and watched them go to ask others; the people of the market looked at me and at each other; and I thought in my heart: no one saw; if no one saw, there's nothing. The next day your uncle went to the officials and said it was he who struck him. He was twenty-five that year, already a deacon; your grandfather was old; the monastery at Balkh was about to be handed to him. The family paid the blood price; your grandmother's dowry was all sold; your uncle was flogged at the gate of the officials and carried home. His taking up his father's office was over from that day; within a few months he was sent off to the Patriarchate. The night I left, your grandfather called me into the room, took this cross off his neck and hung it round mine, said not a word, pressed his hand on my head, pressed it till the lamp-wick sputtered once, and sent me off with the caravan."

He rested a few breaths and said again: "The stele writes of me, among the white-robed Luminous scholars, now we see the man. You say that's praise, and you're right; Tang steles are all written so. What I couldn't get past was this. All my life I've killed men, told one lie, and could treat a few kinds of wounds. Lord Guo told me to see the road, and I saw it. From now on, all these things are yours."

When he had said it he lay with his eyes shut, breathing hard, and when his breath was even had Jingjing go to the cupboard and bring that cloth bundle. Jingjing went and brought it: it was the bronze basin, a dent in its rim, wrapped in cloth. Yisi said: this is Lord Guo's; it goes with him, put it beside him; Lord Guo sent it back to him saying returned to its owner; Lord Guo didn't say who the owner was; he'd thought about it three years and not worked it out; he'd take it along and think about it on the road. Jingjing wrapped the basin and set it by his pillow. Yisi looked at the bundle as if at ease, closed his eyes, opened them again, and asked: is the road still open? Those words Jingjing had heard his uncle ask at Ctesiphon the year he was ten, and later, the night he came back from the Patriarchate to Chang'an, under that lamp at the monastery gate, his Pa too had asked him once in the country speech of Balkh, and that time he answered: it's open. This time he said: "It's open. I've travelled it once now." Yisi looked at him, the corner of his mouth moved, the scar on his left brow puckered with it, and he said: good.

That night he did not open his mouth again. Toward dawn Jingjing heard his breaths grow longer and longer, slower and slower; a breath drawn in, and Jingjing counted in his heart to ten-odd before it went out. Jingjing knelt by the bed holding his hand; Shiyiniang knelt at the other end of the bed; Lingbao stood at the door. The last breath went out and was not drawn in again. Jingjing held that hand; it was very big; it used to be able to cover the whole back of his head. The window paper was turning white. Jingjing stood up, went out of the side courtyard, across the front courtyard, past the stele, to the monastery gate; the lamp on the stone block to the left of the gate was still burning, had burned all night, its oil nearly dry. To put out the lamp at daybreak had been his Pa's first task every morning these years. Jingjing bent down and blew the lamp out.

---

The laying out was done by Narsai. He was past seventy that year, his back bent, his brow-bones still as high; he had pushed the death-cart eight years, and that row of stakes outside the Kaiyuan Gate had already run to a third row. That morning he came and stood at the door of the side courtyard; Jingjing saw him and stood aside. He drew a basin of warm water, undid Yisi's clothes piece by piece, took a cloth, dipped it in the water, and began to wash him from the head. He came to the left brow: a scar on the brow, its skin puckered like a centipede: the third arrow in the fog at the Xiangji Temple, which Yisi pulled out himself, and himself tore off the flap of skin hanging over his eye. He came to the right shoulder: on the shoulder a sword scar, long and deep, left on the river beach by the Mianman before Changshan; to the left ribs: under the ribs a slash laid open, from that time too. He came to the left arm: on the left arm an old sword cut, taken in the winter of the fourteenth year of Tianbao on the bank of the Yellow River saving the caravan. He turned Yisi over and washed the back: on the back a scar, where an arrow had slipped in through the gap between the lower rim of the shield and the leather armour; on the waist another, on the left, the swelling not yet gone down: from the first time he went east to scout at the Xiangji Temple and came back with an arrow in his back, and would not get off his horse. Narsai washed them one by one, his hands very steady, as steady as when he used to trim pens. When he had done he put the cloth in the basin; the water in the basin was cloudy; he carried it into the courtyard and threw it out at the foot of the wall, came back, and dressed Yisi in a clean white robe. Narsai, carrying the empty basin, passed Jingjing, went to the courtyard gate, paused, did not turn his head, and said: "That year, he said, so be it. He was right." And having said it, went off pushing his cart.

---

The funeral was on the third day. More came that day than on the day the stele was set up. All the monastery's people came: Azarel, past eighty, supported by two monks, stood at the very front, Marya and Samaan on either side of him, Babai, Sabrisho, Ishaq, Gabriel and Haddad behind; of the seven who had come east from Ctesiphon in those days, these were what was left, all white-headed, some on sticks. Shiyiniang with Lingbao stood by the coffin; Shiyiniang did not weep; Lingbao wept, and Shiyiniang held his shoulder with her hand. The people of the ward came—those who came every day for gruel at the gruel kitchen, the vinegar-seller, the shoe-seller, those who kept sesame-cake ovens—crowding at the courtyard gate; Shopkeeper Wang's son from the West Market came, bringing the medicine steelyard with which his father in those days had weighed out smoked plum for Yisi, saying his father had charged him on his deathbed: the day the Great Virtue Yi goes, put this beside him; Jingjing took it, and did not put it in the coffin, but set it on the medicine chest. Xu Jing came, past seventy; coming in, he said not a word; the monastery's people all looked at him once; they had known him thirty years, and it was the first time they had seen him come in without saying anything. Hou Siyuan came on his crutch, a dozen-odd old soldiers behind him, some lame, some missing an arm, all of the Shuofang army in those days, standing by the courtyard wall. Official Pei came in his sedan chair, got down, and stood beside the old soldiers. No one came from the Guo family.

The coffin rested in the front courtyard, before the stele. Jingjing stood at the head of the coffin facing the people; he was the one presiding at the funeral; Azarel had said that morning, it's yours; he did not decline. He first recited scripture in Syriac; when it was recited he looked at the courtyard full of people: those in front understood Syriac, those behind did not; he looked at those behind, looked at those crowded at the courtyard gate, and spoke in Tang. To speak Tang at the table had been his Pa's rule. He spoke not fast, like his Pa, every word clearly.

He said his Pa was born in the city of Balkh, a very far place, farther than Xizhou, farther than the Pamirs; his grandfather was called Milis, and was a priest there; his Pa came to Chang'an with a caravan at seventeen, and this year it was forty-two years. He said in the winter of the fourteenth year of Tianbao, at a small town on the Yellow River, a caravan of *hu* merchants was raided by Tongluo horsemen, and his Pa alone, with one shield and one mace, held the gap in the ring of carts, knocked down five, and killed not one; that day a child was lying under a cart, and his Pa told him to count the horses; once he'd counted them clearly, he wouldn't be afraid; that child was he. He said afterwards his Pa followed Lord Guo; Lord Guo said, I don't want you to kill men, I want you to see the road; and his Pa went to see the road: from Shuofang to Hebei, from Hebei to Fengxiang, from Fengxiang to Chang'an. He said there was a man named Du De, who joined the army at sixteen, changed his Pa's dressings twenty days at Changshan, and later, when they pulled out of Hebei, held the rear at the head of a bridge, said he'd hold it as long as it takes to drink a cup of tea, and held it as long as it takes to drink a cup of tea; when he died the hilt of his sword was glued to his hand by blood and could not be prised loose, and he was buried with the sword; the bowl he drank wine from had a chip in its rim, and in the bowl was a pinch of earth from his grave; it was in the monastery's cupboard now, set with his bowl. He said there was a man named Old Duan, a sword scar across his face, who when he smiled moved only half his face; he took a ten-year-old child across the Pamirs to Ctesiphon, and brought that child's diary back and put it into his Pa's hands; afterwards at Xiangzhou, turning his head to curse the wind, he was hit by a stray arrow, and before he died asked his Pa a question, and his Pa had no time to answer. By the courtyard wall, Hou Siyuan bowed his head. He said in the ninth month of the second year of Zhide, at the Xiangji Temple, in a great fog, his Pa took two men, Shi Six and Wei the Third, to scout the ditches to the east; Shi Six died at the second ditch; his Pa sent Wei the Third back first to report and stayed to go on looking himself, took an arrow in the back and one in the waist; Wei the Third led General Pugu and the Uighurs back and died at the sluice gate of the third ditch; his Pa went on into the fog with them, took an arrow in the face, pulled the arrow out himself, tore off the flap of skin hanging over his eye, and said: the road's open. He said the people of the city remembered the rice of that day west of the Qujiang Pool, remembered that the year the Tibetan soldiers came the monastery gate stood open, remembered the hundred and more wounded the monastery took in these months. He said his Pa spoke little; of the things his Pa said, those he remembered were not many.

Here he paused, and said that when he was small in Lingzhou, once at a meal, in front of Uncle Duan, he broke wind, and went red, and said: Pa, how shameful. Pa said: what shames is the lie, not the wind.

Someone at the back laughed first, and quickly covered his mouth; then others laughed, very lightly, holding it down; and laughing, some wept. Xu Jing stood in the crowd, his mouth open, the tears running down line after line, unable to say a word. Jingjing stood at the head of the coffin and neither laughed nor wept. What lay behind those words he had learned only three nights ago; of the whole courtyard of people, he alone knew it, and he did not say it.

When the body was placed in the coffin, Jingjing put that bronze basin in, set by his Pa's right hand, and took down from the wall that short mace, its head the size of a fist, iron, cut with fine grooves, and set it by his Pa's left hand. The round shield and the curved sword he kept, and hung them back on the wall. Eight men bore the coffin: priests of the monastery, guard brothers, and two old soldiers, who walked over by themselves from the courtyard wall; no one called them, and they asked no one; they put their shoulders under the poles. Sargis's son was at the very front; he was too tall; the pole on his shoulder rode higher than the others, and the whole coffin tilted backward; carrying it out of the monastery gate he ducked his head, and when he reached the locust tree suddenly began to weep, weeping like a child, weeping as he walked, and his feet did not miss a step.

The burial ground was west of the city, outside the Kaiyuan Gate, where the monastery's people were all buried; Yonan was there, A-Lian beside Yonan, a foot of ground between. That day the sky was overcast, but it did not rain; the earth was yellow, and soft. Narsai did not come up to the grave; he stood some dozens of paces off, beside his rows of wooden stakes, holding his cart. When the earth was filled in, people dispersed slowly; the old soldiers left, the people of the ward left, Official Pei got into his sedan chair and left; Xu Jing, as he left, turned his head and at last opened his mouth and said one thing: he still owes me a bowl of mutton soup; and when he had said it, did not himself know what he meant by it, and went. In the end only a few of the monastery's people were left. Azarel, supported by a monk, came over to Jingjing, looked at that heap of new earth, had the monk let go, steadied himself, and took from his sleeve a ring of keys: the storeroom's, the scripture cupboard's, the common fund's money chest's; Yisi had been Superintendent twenty years, and this ring of keys had hung at his waist all along; a few days ago Shiyiniang untied it and gave it to Azarel. Azarel put the keys in Jingjing's hand and said: from today, the Superintendent's affairs are yours too. Jingjing took the keys and gripped them in his hand; the keys were cold.

When Jingjing got back to the monastery it was nearly dark. He went first to the side courtyard; the room was empty; Shiyiniang had taken away the bedding; on the wall hung that small round shield and that curved sword, and of the two wooden pegs below the shield, one was empty. He opened the cupboard; in the cupboard were stacked two bowls: one his, a little nick knocked in its rim; one Du De's, a chip in its rim, a pinch of earth in it. He went back to the table and took up his Pa's bowl, the bowl his Pa had eaten from for decades, its bottom worn white; he put it in the cupboard beside the other two, and shut the cupboard door. Then he went out of the side courtyard, across the front courtyard, past the stele, to the monastery gate. The houses of the ward were lighting their lamps one by one. He took up the lamp on the stone block, poured out the dregs of last night's oil, wiped it clean with a cloth, ladled in a spoonful of fresh oil, trimmed the wick, cut it even, struck the flint, and lit the lamp.

Note: The mutiny of the Jingyuan troops in the tenth month of the fourth year of Jianzhong, Dezong's flight to Fengtian, Zhu Ci's proclaiming himself emperor with the dynastic name Qin and the era Yingtian (changed the next first month to the dynastic name Han); Duan Xiushi's striking Zhu Ci with his tablet and being killed; Jiang Yan being forced to accept false office and refusing food on the plea of illness; Li Huaiguang's relief of the siege of Fengtian and his rebellion in the second month of the first year of Xingyuan, and Dezong's second flight to Liangzhou; Li Sheng's recovery of the capital in the fifth month and Dezong's return in the seventh—all follow both Tang histories and the *Zizhi tongjian*. The year of Yisi's death is not recorded in history; this chapter places it at the beginning of winter in the first year of Xingyuan; the false court's summoning of Yisi, the Censorate's questioning, and his memorial of resignation with the rescript refusing it are the novel's invention.
