# Afterword

Sometime around 2003 I played a role-playing game, *Xuan-Yuan Sword III: The Other Side of the Clouds and Mountains*, made by Softstar in Taiwan. In the game I played a Frankish knight named Septem, who travels east all the way from Europe—across the Mediterranean, through Damascus and Baghdad, over the Tianshan mountains—until he reaches Chang'an.

Back then I had a habit with games like that. Whenever I entered a city, I would click on every person in the street, whoever they were, and hear what they had to say. The vegetable seller, the fortune-teller, the soldier on patrol, the old man staring into space on his doorstep: I left out not one. In the market streets of Chang'an there was a man in a cloak and a long gown, standing in the crowd, and you could not tell what he did. I clicked on him, and only then learned that he was a missionary.

If I remember right, the first thing out of his mouth was Revelation, chapter eighteen, verse two:

> Babylon the great is fallen, is fallen, and is become the habitation of devils, and the hold of every foul spirit, and a cage of every unclean and hateful bird.

A missionary from the west, standing in the city of Chang'an in the Great Tang, opens his mouth before a street full of people and proclaims the fall of a great city. As greetings to Chang'an go, it was not exactly polite. I sat at my computer, stopped short, and that scene stayed with me. It was the first time I had seen, anywhere in popular culture, a missionary standing in the streets of Chang'an—only, when he opened his mouth, what he spoke was judgement. I had hoped he would say a little more. Sadly, the writers had given him just that one line, and however many times I clicked on him, he would only ever recite Revelation 18:2.

Only later did I slowly notice the other side of that game. The years it covers run from the Battle of Talas to the An Lushan rebellion, and its Europe is painted very dark: heretics burned, wars fought, everyone afraid, and those in power wanting only the secret of never losing a war; so Septem is sent to the East to look for the so-called Kingly Way. The light is in the East; the West has nothing but darkness.

The phrase "the Dark Ages" only appeared after the Renaissance, and in the Enlightenment it was taken up and made much of by men of letters who bore the Church ill will. Most historians of the Middle Ages today no longer see it that way. Those thousand years had the books copied out in monasteries, had universities, hospitals and cathedrals, and many people quietly doing good. To call that millennium one unbroken darkness is a myth.

What is more, in the very years when Septem walks into Chang'an, there really were Christians from the west in the city. They were far more real, and far gentler, than that cloaked figure in the game who opens his mouth only to pronounce judgement. They came from the land of the two rivers, read their scriptures in Syriac, and had a monastery in the Yining Ward of Chang'an, with a lamp lit at its gate. Their teaching was called *Jingjiao*, the Luminous Teaching. The *Shuowen* dictionary says, "*jing* means light"; and the stele, explaining the name, says that the true and constant Way is wondrous and hard to name, its works shine so plainly that we are forced to call it the Luminous Teaching. More than twelve hundred years ago this band of people looked for a name for their faith that the Tang could understand, and after choosing and choosing, they chose this word. One of them, a man called Yisi, was in those very years fighting beside Guo Ziyi on both banks of the Yellow River.

The light is not only in the East.

---

I first truly wanted to write this novel in 2006; this year it has been twenty years.

It began with a few passages in the sources. One said that in the ninth year of Zhenguan a cleric named Alopen came to Chang'an bringing scriptures, and Taizong sent his chief minister Fang Xuanling with an honour guard to the western outskirts to meet him. One said that during the An Lushan rebellion a Luminous monk named Yisi followed Guo Ziyi to put down the rebels, won merit, received office, and was granted a purple kasaya besides. And one said that centuries later the Luminous Teaching spread widely on the Mongolian steppe, and that Sorghaghtani, the wife of Tolui, was a Luminous believer.

When I read that last passage I sat stunned for a good while.

I am one of the generation born in the eighties who grew up on *The Legend of the Condor Heroes*. "Grew up on" is generous; we read it in secret. In my high-school years the teachers did not allow martial-arts novels, and any they caught were confiscated. So we found a way: we wrapped Jin Yong's books in brown-paper covers and wrote on them, neat and upright, three large characters: *Das Kapital*. They went from hand to hand between classes and nobody gave the game away. If you wanted to borrow one, you leaned over and asked quietly, "Have you finished with your *Das Kapital*? Lend it to me for a couple of days," and the other understood at once and fished it out of his desk for you. In those years our class's zeal for reading *Das Kapital* was probably the strongest in the school.

So when I read about Sorghaghtani, the first thought that sprang into my head was: so in the household of Guo Jing's sworn brother there was a Luminous believer. And push it back five hundred years, and at the side of the Great Tang's Lord Guo there was a Luminous believer too, serving as his claws and teeth, his eyes and ears. One named Guo, and the other named Guo as well—the thought of it was delightful.

I wanted to write it then. I wanted to write how this band of people walked all the way from the land of the two rivers to Chang'an; how they survived in an empire utterly strange to them; how they translated their faith into words the Tang could understand; and how, at a single edict, they packed their things and took to the road again.

Wanting was one thing; I never wrote it. The reasons were not complicated: no time, no energy, and not the ability either. A long novel is not a blog post; there is too much to look up. What was the Tang official system like? How high an office, really, was a vice-commissioner of a military circuit? From what hour to what hour was the battle at Xiangji Temple fought? What were the names of the patriarchs of the Church of the East? How many days was it from Chang'an to Baghdad? Each question was enough to keep me searching for weeks. Searching and searching, I grew weary, and the idea was put down. And there it lay for twenty years.

---

This year I handed the outline and materials I had saved up over the years to Claude, and it quickly wrote the first draft of this book.

To be honest, I spent almost no effort on the writing, but a good deal on the reading. When I had read it, I said where it needed changing, and it changed it. Which sources could be trusted, where invention was allowed, how each character should live and how each should die—those decisions I still made myself. A tool is a tool. A thing that had lain for twenty years was at last done with the help of this tool, and I feel nothing but gratitude.

---

In writing this novel I had a few hopes.

The first was that readers would know that in the Tang, more than a thousand years ago, there really was a band of Christians. Tang officials called them *hu* monks; their teaching counted in Chang'an as merely one of the "three foreign teachings." They flourished—"the Law flowed through the ten circuits, monasteries filled a hundred cities"; they also had their quarrels and their scandals; and in the end, in the Huichang years, they were torn down along with the Buddhist monasteries, their people sent back, and that stele later buried in the earth. Many Christians do not know this history; I myself learned it only from reading the sources.

These people also carry a misunderstanding on their backs. The churches of the West declared them heretics and called them "Nestorians," and the verdict of the Council of Ephesus more than fifteen hundred years ago remains in many people's minds to this day: mention the Luminous Teaching, and the first thing is a frown. But this old case slowly took a turn. At the end of the nineteenth century a book Nestorius wrote in his old age was found in the East, and many scholars who read it concluded that he himself never said the things later people pinned on him; what he and his rival in Alexandria fought over was in large part wording, not necessarily the faith itself. In 1994 the Pope of Rome and the Patriarch of the Assyrian Church of the East signed a common declaration acknowledging that the two share one faith in Christ, and that the old division arose mostly from language, culture and misunderstanding. In theology today this branch is no longer simply regarded as heretical. Only, most ordinary Christians do not know these things. I do not want to plead for anyone here; right and wrong, in the end, are for the Lord to judge. I only often thought, while writing these people: they came from very far away, spoke of their own faith in other people's words, were misunderstood, and in the midst of misunderstanding passed the lamp on, generation after generation.

The second was to write what faith looks like in ordinary days. The Luminous believers ate, and fought, and had stomach troubles, and fell in love with people they should not have loved. Like us, they were all weak; there is not one saint in this book of the kind found in the lives of the saints. Faith did not make them proof against blades; it only meant that when they fell, they knew which way to fall.

The third is a small private wish of my own. Several scenes of famine relief in the book use the method Timothy Richard used in the Shanxi famine: have the hungry sit down first, in groups of fifty, and only then hand out grain. This was something I noticed years ago reading the scriptures, a hearer finding what he was listening for; later I wrote a blog post about it, and I had always wanted to put it into a story; this time at last I have. As for the old soldiers in the book—Old Duan, Du De, Xu Jing and the rest—the histories have not a word on them; I made them up. But I have always felt that in the camps of the Great Tang there must have been men like them.

---

There is one more passage I must write here.

The characters and stories in this book are, for the great majority, invented. Some episodes—especially where they describe struggles for power within the church, jealousy, suspicion, scandal and division—may strike some readers as familiar, and they may not be able to help wondering: is this about so-and-so, about such-and-such an affair? Has the author projected things around him into the book?

I must say it plainly: no.

To give one example. The book has Yisi's elder brother Shapur, during the struggle for the patriarch's throne at the Patriarchate, attacked by a hired assassin; he takes the blow on his right arm and loses it. Such a thing is all but unimaginable in today's church, but in the Church of the East of that time, in the years when the struggle for the throne was fiercest, it truly happened. The histories record that in that very year a man who wanted to be patriarch bribed the local governor, had his men stand with drawn swords at the doors of the altar, and forced the bishops to consecrate him; afterwards he was driven from the throne, and together with his kinsmen kept on working against the new patriarch. The blow in the book comes from sources like these, and has nothing to do with anyone around me.

Other episodes are mostly the same. Ecclesiastes says there is no new thing under the sun. Struggles for power, jealousy, weakness, betrayal—they were in the Daqin Monastery more than a thousand years ago, and they have been in the church in every age; they are things that are in human nature to begin with, and there is no need to go looking for them in any one particular person.

While I was writing I did think of some people I know well. Certain traits of theirs—a favourite saying, a temper, a way of treating people and doing things—I took apart, scattered, and kneaded into different characters in the book. Sometimes one character carries the shadows of several people; sometimes one person's shadow is spread over several characters, the good and the not so good. But there is not one character in the book drawn from a single real person, nor one event that alludes to a real event.

So I ask readers not to look for themselves in it, and still less to take the book's plot and set it against, or read hidden meanings from it about, anyone around them. If at some place you feel it is speaking of you, that is most likely because we are all the same kind of people.

---

The novel is finished, and looking back, there is still much that falls short. Some historical facts were not checked closely enough, some characters not drawn deeply enough, some chapters still read a little hurried. If readers find mistakes, please do tell me.

But it is like that band of people in Book Three who were sent out of Chang'an. They went out of the city gate, lost their monastery, lost their name-board, lost the court's permission; some went west, some went north. They looked back once at the walls of Chang'an, and then went on their way. They had not lost their Lord.

This novel is the same. It is not perfect, but it has been written.

And that is enough.

Lao Tie  
St. Louis, November 7, 2026
