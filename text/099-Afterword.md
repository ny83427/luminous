# Afterword

Sometime around 2003 I played a role-playing game, *Xuan-Yuan Sword III: The Other Side of the Clouds and Mountains*, made by Softstar in Taiwan.

In the game I played a Frankish knight named Septem, who travels east all the way from Europe—across the Mediterranean, through Damascus and Baghdad, over the Tianshan mountains—until he reaches Chang'an.

Back then I had a habit with games like that. Whenever I entered a city, I would click on every person in the street, whoever they were, and hear what they had to say. The vegetable seller, the fortune-teller, the soldier on patrol, the old man staring into space on his doorstep: I left out not one. In the market streets of Chang'an there was a man in a hooded cloak and a long gown, standing in the crowd, and you could not tell what he did. I clicked on him, and only then learned that he was a missionary.

If I remember right, the first thing out of his mouth was Revelation, chapter eighteen, verse two:

> Babylon the great is fallen, is fallen, and is become the habitation of devils, and the hold of every foul spirit, and a cage of every unclean and hateful bird.

These are words of the Last Judgement. A missionary from the west, standing in the city of Chang'an in the Great Tang, opens his mouth before a street full of people and proclaims the fall of a great city. As greetings to Chang'an go, it was not exactly polite.

I sat at my computer, stopped short. That scene stayed with me. It was the first time I had seen, anywhere in the popular culture of the day, a missionary standing in the streets of Chang'an—only, when he opened his mouth, what he spoke was judgement. I had hoped he would say more. Sadly, the game's writers had given him just that one line, and however many times I clicked on him, he would only ever recite Revelation 18:2.

Only later did I slowly notice the other side of that game. The years it covers run from the Battle of Talas to the An Lushan rebellion. Its Europe is painted very dark: heretics burned, wars fought, everyone afraid, and those in power wanting only the secret of never losing a war. So Septem is sent to the East, to China, to look for the so-called Kingly Way. The light is in the East; the West has nothing but darkness.

The phrase "the Dark Ages" only appeared after the Renaissance, and in the Enlightenment it was taken up and made much of by men of letters who bore the Church ill will. Most historians of the Middle Ages today no longer see it that way. Those thousand years had the books copied out in monasteries, had universities, hospitals, cathedrals, and many people quietly doing good. To call that millennium one unbroken darkness is a myth.

What is more, in the very years when Septem walked into Chang'an, there really were Christians from the west in that city. They were far more real than the hooded shadow in the game who opened his mouth only to pronounce judgement, and far gentler. They came from the land of the two rivers, read their scriptures in Syriac, and had a monastery in the Yining Ward of Chang'an with a lamp lit at its gate. Their teaching was called *jingjiao*. *Jing* means light. One of them, a man called Yisi, was in those very years fighting along both banks of the Yellow River with Guo Ziyi.

The light was not only in the East.

---

I first truly wanted to write this novel in 2006—twenty years ago this year.

It began with a few passages from the sources. One said that in the ninth year of Zhenguan a Syrian cleric called Alopen came to Chang'an with scriptures, and the Emperor Taizong ordered his chief minister Fang Xuanling to take an honour guard to the western outskirts to welcome him. One said that during the An Lushan rebellion a Luminous monk called Yisi followed Guo Ziyi to put down the revolt, won merit, received office, and was even given a purple robe. And one said that several hundred years later the Luminous Teaching was widespread on the Mongolian steppe, and that Tolui's wife Sorghaghtani was one of its believers.

When I read that last passage, I sat stunned for quite a while.

I am a child of the eighties who grew up on *The Legend of the Condor Heroes*. "Grew up on" is the polite version; really we read it in secret. In my high-school years the teachers did not allow us to read martial-arts novels, and if they caught you, the book was confiscated. So we came up with a plan. We wrapped Jin Yong's novels in brown-paper covers, and on each cover wrote three large, neat characters: *Das Kapital*. Between classes they passed from hand to hand, and nobody gave the game away. If you wanted to borrow one, you leaned over and asked quietly, "Done with your *Das Kapital* yet? Lend it to me for a couple of days." Your friend understood at once and fished it out of his desk for you. In those years, I suspect, no class in the school was keener on *Das Kapital* than ours.

So when I read about Sorghaghtani, the first thought that popped into my head was: so in the house of Guo Jing's sworn brother there were Luminous believers. And five hundred years before that, beside the Tang's Lord Guo, Guo Ziyi, there was a Luminous believer too, serving as his claws and teeth, his eyes and ears. One named Guo, and the other named Guo as well. The more I thought about it, the more it delighted me.

I wanted to write it even then. I wanted to write about these people walking all the way from the land of the two rivers to Chang'an; about how they survived in an empire utterly strange to them; about how they translated their faith into words the people of the Tang could understand; and about how, under a single edict, they packed their bundles and took to the road again.

I wanted to; I never did.

The reasons are not complicated: no time, no energy, and not the skill. A long novel is not a blog post; there is too much to look up. What did the Tang official system look like? How senior, exactly, was a deputy military governor? From what hour to what hour was the battle at Xiangji Temple fought? What was the name of the Church of the East's patriarch? How many days did it take to go from Chang'an to Baghdad? Each one of those questions was enough to keep me digging for weeks. Digging and digging, I would tire, and the idea would be put away.

Put away for twenty years.

---

This year I handed the outline and the materials I had gathered long ago to Claude, and it very quickly produced the first draft of this book.

To be honest, I spent almost no effort on the writing. On the reading, though, I spent a good deal. When I had read a part, I said where it needed changing, and it changed it. Which sources could be trusted, where invention was allowed, how a given character ought to live and how he ought to die—those decisions I still made myself.

A tool is a tool, I think. Something put away for twenty years has at last been done with the help of this tool, and I feel nothing but gratitude.

---

In writing this novel I had a few wishes.

The first was for readers to know that in the Tang dynasty, more than a thousand years ago, there really was a band of Christians. The churches of the West declared them heretics; the officials of the Tang called them foreign monks; and in Chang'an their teaching counted only as one of the "three barbarian religions." They had their flourishing—"the Law flowed through the ten circuits, the monasteries filled a hundred cities"—and they had their quarrels and their scandals too. In the end, in the Huichang years, their monasteries were pulled down along with the Buddhist ones, their people were sent back where they came from, and their stele was buried in the earth. Many Christians do not know this history. I did not know it either until I read the sources.

The second was to write what faith looks like on ordinary days. The Luminous believers ate, fought, had upset stomachs, fell in love with people they should not have loved. Like us, they were weak. There is not one saint in this book of the kind you find in the lives of the saints. Their faith did not make them proof against sword and spear; it only meant that when they fell, they knew which way to fall.

The third is a small private wish of my own. Several times in the book there is famine relief, and it follows the method Timothy Richard used in the Shanxi famine: first have the hungry sit down, in groups of fifty, and only then give out the grain. That was something I noticed long ago while reading the scriptures—a listener finding more than the speaker meant—and later I wrote a blog post about it. I had always wanted to put it into a story, and this time, at last, I did. As for the old soldiers in the book—Old Duan, Du De, Xu Jing and the rest—the histories have not one word about them; I made them up. But I have always felt that the army camps of the Great Tang must have had men like that.

---

I want to dedicate this book to one person: Pastor David Jang.

The church I have belonged to since I came to faith was founded by Pastor Jang. All his life he has served the Lord with a burning heart. He is Korean, and he has two sons; when he named them, he put the character *jing* into both their names. He often mentions this when he talks with Chinese believers. As he sees it, *jing* is light, and light is truth.

Later I read in the *Shuowen* dictionary that "*jing* means light." Then I read the stele again, and on the stele, explaining the name of the Luminous Teaching, it says that the true and constant Way is wondrous and hard to name, its works shine so plainly that we are forced to call it the Luminous Teaching. More than twelve hundred years ago, that band of people who came from the west to Chang'an, looking for a name for their faith that the people of the Tang could understand, chose among all the possibilities this one character. When I read that, I thought of the names Pastor Jang gave his children, and of the Korean missionaries Pastor Jang has sent to preach in China.

The church I belong to is regarded by some as heretical. In its day, the Luminous Teaching too was declared heretical by the churches of the West.

That old case has slowly taken a turn. At the end of the nineteenth century a book that Nestorius wrote late in life was found in the East, and many scholars who read it concluded that he himself had never said the things later generations pinned on him; that what he had fought over with his rival in Alexandria was in large part a matter of words, and not necessarily the faith itself. In 1994 the Pope of Rome and the Patriarch of the Assyrian Church of the East signed a common declaration, acknowledging that the two sides confess the same faith in Christ, and that the old division arose largely from language, culture and misunderstanding. In theology today, this branch of the church is no longer simply regarded as heretical.

But most ordinary Christians do not know these things. In many people's minds the Luminous Teaching is still the heresy condemned by an ecumenical council, and at the mention of it they still frown first. A judgement of more than fifteen hundred years ago is still there in many people's impressions today.

I do not mean to argue anyone's case here. Right and wrong I leave, in the end, to the Lord's judgement. I only found, as I wrote about these people of a thousand years ago, that I often thought of ourselves: we too came from far away; we too must tell our faith in other people's words; we too have been misunderstood; and we too, in the midst of misunderstanding, pass the lamp on, one generation after another.

So this book is about the Luminous Teaching, and in some measure it is about us as well. I dedicate it, respectfully, to Pastor David Jang.

---

There is one more thing I must set down here.

The characters and stories in this book are, for the great majority, invented. Some episodes, especially those that touch on power struggles inside the church, on jealousy, suspicion, scandal and schism, may look familiar to some readers, and they may not be able to help wondering: is this about so-and-so, is it about such-and-such an affair, has the author projected things from around him into the book?

I want to be clear: no.

To give one example. In the book, Yisi's brother Shapur, during the struggle at the Patriarchate over the patriarch's seat, is set upon by a hired assassin; he takes a blow on his right arm and loses the arm. A thing like that is simply impossible in a church today—at least in ours. But in the Church of the East of that time, in the years when the fighting over the seat was fiercest, such things really did happen. The chronicles record that in that very year a man who wanted to become patriarch bribed the local governor to have his men stand with drawn swords at the door of the sanctuary and force the bishops to consecrate him; afterwards he was driven from office, and he and his kinsmen went on opposing the new patriarch without rest. That sword-blow in the book comes from sources like these, and has nothing whatever to do with anyone around me.

The other episodes in the book are mostly the same. *Ecclesiastes* says there is no new thing under the sun. Power struggles, jealousy, weakness, betrayal—there were all of these in the Daqin monastery more than a thousand years ago, there have been all of them in the churches of every age. They are things that belong to human nature itself, and there is no need to go looking for them in any one particular person.

When I wrote, I did think of some people I know well. Certain of their traits—a favourite saying, a temper, a way of treating people and doing things—I took apart, scattered, and worked in all sorts of ways into different characters in the book. Sometimes one character carries the shadows of several people; sometimes one person's shadow is spread over several characters. Good traits and bad ones both. But there is not one character in the book drawn after one real person, and not one event that alludes to one real event. In this book there is simply no key by which anyone can be matched up with anyone.

So I ask readers not to try to match the characters to people, and still less to take the plot and use it to compare, to decode, or to gossip about anyone around them. If at some point you feel it is talking about you, that is most likely because we are all the same kind of people.

---

Now that the novel is finished and I look back, there is still much that falls short. Some facts I did not check carefully enough; some characters are not drawn through; some chapters still read a little hurried. If readers find mistakes, please do tell me.

But it is like that band of people at the end of the novel who came out of Chang'an. They were driven out of the city gate, lost their monastery, lost their name-board, lost the court's permission, and walked on west. They looked back once at the walls of Chang'an, and then went on their way. They had not lost their Lord.

This novel is the same. It is not perfect, but it has been written.

And that is enough.

Lao Tie  
St. Louis, November 7, 2026
