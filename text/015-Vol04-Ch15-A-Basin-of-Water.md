# Volume Four　Xiangji Temple

## Chapter 15　A Basin of Water

> The bath of the Law, by water and wind, washes away vain show and makes the heart clean and clear.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

At the travelling court in Fengxiang Guo Ziyi asked to be punished, and was reduced to Left Vice-Director.

When the edict came down, opinion in camp was divided. Some said the seal of Deputy Marshal was still in his hands, and only his title had shrunk; some said from now on he was only a vice-director. The soldiers did not understand much of this.

By then the main army had moved from Wugong to outside the walls of Fengxiang. The day Guo Ziyi came back to camp he rode his dappled grey as steadily as ever. Once in camp he first went to see the wounded, and when he had seen them he took his seat in the tent and went through the rosters the camps had sent in once more, filling up what needed filling, merging what needed merging. His face was just as when he had left; nothing could be read in it.

---

That summer, Guo Ziyi often sent for Yisi at night to come to the command tent.

He sent for him to ask about the west. The court meant to borrow troops from the Arabs, and what Arab troops were like hardly anyone in camp had seen; the only man close at hand whom Guo Ziyi could ask was Yisi. He asked how the Arabs pitched camp, what grass their horses ate, how long their bows were, which passes east of Balkh could not be crossed in winter. Yisi answered each thing, and a clerk beside them wrote each thing down.

When it had been written, Guo Ziyi would sometimes send the clerk back first and sit a while longer himself. One lamp lit in the tent, a bowl of water on the table, Guo Ziyi on the folding stool, Yisi standing. Only when Guo Ziyi told him to sit did he sit down on the mat.

What he asked then were things of no consequence.

He asked what the wells in Balkh were like. He asked how many times a day they chanted the scriptures at that monastery in the Yining Ward of Chang'an. He asked what those few mumbled words before meals were.

Yisi answered each thing.

Once Guo Ziyi asked: "Your scriptures—have they got stories in them?"

Yisi said they had.

"Tell me one."

Yisi thought and told it. He told a story exactly the way he gave a report in the command tent.

"There was a man with two sons. The younger wanted his share. Took the money and left. Went a long way. Spent it all. Kept pigs for someone. Hungry. Wanted to go home. Went home. His father was waiting at the crossroads, saw him, ran to him, held him."

He stopped.

Guo Ziyi waited a while and asked: "Is that it?"

"That's it."

"And the elder one?"

Yisi thought again. "The elder one wasn't pleased."

Guo Ziyi looked at him, looked at him a long time, and said: "You tell a story the way Old Duan tells a joke. Before anyone else has understood it, he's finished."

Yisi said: "There's a man at our monastery who tells them well. He's in the west."

Guo Ziyi nodded, picked up the bowl and drank a mouthful of water. When he had drunk he set the bowl down again and looked at the little water left in it for a while.

"The elder one," he said. "What became of him?"

"The scripture doesn't say."

Guo Ziyi grunted.

Guo Ziyi had many sons. Everyone in the camp knew it.

---

One evening in the seventh month, Guo Ziyi sent for him again.

This time there was no lamp in the tent. Guo Ziyi sat in the dark, and he had sent the guards at the tent entrance off twenty paces, to stand with their backs turned.

Yisi went into the tent and stood at the entrance, and only when his eyes had grown used to the dark did he see Guo Ziyi sitting on the folding stool, his two hands on his knees.

"Sit," said Guo Ziyi.

Yisi sat.

The two of them sat a long time in the dark. Outside the tent insects were chirring, chirring a while, stopping a while. Far off a horse snorted.

"I want to enter your teaching," said Guo Ziyi.

Yisi said nothing.

He sat there and listened to the words slowly settling in the tent, settling to the ground, coming to rest. He was not surprised. There was a place in his heart that seemed to have known long ago that these words would come, only not on which day.

"Why, my lord?" he asked.

Guo Ziyi thought a while.

"The night of the Qing Canal," said Guo Ziyi, "you said he died once. Died on wood, and his men all ran away."

"Yes."

"The day of the Qing Canal, my men ran too," said Guo Ziyi. "I ran too."

Yisi said nothing.

"His men all ran away," said Guo Ziyi, "and to this day there are people who worship him. In Balkh there are, in Chang'an there are, and in this camp of mine there's one."

Outside, the insects began chirring again.

"These things," said Guo Ziyi, "I can't say well. Don't ask any more."

Yisi said: "I won't ask any more."

The two of them sat a while longer.

"There's one more thing," said Guo Ziyi, "that I must ask."

"Ask, my lord."

"If I enter your teaching," said Guo Ziyi, "can my sons and grandsons be kept safe?"

When he said this his voice was not as it had been a moment ago; it was a little lower, and a little quicker, as if he had held the words in his mouth a long time and, taking advantage of the dark, spat them out all at once.

Yisi did not answer at once.

He knew Guo Ziyi had lost a son in the second month. He knew too how many men in camp had lain awake at night since the Qing Canal, how many had slipped off to burn incense at the Buddhist temple outside the city, how many had sent word home telling their families to go and light lamps at a Daoist shrine. And he knew that if he went along with it and said what was wanted, Guo Ziyi could sleep well tonight.

"They can't," said Yisi. "There's nothing like that in the scripture."

In the dark, for a long time Guo Ziyi made no sound.

"Then what do you ask for?"

Yisi thought and said: "Not for that."

The insects outside stopped a moment, and began again.

"I still want to be washed," said Guo Ziyi.

---

"There's one thing," Guo Ziyi said again, "that must be made clear first."

"Say it, my lord."

"This thing—you know it, I know it," said Guo Ziyi. "There's no third man."

Yisi thought and said: "There's one more."

Guo Ziyi's brows drew together at once. "Who?"

Yisi raised his hand and pointed up at the roof of the tent.

Guo Ziyi looked at his finger for a while, and his brows relaxed.

"He won't tell," said Guo Ziyi.

"No."

"Then that's all right."

Guo Ziyi said that he was a great general of the court with tens of thousands of men under him, some *hu*, some Tang. If he entered a *hu* teaching, tomorrow someone at court would say Guo Ziyi was in league with the *hu*. Saying it of him alone wouldn't matter; but the men who followed him, ever after, at court and in the field, would have it held against them. The *hu* would have it said of them, the Tang men too.

"So it can't be told," said Guo Ziyi. "Not while I live, and not when I'm dead."

Yisi said: "Very well."

"You can't write it either."

"Very well."

"Your son," said Guo Ziyi. "He can't be told either."

Yisi paused. "Very well."

---

"What's needed?" asked Guo Ziyi.

"Water," said Yisi.

"What water?"

"Any clean water will do."

Guo Ziyi stood up, felt about in the dark a while, found something and set it on the table. From the sound it made Yisi knew it was bronze.

Guo Ziyi lit a lamp. The wick was turned down very small, the flame no bigger than a bean; it did not light the tent, only the little patch of table.

On the table stood a bronze basin.

It was the kind used in the army, wide-mouthed and shallow, with a dent in the rim, knocked who knew what year or month or where. Inside it had gone black, but outside it shone from being handled. Yisi knew it. Guo Ziyi washed his face in it every morning, and on the march when he was thirsty he drank from it too. From Shuofang to Hebei, from Hebei to Hedong, from Hedong to Fengxiang, it had always hung behind Guo Ziyi's saddle.

"Use this," said Guo Ziyi.

He fetched a waterskin from the corner of the tent, pulled the stopper and poured water into the basin. The sound of the water was loud in the tent, a splashing gush. When it was full he set the waterskin down and sat back on the folding stool.

Yisi looked at that basin of water.

He had baptised in Chang'an. When he baptised, the monastery was full of lamps, the elders stood in a row and chanted, chanted a long time. The one to be baptised wore white, walked in from outside the sanctuary and knelt before the stone font. The font had been brought from the west, and carved with crosses and vines.

Here there was nothing. One small lamp, a bronze basin with a dented rim, an old man in an old robe.

He stood up.

---

"Kneel, my lord," said Yisi.

That year Guo Ziyi was sixty-one. He got down from the folding stool, propped his two hands on his knees, and slowly knelt on the felt. As he knelt, his knees cracked.

Yisi took up the bronze basin and stood before him.

First he recited a passage in Syriac. He recited it very low and very fast, like water running through the cracks in a rock. His father Milis had taught it him, and his father had learned it from his father. When he had finished he stopped and said it again in Tang speech. He said it very slowly, biting each word clear, as if afraid of biting one wrong:

"I baptise you in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Pure Wind."

He tipped the basin.

The water poured down over the crown of Guo Ziyi's head. Once, twice, three times. It ran down his white hair, over his forehead, over his eyebrows, into his beard, and from the tip of his beard dripped drop by drop onto his robe, onto the felt.

Guo Ziyi had his eyes shut and did not move.

Yisi set down the basin and laid his hand on his head. His hair was wet and cold. Yisi's hand lay on it, lay there a while. He murmured a few more words; what they were, even he could not remember afterwards.

Then he took his hand away.

"It's done," he said.

Guo Ziyi opened his eyes. He did not get up at once but stayed kneeling, looking at the little wet patch on the felt. The lamplight shone on the wet, bright.

"That's all?" he asked.

"That's all."

Guo Ziyi thought and said: "It's less than I thought."

Yisi said: "There was never much."

---

As Guo Ziyi got up, Yisi put out a hand to help him. His arm was very heavy and very hard, like Hun Shizhi's arm, like a length of tree trunk.

When he was steady on his feet he did not wipe the water from his face at once, but let it stay on his face, on his beard, dripping drop by drop.

"Should I take a name?" he asked. "A name in your teaching."

"Some take one, some don't."

"I won't," said Guo Ziyi.

"Very well."

Guo Ziyi picked up the bronze basin from the table. There was a little water left in it, a shallow film, not enough to cover the bottom. He looked at it a while, went to the tent entrance, lifted the curtain and threw that little water onto the ground outside. When he had thrown it he dried the inside of the basin with his sleeve, very carefully, once and then again.

Then he hung the bronze basin back on the saddle.

He took a cloth from the rack, dried his hair and beard, changed out of the robe that was wet at the collar, and turned the wet patch of felt over and pressed it under the leg of the table. When it was done he turned the wick up, and the tent was bright, just as on any night when he sat reading documents.

Tomorrow morning, he would wash his face in that basin again.

"Go," said Guo Ziyi.

---

When Yisi came out of the tent, it was full dark.

His right cuff was wet in a ring, splashed when he tipped the basin. He folded the cuff in twice and walked out with his head down, past the guards standing with their backs turned, and a few steps on, suddenly stopped.

Beyond the guards, a few steps further, a man was squatting on the ground.

He squatted by the wheel of a grain cart, his back against it, earth all over him, a felt cap on his head, and the cap the colour of earth too. He seemed to have been squatting there a long time; on the ground by his feet he had drawn a lot of lines with a twig, every which way.

Yisi looked at him a while.

The man stood up and took off the felt cap.

There was a sword scar across his face, drawn slantwise from his left brow bone to the corner of his mouth. He grinned at Yisi, and only half his face grinned.

"Master Yi," said Old Duan.

---

Old Duan had come into camp with the grain carts.

From Xizhou he had come all the way east, past Yizhou, past Guazhou, past Suzhou and Ganzhou, to Liangzhou, just as the grain convoy from Hexi to Fengxiang was setting out. He flashed the travel pass from the Xizhou Area Command at the grain officer, the grain officer looked it over and let him come along. The carts went slowly; he was in no hurry either, and followed all the way, followed them to Fengxiang and into camp.

Once in camp, the first thing he did was go to report to Guo Ziyi at the command tent. Before he reached the tent he was stopped by the guards, who said Lord Guo had given orders, he would see no one. Old Duan knew those guards and the guards knew him, but they stopped him just the same. So he went no further, and squatted by the grain cart to wait.

At night the camp was quiet, and the command tent was not far from the grain carts. Squatting there, he heard water being poured in the tent, and someone reciting something, in *hu* speech, mumble mumble. Then a few words of Tang speech, low; he caught only a word or two.

How long he had waited, he did not say.

Yisi did not ask either.

The two of them stood by the grain cart and looked at each other. Old Duan was thinner and darker, the skin of his face so dry it was peeling, his lips split in several places. His eyes were the same as ever, one open wider, one narrower.

"And Jingjing?" asked Yisi.

"In Xizhou," said Old Duan. "Doing fine. Xu Jing's watching over him. The seven teachers, the twenty-four monk-soldiers, all there."

Yisi nodded. He nodded very slowly, once, and again.

Just then a bodyguard officer came over, gave Old Duan a look, and went into the tent to announce him. Not long after he came out again and said: "Lord Guo says you're to go in."

---

Old Duan went into the tent.

The lamp in the tent was turned up very bright. Guo Ziyi sat on the folding stool in a clean robe, his hair bound up neatly, just as when he took his seat in the tent on any ordinary day. On the table there were only a few rolls of documents and a bowl of water. His saddle hung on the tent pole, and behind the saddle hung the bronze basin.

Old Duan knelt, knocked his head to Guo Ziyi, got up, stood, and began.

He told of the Arabs. Told how the Arab troops wore black and carried black banners, their horses strong, their bows short, their swords curved. Told how they pitched camp and how they set their watches. Told of that ruined palace at Ctesiphon, nothing left but one archway, under which a dozen horses could walk abreast. Told of the church at Balkh, and how the elder Ahad had stayed there and would not come back. Told how the twenty-four monk-soldiers drilled before light, six to a rank, shield against shield, and when they turned it made a ring. Told how they crossed the Pamirs in the first month, the snow up to the horses' bellies, two camels dead, and one monk-soldier who lost two toes to the frost and bit on a stick and never made a sound.

Guo Ziyi listened, now and then asking a question.

He talked for the better part of an hour, and was done.

Guo Ziyi nodded and said: "You've had a hard time. Go and rest."

Old Duan saluted, fist in palm, and turned to go. At the tent entrance Guo Ziyi called him back.

"Old Duan."

Old Duan turned his head.

Guo Ziyi sat under the lamp, looking at him.

"Coming into camp today," said Guo Ziyi, "what did you see?"

Old Duan stood at the tent entrance and thought.

"Grain carts," he said. "From Hexi. Twenty-odd carts. More millet than wheat."

Guo Ziyi looked at him a while.

"Go," said Guo Ziyi.

---

That night Old Duan squeezed into a tent with Yisi.

He pulled an oilcloth packet from inside his coat. The packet had been kept warm against his body for more than two months, warmed until it had gone soft and limp, its edges and corners worn furry. He set it in front of Yisi and opened it, layer by layer.

Inside were two rolls of paper and two single sheets.

"Jingjing wrote them," said Old Duan. "Wrote them all along the way. He says they're for you."

Yisi looked at the two rolls and did not put out his hand at once.

Old Duan said: "And the two sheets. One's the names of the seven teachers, he wrote them in Tang characters, with notes beside them on which Tang words they've learned. One's things the elder Ahad said, about the monastery in Lingzhou. He said it's so you'll know the men are on the road. The elder's words are on it too."

Yisi put out his hand and picked up the two rolls. They were rolled very tight, the outer layer rubbed shiny by the oilcloth. He held them in his hand, held them a while, and did not open them.

Old Duan lay down beside him and put the felt cap over his face.

"Master Yi," he said from under the cap.

"Mm."

"Coming into camp today, I came in with the grain carts," said Old Duan. "I saw grain carts."

Yisi said nothing.

"Anything else, I didn't see," said Old Duan. "Washing or not washing, I don't understand any of that. I'm a soldier; all I know is grain carts."

Yisi turned his head and looked at the felt cap over Old Duan's face. The cap rose and fell, rose and fell.

After a while, snoring came from under the cap.

---

Yisi turned the wick down a little and opened the first roll.

The characters were written crooked and lopsided; where he could not write a character, he drew a circle. The first page said:

"Left Lingzhou. Wind strong. Camels slow. The scar on Uncle Duan's face goes red when the wind blows."

He looked at it a while and glanced at Old Duan beside him. Old Duan had the cap over his face; the scar could not be seen.

He turned on.

"Crossed the Yellow River. Walked on the ice. Uncle Xu says you mustn't stop on the ice, if you stop you go through. I didn't stop."

"Uncle Xu's fingers—he gave them to the beacon fire."

"The elder Ahad says if you hold the scriptures in your arms you're not so afraid."

"Uncle Duan got an arrow. He says it grazed him. I saw the arrowhead was still in. Uncle Xu dug it out. He didn't cry out. I didn't cry out either. Last night I was so scared I nearly wet myself, but I didn't."

At this Yisi paused. He glanced again at the felt cap beside him.

He turned on.

He came to a page with a line of curving letters traced on it, Syriac, traced very clumsily, every stroke separate, as if copied bit by bit from cuts in stone. Two names—his father's, his mother's. Under each of the two names a curved M was traced, traced twice over.

Below there was a line in Tang characters:

"Grandfather's and Grandmother's graves. Behind the church in Balkh. Grandfather's cross and my cross are the same on the back. I came and saw them for Pa."

Yisi looked at that page.

He looked at it a long time. The wick spat, the flame jumped, and he did not move.

Then he turned the page over.

---

The second roll was about the Patriarchate, about Ctesiphon, about his uncle.

"Uncle's eyes are like Pa's."

"Uncle asked: is the road still open? I said: it's open. I've walked it once."

"Every day Uncle ties a knot in his sleeve. He ties it very tight."

"The elder Ahad isn't coming back. He's staying in Balkh to watch over Grandfather and Grandmother for Pa. He gave me a cord. One knot, one prayer."

"The elder Ahad says *wan* for *fan*. I taught him to say *fan*. He says once we get to Xizhou, it'll be just me teaching."

"The elder Gabriel told about Joseph. One of the monk-soldiers cried. I didn't cry. I thought of Pa."

Then came Xizhou. The church, the grass, the dust on the cross. Seven men kneeling in the grass.

The last page was written more neatly than all the rest, as if he had thought a long time before setting down the brush:

"Pa, I've reached Xizhou. Uncle Duan is going back. I've asked him to bring you this. I'm very well. My stomach's well too. I haven't drunk cold water."

Below, after a line's gap, one more sentence had been added, the characters a size smaller:

"Once I did."

---

Yisi opened one of the single sheets.

It was the elder Ahad's words. Jingjing had taken them down sentence by sentence, the characters crammed close together.

"Of the church keys, one is with your pa, one is in the crack in the wall behind the stove, stopped up with a brick."

"The rafter in the northeast corner is rotten, it leaks when it rains, put a basin under it. To mend the roof, don't get that plasterer called Wang from the West Market."

"Auntie Kang, who sells flatbread in the east of the city, sends the church flatbread every Cold Food. Your pa must give her a bolt of cloth back."

"There's an old groom in Mafang Lane; on the first of every month take him his medicine."

Yisi read them down one by one. With each one he remembered something—the key, the rafter, Auntie Kang's flatbread, the mudhole at the mouth of Mafang Lane. He had left the church in Lingzhou more than a year ago, and he himself had all but forgotten these things; the elder had remembered every one.

At the second to last, he stopped.

"The apricot tree in the back courtyard—in spring when it flowers, your pa always stands under it a while. Don't go and call him. Let him stand."

Yisi looked at that line a long time.

The last was not like the ones before; the ink was paler, as if it had been added later:

"His pa's and his ma's graves, I'll watch over them for him. Every seventh day I'll take people to the graves and pray. Tell him not to worry."

Yisi folded this sheet, laid it on his knee and pressed it flat with his palm. He pressed it a while before he opened the other.

On it were seven names, written in Tang characters, one below another, with a few lines of small characters noted under each.

"Narsai. Can write *vow*. *Break* on top, *word* underneath. He writes it very heavy."

"Ishaq. Can write *medicine*, *sick*, *peace*. Says *mo pa* as *mo pa*, grind and crawl, and *fan* as *wan*."

"Babai. Learns fastest. Writes *scripture* best."

"Gabriel. Can write *light*. Also asked how to say *grace*. I don't know."

"Sabrisho. Can write *time*. Can say *won't do*."

"Haddad. Can write *letter*, *road*, *money*. Of all the Tang words, he learned the swearwords fastest. I didn't teach him."

"Yonan. Can write *lotus cross*. *Lotus* is hardest; he wrote it more than twenty times."

Yisi read down them line by line. At "Sabrisho. Can say *won't do*," the corner of his mouth moved. At Haddad's line, the corner of his mouth moved again.

At the last line, he stopped.

Yonan.

He looked at that name a long time. The lamp spat again.

Beside him Old Duan turned over, and the felt cap slid off his face. Yisi reached out, picked it up and put it back over his face.

Then he laid the two rolls and the two single sheets together, wrapped them in the oilcloth and put them by his pillow. He blew out the lamp, lay down, and lay with his eyes open.

Outside the tent the insects were still chirring. Old Duan's snoring rose and fell. Yisi knew Old Duan slept with one eye open.

He reached over and laid his hand on the oilcloth packet.
