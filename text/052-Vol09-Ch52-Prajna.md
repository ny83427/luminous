# Volume Nine　The Translation Hall

## Chapter 52　Prajna

> In the Shengli years the sons of Śākya used their strength and raised their voices in Eastern Zhou; at the end of Xiantian, lesser scholars laughed aloud and slandered us in Western Hao.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

In the spring of the year after Yisi died, Azarel died too. He was past eighty; in the winter he still had two monks support him to the hall to lead worship; after the New Year he could not get up, lay twenty-odd days, and on the night he died sent for Jingjing; Jingjing went; he could no longer speak, and with his finger drew a cross in Jingjing's palm, and when it was drawn his hand fell. Azarel was buried outside the Kaiyuan Gate, beside Yisi. On the day of the burial, coming back, Jingjing did not sleep all night; before light he got up and dressed, did not go to the hall, but walked first into the middle of the courtyard and stood there. The courtyard was dark; a thin frost lay on the well platform; the window paper of the scripture room was not yet lit. He remembered that year at the Patriarchate his uncle standing every morning in the middle of the courtyard tying that sleeve-knot, tying it nine years and not missing one day; his uncle asking whether the arrow in Pa's waist hurt on overcast days, and he saying it did, and his uncle saying: then he's well. He took from inside his robe Ahad's knotted cord, of wool, twisted under that mulberry tree at Balkh, nearly thirty years now, black and shining, and told it knot by knot, at each knot saying one *Lord, have mercy*, and when he came to the end there was only a little grey at the edge of the sky. He went back to the scripture room, lit the lamp, opened that Syriac roll of the psalms, turned at random to a page—"I will lift up mine eyes unto the hills, from whence cometh my help"—read the line several times, shut the book, took from the head of his bed that old board brought back from Xizhou, wrote a character on it in charcoal—*shan*, hill—and sat facing it until the striking of the wood sounded outside; then he put down the charcoal and went to the hall to lead the morning office.

From that day on he did so every day. He rose before the wood was struck, stood a moment in the middle of the courtyard, told the knotted cord through once, went into the scripture room and read a passage of scripture, picked one line from it, turned it over and over in his mind until a character came out of it, and wrote it on the board; when it was written he carried it with him through that day, and the next morning wiped it off and wrote another. Monks of the monastery who rose early would sometimes see the lamp in the scripture room and think someone had forgotten to blow it out, and go over to look, and see the Master sitting under the lamp facing a wooden board, as if talking to someone.

He was forty then, and the monastery's people called him Master. The standing the Patriarch's letter gave him was *papash*, but the laying on of hands had not yet been done; in Samaan's account book it said "the *papash* Adam"; the ring of keys Azarel had put in his hand the day his Pa was buried hung at his waist, and the monastery's affairs great and small all came down in the end on him alone. The monastery's accounts he checked himself once a month; the oil for the scripture room lamps he counted himself once a month; the lamp on the stone block at the gate he went out and lit himself every evening at dark. He was still afraid of cold water; in summer, even with his throat smoking with thirst, he would wait for the well water to be boiled on the stove and cooled to warm before drinking it; the young newcomers of the monastery laughed at him behind his back and said the Master's stomach kept the rules better than the Master; this reached his ears, and he thought about it and decided it was true.

---

In the spring of the second year of Zhenyuan, someone knocked at the monastery gate: a military officer of forty or so, not tall, his face darkened by wind and sun, a sword at his waist, in the robe of the Shence Army. The Shence Army was the Son of Heaven's guard, these years under the command of the palace eunuchs, and its men walked the city of Chang'an with a heavier tread than others; the old believer at the gate saw that robe and his legs went half to water first; he ran in to report and could not get his words out whole. Jingjing went out and saw behind the officer a monk of fifty-odd, not old, yet looking old, very thin, thinner than Jingjing, his skin dark with a red underneath, as if burned by another kind of sun, in a brownish-yellow kasaya, his head bare, his eye sockets very deep, his eyes bright. He stood outside the threshold looking up at the cross chiselled in the lintel, looking very earnestly, as if reading a character. The officer said his name was Luo Haoxin, a field commander of the Shence Army, and this was his cousin, the Tripitaka Master Prajna, from Kashmir in northern India. The monk brought his eyes back from the cross and joined his palms to Jingjing, and Jingjing joined his palms too; he did not know whether this was the right way to return it, but could think of no other.

Luo Haoxin was a forthright man; he came into the courtyard, sat down, drank a mouthful of hot water, and set out the matter. His father was a man of Kashmir who came to Tang land and married, and fathered him; he had grown up in Chang'an from childhood and knew only a few words of Kashmiri, learned as a child from his father; Prajna was his father's sister's son, so that reckoning it out he was the son of Prajna's maternal uncle; Prajna had left home in youth and studied the scriptures in the great monasteries of India for decades, and made a vow to come east; in the second year of Jianzhong he came by sea-going ship to Guangzhou, and the next year entered Chang'an. When Jingjing heard "the second year of Jianzhong," something stirred in his heart: in the first month of that year the stele was set up before the monastery, and when the stele was set up this old monk was being tossed on the waves of the southern sea. Luo Haoxin said that after his cousin entered Chang'an he lived in a Buddhist temple in the city, and no one could talk with him; he knew Sanskrit and several languages of India, and not one word of Tang; at the temple there were some who knew a little Sanskrit, but the scripture his cousin had brought was a *hu* text, not a Sanskrit one, called the *Six Paramitas Sutra*, seven rolls. He said that some days ago he had gone about asking who in the city knew *hu* languages and whose Tang writing was good, and an old officer in his camp, a *hu* too, surnamed Mi, told him: go to the Daqin monastery in the Yining Ward and find Jingjing; the stele is his writing, and he's translated dozens of scriptures too; his Tang writing is better than a *jinshi*'s. Jingjing said what we translate are our scriptures. Luo Haoxin was taken aback, and said: aren't scriptures all scriptures? He put down the water bowl and said he didn't understand scriptures; he was a soldier; his cousin had walked from India to Chang'an, walked several years, for the sake of these seven rolls; he, as cousin, would find someone to translate them, to gain a little merit for him and a little merit for himself; would you be willing?

Jingjing looked at Prajna. Prajna sat with his two hands on his knees, not moving; he could not understand what they were saying, but seemed to know they were speaking of him. He looked at Jingjing, and in his eyes there was neither pleading nor pride. Jingjing remembered many years ago, in that ruined monastery north of the city of Xizhou, the seven teachers sitting before him while an eleven-year-old child taught them to write water, fire, gate, road; when they looked at him it was like this too. He said he would think about it.

That evening some of the elders sat in the scripture room till midnight. Samaan was the first to object; he folded his two hands in his sleeves and said the first article of the order's plan of conduct was not to contend in words with the other religions; translating scripture for the Buddhists was more trouble than contending in words: translate it well, and people will say we're currying favour with the Buddhists; translate it badly, and people will say we've spoiled the Buddhist scriptures; however you translate, you'll get nothing good of it; he asked too whether Jingjing understood Buddhist scriptures; Jingjing said he didn't; Samaan said: then what will you translate? Marya's voice had long gone hoarse, and he said only one thing: he had preached more than twenty years in Chang'an, borrowing the Buddhists' and Daoists' words; for them to borrow ours once is no great loss. The next day Jingjing went to the Guangde Ward to see Official Pei and told him of it. Official Pei was nearly seventy that year, his legs bad; he sat in the gallery in the sun, heard it out, stroked his beard, and said: the Shence Army is the eunuchs' army now; one word from a eunuch counts for more than ten from a chief minister; a field commander of the Shence Army comes in person to your door leading his cousin and asks you courteously; say you won't translate, and the next time he comes to your door it won't necessarily be courteously; he said he wouldn't have approved of this matter, but he was old, and in future the monastery's affairs he could shield you from would be fewer and fewer; you must learn to take them yourself; when people hold out their hand and you draw yours back, they remember it. He said too: translate, you may; but remember one thing: what you don't understand, say you don't understand; don't pretend.

Jingjing went back to the monastery and remembered something his Pa had said: to use another's words is like living in another's house; live there, you may, but live there long and don't forget it's not your own house. He thought that this time it was another who wished to borrow his hand. He told Luo Haoxin he would translate.

---

The translating was done in the monastery's scripture room. Prajna came every morning, and on days Luo Haoxin was not on duty he came too; the three sat round a long table, on it the *hu* text spread open, beside it brushes and ink and a stack of paper. On the very first day they could not get on. The *hu* text was in a kind of *hu* script; Jingjing knew the letters and could spell out the sounds one by one, and of the words he spelled out he understood half and did not understand half, and the half he did not understand was exactly the important words. Prajna understood those words, but Prajna could not say them; he spoke Sanskrit, a long string at a time, very urgently, and Jingjing could not understand, and Luo Haoxin beside them knitted his brows and now and then picked out a word or two heard from his mother in childhood and put them into Tang. So one word passed through three hands: Prajna told Luo Haoxin, Luo Haoxin told Jingjing, and Jingjing guessed against the *hu* text; if he guessed right, Prajna nodded; if wrong, Prajna shook his head; and he shook his head more often than he nodded.

That night Jingjing stayed alone in the scripture room, a whole day's drafts spread on the long table, the paper covered with corrections, and no more than a dozen-odd lines legible. He went to the corner, opened that wooden box, reached to the very bottom, and felt out Alopen's parchment book. In the spring of the first year of Jianzhong when he was writing the stele, he had held it in his hands nearly every night, searching it for years and months, for names, for sentences of edicts, and what the stele had no use for he had turned past page by page. What he was looking for this time was different: he wanted to know how, a hundred and fifty years ago, that man who could not speak one sentence of Tang had translated scripture.

The four mouths in the Library Hall he had known about when writing the stele: Syriac, Persian, Tang, and then the Imperial Library's amanuensis setting it down in literary Chinese. This time, reading page by page closely, he found something he had not noticed before. After Lord Fang came once to the Library Hall to look at the drafts, he laid down a rule, which Alopen recorded very carefully, drawing a circle beside it: when the amanuensis had settled a passage in writing, it was not finished; the interpreter must read it back from the Tang into *hu* speech, and the disciple read it back again from the *hu* into Syriac, and read it sentence by sentence to Alopen, and Alopen would listen and match it against what he had first said; what matched was kept, what did not was circled, and it went through the four mouths again from the beginning. One page of the little book said: "Walking it backward, of ten sentences three were not mine." A few pages on: "Today, of ten sentences two were not mine." A few pages further: "Today only one. Lord Fang says: one is one; slowly. The Tang he speaks I still cannot understand, but when he says slowly, his face means it." When Jingjing had read this he put the book down and thought of that long table by day. Prajna told Luo Haoxin, Luo Haoxin told him, he set it down in Tang: three men, three hands, going all one way forward, and not one going back; Prajna could not read Tang characters, Luo Haoxin did not know scripture; whether what he wrote was right, there was not one man in the world who could walk it backward for him and see. When Prajna nodded, it was because what Luo Haoxin said was right in meaning; whether what Luo Haoxin said was right in meaning, Prajna, hearing a few lines of half-broken Kashmiri, could only guess too. Those dozen-odd lines he had written by day looked like Tang writing, and their sense ran smoothly; but how many sentences in them were Prajna's, he did not know. Alopen in his day had known; Alopen could not read Tang characters, but he knew what he meant to say, and every sentence he pulled back into his own ear to hear once.

He wrapped the book, put it back at the bottom of the box, and blew out the lamp. The next morning what he read was the passage Gabriel had preached in those days at Balkh: the younger son spent all he had, came to himself, and arose and went to his father. He wrote a character on the board: *hui*, return; and when it was written thought: Alopen turned back to listen; the younger son turned back to walk; he himself, sitting at the long table by day, went all one way forward, and never turned back.

---

The three characters *boluomi*, paramita, took three days to translate. Prajna said a great deal; Luo Haoxin listened, nodding and shaking his head, and at last said: he says it's crossing the river, crossing from this side to that. Jingjing asked: across to which side? Luo Haoxin went to ask again; Prajna said still more; Luo Haoxin scratched his head and said: he says over there there's no suffering. Jingjing held the brush suspended over the paper and could not set it down; he thought he understood this, and thought he did not understand it; what he understood was that there was a river, and on the far side of the river a place where, once there, men wept no more; what he did not understand was whether the river Prajna spoke of was the river he knew. Afterwards an old believer of the monastery who had once believed in the Buddha told him the Buddhist scriptures already had a ready-made rendering: *dao bi'an*, to reach the other shore. So he wrote *to reach the other shore*. The characters were good characters; when he had written them he put a small dot beside them with his brush: his own mark, meaning here he had borrowed from another.

Of the six paramitas, there was one he translated most smoothly. That day they came to "forbearance," *renru*, and Prajna said a great string; Luo Haoxin could not make it out; Prajna grew agitated, stood up and gestured, put out one hand and made as if to strike his own face once, turned the other cheek and made the gesture again, then joined his palms and stood still. Luo Haoxin said: it means being struck and not striking back. The hand with which Jingjing held the brush stopped; he thought of his Pa on the bank of the Yellow River, one man with one shield and one mace, knocking down five and killing not one; thought of Yonan slipping on the wet stone by the well, Narsai going to catch him with the knife still in his hand, and Yonan on the ground telling Narsai: it was you who caught me; he thought of that line in the scripture: whosoever shall smite thee on thy right cheek, turn to him the other also. He bent his head and wrote; that roll he translated very fast, translated in a day what other days took three; Luo Haoxin, watching him write, said: this time you don't need to ask. Jingjing said: this we have too. Luo Haoxin told Prajna, and Prajna looked at Jingjing and smiled; when he smiled all the wrinkles of his face crowded toward his eyes, like a sheet of paper crumpled and spread out again.

Later in the translating the two of them spoke of other things too. Prajna asked: what is your Buddha called? Jingjing said we have no Buddha; we have Meshiha; he came from heaven, was born among men, was nailed to wood by men, died, and after three days lived again. Luo Haoxin translated it, haltingly; Prajna heard it, nodded, and said a long string; Luo Haoxin said: he says the Buddha too had many lives, and in one he gave his body to feed a starving tigress. Jingjing said: it's not the same. Luo Haoxin opened his mouth and shut it again, looked at Jingjing, looked at Prajna, and said: this one I won't translate; you gesture it out yourselves. The two of them looked at each other: one an old monk come from India, one a *hu* monk born in Chang'an, one who could not speak Tang, one who could not speak Sanskrit, sitting at the two ends of the long table with a *hu* text between them that neither could read entire; each bent his head and went on translating the next line. Prajna ate one meal a day, only once before noon, and very little; Jingjing's stomach was bad, and he could not eat much at a meal either; the woman who cooked brought two bowls of gruel, and the two of them each drank only half a bowl, and pushed the rest aside to cool; Luo Haoxin looked at one and then the other, and drank both half bowls. A few days later Prajna brought a small packet of dried fruit, yellowish brown, shrivelled like an old man's fingers, pressed it on Jingjing, pointed to his own belly, then to Jingjing's. Luo Haoxin said it was *helile*, myrobalan, an Indian medicine; he says your stomach is bad. Jingjing did not know how he had seen it, and took it; that packet of myrobalan lasted him more than a year.

---

The seven rolls took more than seven months to translate. On the day they were finished Luo Haoxin carried the drafts home, made a fair copy, and presented it with a memorial; the memorial Luo Haoxin had someone write; what it said Jingjing did not know; Luo Haoxin said the Sage One believed in the Buddha and would be pleased when he saw it. Then there was waiting: a winter and a spring, and a summer and an autumn. On the day of the first snow of the first month of winter in the third year of Zhenyuan, Luo Haoxin came alone, without Prajna, stood in the snow and would not come into the house, took a paper from his coat and handed it to Jingjing, his face as if he had eaten something bad, and said: it's copied out; read it yourself. The paper did not have many characters on it: it called him the Persian monk Jingjing of the Daqin monastery; said Prajna was not versed in the *hu* language and did not understand Tang speech, and Jingjing did not know Sanskrit and was not versed in the Buddhist teaching; though they called it translation, they had not obtained half a pearl; they sought to steal an empty name, not to bring blessing; said the Sage One had examined what they translated, and its reasoning was obscure and its words loose; said further that the Buddhists' monasteries and the Daqin monks' temples were different dwellings, and their ways of practice wholly at variance; Jingjing should spread the teaching of Mishishi, and the Buddhist monks expound the Buddhist scriptures, so that the teachings be kept apart and men not wander between them; right and wrong are different, as the clear Jing and the muddy Wei flow apart. Jingjing read it through, and read it again from the beginning, and at the sentence "right and wrong are different" stopped; snow fell on the paper and melted into little drops, blurring a few characters; he dabbed them lightly with his sleeve, and read aloud once: reasoning obscure, words loose. Luo Haoxin rubbed his hands and said it was his fault; he shouldn't have come to you; and said the Sage One had ordered his cousin to translate it again, together with several Great Virtues of the Ximing Temple. Jingjing said the Sage One was right; he didn't understand the Buddha. Luo Haoxin seemed not to have expected him to say that; he sighed, made a salute, and left.

Samaan heard of it and came to the door of the scripture room and stopped. Jingjing thought he would say: I told you so; Samaan did not say it, but asked for the paper and read it once, gave it back to Jingjing, and said: keep it; it's an official document; it'll be of use some day. Jingjing said: this is a scolding of us. Samaan said: a scolding of us is a document too. Jingjing took the paper again to the Guangde Ward; Official Pei was lying in bed; he had himself propped up, held the paper far off, peered at it under the lamp, and when he had read it said: look at the last sentence. Jingjing said he had looked: Jingjing should spread the teaching of Mishishi. Official Pei said: in this paper the words scolding you say ten things, and even use the word *wrong*, and the words allowing you are only one sentence; but that sentence is the Sage One's mind; from now on whoever says you shouldn't spread your teaching in Chang'an, copy this sentence out for him to see. When he had said it he lay down again, and said he couldn't get up these days; this winter, he feared, would be hard to get through.

That night when Jingjing got back to the monastery the snow was heavier, and the lamp on the stone block at the gate was not yet lit. He went first into the scripture room, folded that paper, opened Alopen's book, and turned to the latter part: the page of the winter of the tenth year of Zhenguan, when the Son of Heaven summoned Alopen into the forbidden palace. On this page the Syriac was scrawled; Elder Babai had read it twenty years and made out half; the other half, when writing the stele, he had not had time to make out closely; this time he made it out letter by letter. Only now did he learn that those four characters on the stele, "questioned on the Way within the forbidden gates," had really happened. The first thing the Son of Heaven asked was: of the Heavenly Worthy, the sovereign, and one's parents, which comes first? Alopen answered: first revere the Heavenly Worthy, next revere the sovereign, next revere one's parents; the Heavenly Worthy teaches men to revere the sovereign, and teaches them to revere their parents too; the three do not contradict one another; the Sogdian interpreter opened his mouth and could not make a sound, Alopen wrote: "his face was white; only when Lord Fang looked at him did he say it." The second thing the Son of Heaven asked was why the monks shaved the crowns of their heads and yet kept their beards; Alopen answered: keeping the beard is to say that outwardly we still do men's work as ordinary men do; shaving the crown is to say that inwardly we keep no private desire. Jingjing knew this sentence; he had carved it on the stele: "they keep the beard because they have outward conduct; they shave the crown because they have no inward passion"; he had taken it then only for an old rule handed down in the monastery, and had never thought that its source was a weather-beaten *hu* monk kneeling in the forbidden palace, answering through four mouths a Son of Heaven's question. Last the Son of Heaven asked: you came from so far; on the road men died and beasts died; having come here, what do you want? Alopen answered: on the road, when men were thirsty, we gave them water; when they were cold, we gave them clothes; having come here, we would do the same. Coming out of the palace gate, Lord Fang called him back and said one thing, and this time the interpreter passed it on at once: "The Son of Heaven looks at the man."

Jingjing read the page once more. He thought: Alopen's scripture was mistranslated—the Lord's name written as "shifting rat," the Heavenly Worthy borrowed from the Daoists, the page full of others' words—and the Emperor Taizong read it and permitted him to build a monastery in the Yining Ward, and he himself on the stele wrote for Taizong "deeply knowing it to be right and true, he gave special command for it to be spread"; these seven rolls of his were smooth in every word, even *to reach the other shore* being the Buddhists' own ready phrase, and the present emperor read them and said the reasoning was obscure and the words loose. He copied the sentence "Jingjing should spread the teaching of Mishishi" alone onto a small slip of paper, and as he copied it wrote the character *shi*, corpse, as *shi*, to bestow—Mishi*he*, Meshiha, as the stele had it—and pressed it under the inkstone, a corner showing, ground his ink, and went on translating his own scriptures. He translated till it was full dark before he remembered the lamp at the gate, put down his brush, took up the flint, and went out.

What came after Jingjing heard of by degrees. Prajna translated that scripture again with the monks of the Ximing Temple, translated it into ten rolls, and the title grew longer too: the *Mahayana Principle-Purport Six Paramitas Sutra*; it was said to be very well translated, and the Sage One himself wrote the preface; and a few years later he heard Prajna had been granted the purple robe. When Jingjing heard it he was grinding ink in the scripture room; he stopped, and thought of his Pa's purple robe, folded square after his Pa died and put away at the bottom of the chest, which he took out once a year to air. He thought: that old monk too came from very far away; and went on grinding ink.

---

Note: Prajna was a native of Kapisa in northern India (Yuanzhao says "to call it Kashmir is a careless abbreviation"); he reached Guangzhou in the second year of Jianzhong and Chang'an in the third; in the second year of Zhenyuan, with "the Persian monk Jingjing of the Daqin monastery," he translated from a *hu* text the *Six Paramitas Sutra* in seven rolls; "Prajna was not versed in the *hu* language and did not understand Tang speech; Jingjing did not know Sanskrit and was not versed in the Buddhist teaching; though they called it translation, they had not obtained half a pearl; they sought to steal an empty name, not to bring blessing"; Dezong "examined what they translated, and its reasoning was obscure and its words loose"; "Jingjing should spread the teaching of Mishishi, and the Buddhist monks expound the Buddhist scriptures, so that the teachings be kept apart and men not wander between them; right and wrong are different, as the clear Jing and the muddy Wei flow apart"; afterwards Prajna, with Yuanzhao and others of the Ximing Temple, retranslated it in ten rolls—all in Yuanzhao's *Zhenyuan xinding shijiao mulu* (quotations from the common transcription, to be checked against the Taishō original). Luo Haoxin, field commander of the Shence Army, was the son of Prajna's maternal uncle. Alopen's notebook, the method of "walking it backward," and the questions and answers in the forbidden palace in the tenth year of Zhenguan are the novel's invention; "they keep the beard because they have outward conduct; they shave the crown because they have no inward passion" is from the stele.
