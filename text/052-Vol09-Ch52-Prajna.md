# Volume Nine　The Translation Hall

## Chapter 52　Prajna

> In the Shengli years the sons of Śākya used their strength and raised their voices in Eastern Zhou; at the end of Xiantian, lesser scholars laughed aloud and slandered us in Western Hao.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

One morning early in the spring of the first year of Zhenyuan, before it was light, Jingjing got up and dressed and, instead of going to the hall, went first into the middle of the courtyard and stood still.

The courtyard was dark. A thin frost lay on the well curb; the paper of the scripture room's windows was not yet light. The day before, the monastery had buried Azarel outside the Kaiyuan Gate, beside Yisi. After the New Year the old man could not get up, lay twenty-odd days, and on the night he died sent for Jingjing. When Jingjing got there he could no longer speak; he drew a cross with his finger in Jingjing's palm, and when it was drawn, his hand dropped. That night Jingjing did not sleep. Lying there, he remembered how at the Patriarchate his uncle had stood every morning in the middle of the courtyard tying that sleeve knot, nine years, not missing one day. He took from his breast Ahad's string of woollen knots. He had been telling it since under that mulberry tree in Balkh, nearly thirty years now, and it was black and shining. He told it knot by knot, one knot, one *Lord, have mercy*, and when he reached the end there was only a little grey at the edge of the sky.

He went back to the scripture room, lit the lamp, opened the Syriac roll of the psalms, and turned at random. It was *I will lift up mine eyes unto the hills, from whence cometh my help*. He read the line several times, closed the book, took from the head of his bed the old wooden board he had brought back from Xizhou, wrote on it with charcoal the character *shan*, hill, and sat facing it until the wooden clapper sounded outside, and only then laid down the charcoal and went to the hall to lead the morning office.

From that day on he did this every day: rose before the clapper, stood a while in the middle of the courtyard, told the string of knots once, went into the scripture room and read a passage, picked out a line, and turned it over and over until a character came of it; this he wrote on the board and took with him through the day, and the next morning wiped it off and wrote another. Monks who rose early sometimes saw the lamp in the scripture room and thought someone had forgotten to blow it out; they went over to look and saw the Master sitting under the lamp facing a wooden board, as if talking to someone.

He was forty that year, and the monastery called him Master. The standing the patriarch's letter gave him was Father of the Monks, though the laying on of hands had not yet been done. The bunch of keys Azarel had handed him the day Pa was buried hung at his waist, and every matter in the monastery, large and small, came down in the end on him alone. He was still afraid of cold water: in summer, his throat smoking with thirst, he would still wait until the well water had boiled on the stove and cooled to warm before he drank it. The young monks who had come lately laughed behind his back that the Master's stomach kept the rules better than the Master did. When this reached his ears he thought about it and decided they were right.

---

In the spring of the second year of Zhenyuan someone knocked at the monastery gate. The old believer who kept the gate came running in to report, so flustered he could not get the words out: "Master—outside—outside it's men of the Shence Army, with swords!"

The Shence Army was the Son of Heaven's own guard, and these few years it was under the palace eunuchs; its men walked a little heavier than anyone else in the streets of Chang'an. Jingjing went out. An officer was standing at the gate, forty or so, not tall, his face darkened by wind and sun, a sword at his waist. Behind the officer stood a Buddhist monk, fifty or so, very thin, thinner than Jingjing, his skin dark with red under it, as if burned by some other sun, in a tawny-yellow *kasaya*, his eyes deep-set but bright. He stood outside the threshold with his face tilted up, looking at the cross cut into the lintel, looking very attentively, as if reading a character.

The officer saluted, fist in palm, in a ringing voice: "Don't be alarmed, Your Reverence. I haven't come to arrest anyone; I've come to ask a favour. My name is Luo Haoxin; I'm a commander in the Shence Army. This is my cousin, the Tripitaka Master Prajna, from Kashmir in northern India."

The monk brought his eyes down from the cross and joined his palms to Jingjing. Jingjing joined his palms too. He did not know whether that was the right way to return it, but could think of no other.

Luo Haoxin was a straightforward man. He came into the courtyard, sat, drank a mouthful of hot water, and stated his business. "My father was a man of Kashmir who came to Tang land and married and had me. I grew up in Chang'an; I only know a few words of Kashmiri, learned from my father as a boy. This cousin of mine left home young, studied the scriptures for decades in the great monasteries all over India, and vowed to come east. In the second year of Jianzhong he came by sea-going ship to Guangzhou, and the year after reached Chang'an. In Chang'an he lodged in the Buddhist monasteries, and not one person could talk to him. He knows Sanskrit and several Indian tongues; Tang, not a word. There are people in the Buddhist monasteries who know a little Sanskrit, but the sutra he brought is a *hu* text, not a Sanskrit one. It's called the *Six Paramitas Sutra*, seven rolls, and nobody can read it."

When Jingjing heard "the second year of Jianzhong," something stirred in him. In the first month of that year the stele had been set up before the monastery; while it was being raised, this old monk had been tossing on the waves of the South Sea.

"These last days I've been asking all over who in the city understands *hu* tongues, whose Tang writing is good," Luo Haoxin went on. "There's an old general in the camp, a *hu* too, surnamed Mi, who told me: go to the Daqin monastery in the Yining Ward and find Jingjing. He wrote the stele, and he's translated dozens of scriptures, and his Tang writing is better than a Presented Scholar's. So I came."

Jingjing said, "General, what I translate are our own scriptures."

Luo Haoxin was taken aback. He set down the bowl and said, "Aren't scriptures all scriptures? Your Reverence, I don't understand scriptures; I'm a soldier. All I know is my cousin walked from India to Chang'an, walked for years, for the sake of these seven rolls. As his cousin, I'll find someone to translate them for him, to gain him a bit of merit, and a bit for myself. Will you or won't you? Give me a word."

Jingjing looked at Prajna. Prajna sat there with his hands on his knees, quite still. He could not understand what they were saying, but he seemed to know they were talking about him. He looked at Jingjing, and in his eyes there was neither pleading nor pride. Jingjing remembered how, many years before, in that ruined monastery north of the city of Xizhou, seven teachers of the law had sat before an eleven-year-old child while he taught them water, fire, gate, road; they had looked at him like this too.

"Let me think about it, General," he said.

---

That night several of the elders sat in the scripture room until midnight.

Samaan was the first to refuse. He tucked his hands into his sleeves, and only when they were tucked in did he speak. "The first article of method in the patriarch's instructions: do not quarrel with the other religions. Translating scripture for the Buddhists is more trouble than quarrelling. Translate it well, and people say we're currying favour with the Buddhists; translate it badly, and people say we've spoiled their scripture. However you translate it, you'll get no credit." He turned to Jingjing. "Let me ask you: do you understand the Buddhist scriptures?"

"No," said Jingjing.

"Then what are you going to translate?"

Marya's voice had long since gone hoarse. He sat in a corner and said only one sentence: "Twenty-odd years I've preached in Chang'an, and the words I used were borrowed from the Buddhists and the Daoists. If they borrow our hand once, we're none the worse."

The next day Jingjing went to the Guangde Ward to see Official Pei and told him about it. Official Pei was nearly seventy that year and his legs were bad; he sat in the gallery in the sun, and when he had heard it out, stroked his beard and said, "The Shence Army these days is the eunuchs' army. A word from a eunuch counts for more than ten from a chief minister. A commander of the Shence Army brings his cousin to your door himself and asks you politely, and you say no—next time he comes to your door, it may not be politely. I wouldn't have favoured this, to begin with. But I'm old. From now on there's less and less in the monastery's affairs that I can shield you from; you have to know how to take things yourself. When someone holds out a hand and you pull yours back, they remember it."

He stroked his beard to the end and went on: "Translate it, then. You can translate it. Only remember one thing: what you don't understand, say you don't understand. Don't pretend."

Jingjing went back to the monastery and remembered something Pa had said: using other people's words is like living in someone else's house. You may live there, but when you've lived there a long time, don't forget it isn't your own house. This time, he thought, it was someone else coming to borrow his hand. He told Luo Haoxin he would translate.

---

The translating was done in the monastery's scripture room. Prajna came every morning, and on days Luo Haoxin was off duty he came too, and the three of them sat round a long table with the *hu* text spread open on it and brush, ink and a stack of paper beside.

On the very first day they got nowhere. The *hu* text was written in a *hu* script whose alphabet Jingjing knew; he could spell out the sounds letter by letter, and of the words he spelled out he understood half, and the half he didn't understand were precisely the important words. Prajna understood them but could not say them; when he opened his mouth out came a long string of Sanskrit, very fast. Luo Haoxin, listening at the side with knitted brows, now and then picked out one or two words he had heard as a child and turned them into Tang, and having turned them, was the first to shake his head. "Your Reverence, all I understood was one 'water' and one 'walk.' The big stretch in the middle, I guessed."

So each word passed through three hands: Prajna said it to Luo Haoxin, Luo Haoxin said it to Jingjing, and Jingjing guessed at it against the *hu* text. When he guessed right, Prajna nodded; when he guessed wrong, Prajna shook his head; there was more shaking than nodding.

That night Jingjing stayed alone in the scripture room. The whole day's draft lay spread on the long table, every page a mass of corrections; what could be made out was no more than a dozen lines. He went to the corner, opened the wooden box, reached to the very bottom, and took out Alopen's parchment book. In the spring of the first year of Jianzhong, writing the stele, he had held it nearly every night, searching it for dates, for names, for the wording of edicts. This time what he was looking for was different. He wanted to know how, a hundred and fifty years ago, a man who could not speak one sentence of Tang had translated scripture.

The four mouths in the library hall he had known about since writing the stele: Syriac, Persian, Tang, and then the scribe of the Imperial Library setting it down in literary Chinese. Reading it page by page this time, he found something he had never noticed. After Lord Fang came to the library hall once to look at the drafts, he set a rule, which Alopen recorded very carefully, with a circle drawn beside it. When the scribe had fixed a passage, that was not the end of it: the interpreter had to read the Tang back into the *hu* tongue, and the disciple read the *hu* tongue back into Syriac, sentence by sentence, to Alopen. What matched stayed; what did not was circled, and the four mouths went through it again from the start. On one page the book said: "Walked back the other way, three sentences in ten are no longer mine." A few pages on: "Today two in ten were not mine." A few pages further: "Today only one. Lord Fang says, one is one; slowly. I still cannot understand his Tang, but when he says slowly, his face means it."

Jingjing put the book down and thought of the long table that day. Three people, three hands, all going forward, not one going back. Prajna could not read Tang characters; Luo Haoxin did not know the scripture; whether what he wrote down was right, there was no one in the world who could walk it back for him and look. The dozen lines he had written that day looked like Tang writing, and read smoothly, but how many sentences in them were Prajna's, he did not know.

He wrapped the book up, put it back at the bottom of the box, and blew out the lamp. The next morning what he read was the passage Gabriel had once preached in Balkh: the younger son spent all he had, came to himself, and arose and went to his father. On the board he wrote the character *hui*, return.

---

The three syllables *po-luo-mi*, paramita, took three days to translate.

Prajna said a great deal. Luo Haoxin nodded as he listened, and shook his head as well, and at last said, "He says it's just crossing a river. Crossing from this side to the other."

Jingjing asked, "To which other side?"

Luo Haoxin asked again. Prajna said more, and Luo Haoxin scratched his head. "He says on the other side there's no suffering."

Jingjing held his brush over the paper and could not set it down. He thought he understood this, and thought he did not. What he understood was that there was a river, and on the other side of the river a place where, when people got there, they wept no more. What he did not understand was whether the river Prajna spoke of was the river he knew. Later an old believer at the monastery who had once been a Buddhist told him the Buddhist scriptures already had a ready-made rendering: *dao bi'an*, reaching the other shore. So he wrote *reaching the other shore*. The words were good words. When he had written them he put a small dot beside them with his brush, his own mark, meaning: borrowed here from someone else.

Of the six paramitas there was one he translated most smoothly. The day they came to *renru*, forbearance under insult, Prajna said a long string, Luo Haoxin couldn't make it out, and Prajna, impatient, stood up and acted it: held out a hand and struck lightly at his own face in the air, turned the other cheek, struck at it again, then joined his palms and stood still.

"Being hit and not hitting back," said Luo Haoxin. "He means being hit and not hitting back."

Jingjing's hand on the brush stopped. He remembered Pa on the bank of the Yellow River, one man with a shield and a mace, knocking down five and killing not one. He remembered Yonan slipping on the wet stones by the well, and Narsai going to help him up with the knife still in his hand, and Yonan lying on the ground saying to Narsai, it was you who helped me up. He remembered the line in the scripture: whoever smites thee on thy right cheek, turn to him the other also. He bent his head and wrote. That roll went very fast; in one day he did what took three on other days.

Luo Haoxin, watching beside him, said, "Your Reverence didn't need to ask this time."

"This one we have too," said Jingjing.

Luo Haoxin told Prajna what he had said, and Prajna looked at Jingjing and smiled. When he smiled all the wrinkles of his face crowded up toward his eyes, like a piece of paper crumpled and spread flat again.

Later in the translating the two of them talked of other things too. Prajna asked what their Buddha was called. Jingjing said, "We have no Buddha. We have Mishihe, the Messiah. He came from heaven and was born among men, and men nailed him to a piece of wood, and he died, and after three days lived again." Luo Haoxin rendered it, haltingly. Prajna nodded as he listened and said a long string, and Luo Haoxin said, "He says the Buddha too had many lives, and in one of them he gave his body to feed a starving tigress."

"That's not the same," said Jingjing.

Luo Haoxin opened his mouth, closed it again, looked at Jingjing, looked at Prajna, and said, "That one I'm not translating. You two act it out yourselves."

The two of them looked at each other. One an old monk come from India, one a *hu* monk born in Chang'an, one without Tang, one without Sanskrit, sitting at the two ends of the long table with a *hu* text between them that neither could read entire; each bent his head and went on translating the next line.

Prajna ate one meal a day, at noon. Jingjing's stomach was bad, and he could not eat much at a sitting. The woman who cooked brought in two bowls of gruel, and the two of them each drank only half a bowl and pushed the rest aside to go cold. Luo Haoxin looked at the one and then the other, sighed, and drank both half-bowls himself. A few days later Prajna brought a small packet of yellow-brown dried fruits, shrivelled, like an old man's fingers, pressed it on Jingjing, pointed at his own belly, and pointed at Jingjing's. Luo Haoxin said, "It's *harītakī*, an Indian medicine. He says your stomach isn't good." Jingjing did not know how he had seen it, and took it; that packet of *harītakī* lasted him more than a year.

---

The seven rolls took more than seven months. The day they were done, Luo Haoxin carried the draft home to make a fair copy and submitted it with a memorial. Then there was waiting: a winter and a spring, then a summer and an autumn.

In the first month of winter in the third year of Zhenyuan, on the day of the first snow, Luo Haoxin came alone, without Prajna. He stood in the snow and would not come in, took a sheet of paper from his breast and handed it to Jingjing, his face as if he had eaten something bad. "Your Reverence, this is a copy. Read it yourself."

There were not many characters on the paper. It called him the Persian monk Jingjing of the Daqin monastery. It said that Prajna was not versed in the *hu* language and did not understand Tang speech; that Jingjing did not know Sanskrit and was not versed in the Buddhist teaching; that though it was called a translation, not half a pearl had been obtained; that they sought a vain name, and it was no blessing. It said that the Sage, examining the translation, found its principles obscure and its wording loose. And it said: the monasteries of the Buddhists and the monasteries of the monks of Daqin are separate dwellings, and their practice entirely different. Jingjing should propagate the teaching of Mishihe; the sons of Śākya should expound the Buddhist sutras; let the teachings be kept distinct, that men not stray between them; for the true and the heterodox differ, as the Jing and the Wei run in separate streams.

Jingjing read it to the end, then read it again from the beginning, and at the words *the true and the heterodox differ* he stopped. Snow fell on the paper and melted into small drops of water, blurring several characters; he blotted them gently with his sleeve.

Luo Haoxin rubbed his hands and said, "It's my fault. I shouldn't have come to you and got you this drubbing. The Sage has ordered my cousin to translate it again, with some of the great masters of the Ximing Monastery."

"The Sage is right," said Jingjing. "I don't understand the Buddha."

Luo Haoxin seemed not to have expected him to say this. He looked at him a long while, sighed, made a salute with crossed hands, turned and left, a line of footprints in the snow all the way to the ward gate.

When Samaan heard, he came to the scripture-room door and stood there. Jingjing thought he would say *I told you so*. Samaan did not say it. He only asked for the paper, read it through once, and gave it back to Jingjing. "Keep it safe. A government document. It'll be of use one day."

"It's abusing us," said Jingjing.

"Abusing us, and still a document."

Jingjing took the paper to the Guangde Ward again. Official Pei was lying in bed. He had himself helped up, held the paper far off, leaned in under the lamp and read it through, and said, "Look at the last sentence."

"I've looked. *Jingjing should propagate the teaching of Mishihe.*"

"On this one sheet of paper, ten sentences abuse you; they even use the word *heterodox*. One sentence allows you." Official Pei tapped the line with his finger. "But that sentence is the Sage's meaning. From now on, if anyone says you people ought not to preach in Chang'an, copy that sentence out and show it to him." When he had said it he lay down again and panted a while. "I can't get up these days. This winter, I'm afraid, will be hard to get through."

---

That night when Jingjing got back to the monastery the snow had grown heavy. He went first into the scripture room, folded the paper, opened Alopen's book, and turned to the latter half, to the page for the winter of the tenth year of Zhenguan, when the Son of Heaven summoned Alopen into the palace. The Syriac on this page was scrawled. Elder Babai had worked at it twenty years in his day and made out half; the other half Jingjing had not had time to make out when he wrote the stele. This time he made it out character by character.

Only now did he learn that the four characters on the stele, *questioned on the Way within the palace*, had really happened. The first thing the Son of Heaven asked was: the Heavenly Worthy, the ruler, and one's parents—which of the three comes first? Alopen answered: first revere the Heavenly Worthy, next revere the ruler, after that revere one's parents. The Heavenly Worthy teaches men to revere the ruler, and teaches them to revere their parents; the three do not go against one another. The Sogdian interpreter opened his mouth and no sound came. Alopen wrote: "His face was white. Lord Fang looked at him once, and only then did he say it out." The second thing the Son of Heaven asked was why the monks shaved the crowns of their heads but kept their beards. Alopen answered: we keep our beards to show that outwardly we still do the business of the world as other men do; we shave our crowns to show that inwardly we keep no private desire. Jingjing knew this line; he had carved it on the stele: *they keep their beards, to show that they have outward conduct; they shave the crown, to show that they have no inward passions.* He had taken it for an old rule handed down in the monastery, and had never thought that its source was a weather-beaten *hu* monk kneeling in the palace, through four mouths, answering a Son of Heaven's question. Last of all the Son of Heaven asked him: you have come from so far, men have died on the road and beasts have died; now that you are here, what do you want? Alopen answered: on the road, when a man was thirsty we gave him water, when a man was cold we gave him clothes; now that we are here, we want to do the same. Outside the palace gate Lord Fang stopped him and said one sentence, and this time the interpreter passed it on at once: "The Son of Heaven looks at the man."

Jingjing read the page again. Alopen's scripture had been mistranslated: the Lord's name written as *Yishu*, the Heavenly Worthy borrowed from the Daoists, the whole page full of other people's words; and when Emperor Taizong had read it, he allowed him to build a monastery in the Yining Ward. These seven rolls of his read smoothly in every word, even *reaching the other shore* the Buddhists' own ready-made phrase; and when the present emperor had read them, he said the principles were obscure and the wording loose.

He copied the sentence *Jingjing should propagate the teaching of Mishihe* by itself onto a small slip of paper. As he copied it, he wrote the *shi* of Mishihe not as 尸, corpse, but as 施, to give. He tucked it under the inkstone with one corner showing. Then he ground ink and went on translating his own scriptures, translated until it was fully dark, and only then remembered that the lamp at the gate had not yet been lit.

---

Note: Prajna, a man of Kāpiśa in northern India (Yuanzhao says "to say Kashmir is a corruption"), reached Guangzhou in the second year of Jianzhong and Chang'an in the third. In the second year of Zhenyuan, with "the Persian monk Jingjing of the Daqin monastery," he translated from a *hu* text the *Six Paramitas Sutra* in seven rolls; "Prajna was not versed in the *hu* language and did not understand Tang speech; Jingjing did not know Sanskrit and was not versed in the Buddhist teaching; though it was called a translation, not half a pearl was obtained; they sought a vain name, and it was no blessing"; Dezong "examined the translation and found its principles obscure and its wording loose"; "Jingjing should propagate the teaching of Mishihe; the sons of Śākya should expound the Buddhist sutras; let the teachings be kept distinct, that men not stray between them; the true and the heterodox differ, as the Jing and the Wei run in separate streams." Afterward Prajna, with Yuanzhao of the Ximing Monastery and others, retranslated it in ten rolls as the *Mahāyāna Naya Six Pāramitā Sutra*. All of this is in Yuanzhao's *Zhenyuan xinding shijiao mulu* (quotations follow the common transcription; to be checked against the Taishō text). Luo Haoxin, a commander of the Shence Army, was the son of Prajna's maternal uncle. Alopen's book, the method of "walking back," and the questions and answers in the palace in the tenth year of Zhenguan are the novel's invention; "they keep their beards, to show that they have outward conduct; they shave the crown, to show that they have no inward passions" is from the stele.
