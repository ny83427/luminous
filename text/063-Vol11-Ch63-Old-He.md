# Volume Eleven　Jade Gate

## Chapter 63　Old He

> The cross is their seal; it gathers the light of the four quarters and joins all without hindrance.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

Two days east of Shazhou was Guazhou. North of Guazhou, across a river, was the Jade Gate.

The Jade Gate was not what Jingfu had imagined.

He had thought it would be a great pass, its walls high, its gates thick, three great characters written over the gate. When they reached it, it was only a ring of earthen wall, half of it fallen, and inside a few earthen houses whose roofs were gone, their walls holed through, hole after hole, by the wind. Before the pass ran a river called the Hulu, not wide, but very fast, and along its bank grew a line of red willow.

No one held the pass. The Tibetans kept no troops here; they said there was no need. The country beyond the pass needed no one to hold it. It held itself.

The head of the caravan was an old Sogdian surnamed He, who had travelled this road for forty years. He stood before the pass, pointed north, and said: "From here north to Yizhou is eight hundred *li*. It's called the Moheyan Desert."

Salang asked: "Eight hundred *li*—how many days?"

"If things go well, ten," said Old He. "If not, there's no point counting."

"What's out there?"

Old He thought and said: "Nothing."

He paused a while, and said: "In the old days the Tang built five beacon towers along the road, and under each beacon tower there's a well. The beacon towers are still there. Some of the wells are dry, some have water, and the water's brackish."

"Which ones have water?"

"You know when you get there," said Old He.

That night the caravan rested before the pass. Old He had everyone fill every waterskin and water jar, fill them till not a drop more would go in. Salang filled the jar the old priest had given him too, tied the cloth tight over it, and lashed it to his back.

Jingfu hardly slept that night. He sat at the foot of a breach in the wall of the pass, listening to the water of the Hulu. It was very fast, rushing; not like the water of the Tanzheng Gorge, it had no high and low, it only drove on and on.

Near dawn, he heard Salang speaking beside him.

"Master," said Salang, "there's no one at the pass."

"Mm."

"Then when we go out of the pass," said Salang, "who's sending us back?"

Jingfu thought.

"We are," he said.

---

The Moheyan Desert was black.

The ground was spread with a layer of black pebbles, and under the pebbles was sand, and the sand was grey. The sun shone on the black pebbles and baked them hot; the heat rose up off the ground, and everything in the distance shimmered, wavering, as if seen through water.

No grass, no trees, no birds. Sometimes they walked a whole day without seeing one living thing. Only beside the road, every so often, lay a heap of white bones—camels', horses', and men's too. The bones had been ground smooth by wind and sand, and shone white.

Old He said these were the road markers.

"Follow the bones," he said, "and you won't go wrong."

By day they rested and by night they walked. By day it was too hot; neither men nor camels could bear it, so they stretched a felt over a hollow in the sand, hid under it, and waited without moving for the sun to set. The moment the sun went down the sky cooled, cooled as if they had gone to some other place. Everyone got up, pulled the camels to their feet, and walked by the stars.

Old He found the way at the front. He did not look at the ground; he looked at the sky. He said which star was where, keep to the left of which star, and when which star set, it was time to rest.

On the third night they reached the first beacon tower.

The beacon tower was built of adobe, square, higher than a house, one corner of its top fallen in. At its foot was a well, its mouth ringed with stones. Salang let the rope down, let it down very deep, before he heard a *plop* below. He drew it up: half a bucket of water, muddy, yellow.

Old He tasted a mouthful and said: "Drinkable."

They filled the waterskins again.

On the fifth night they reached the second. The well was dry. Salang let the rope to the bottom, drew it up, and on the rope's end was a little wet sand.

On the seventh night they reached the third. There was water, brackish, so salt it was bitter. Old He said: men mustn't drink it, give it to the camels.

That night, Kang Zhen collapsed.

---

Kang Zhen collapsed as he walked.

He was walking behind Elijah, not making a sound, and his legs went soft under him and he knelt, and once he had knelt he fell forward onto the ground.

Salang turned him over. His lips had split, split into cut after cut, and blood was seeping from the cuts. His eyes were half open, looking at the sky, at what no one knew.

"Water," said Jingfu.

Salang unlashed the jar from his back, untied the cloth, raised Kang Zhen's head, and poured into his mouth. He poured very slowly, a little at a time. Kang Zhen's mouth would not move; the water ran out of the corners of his mouth and down his neck. So Salang poured again, a little, waited, a little more.

When a third of the jar had been poured, Kang Zhen's throat moved once.

And moved again.

He shut his eyes and opened them again. This time he saw Salang.

"The channel," he said.

Salang did not understand.

"The channel by our door," said Kang Zhen. "The water's cold."

Salang said: "Mm, it's cold."

He did not really know whether it was cold. That jar of water had ridden on his back seven days, sunned seven days; it was warm.

That night they went no further. Old He said if they went on, someone would die. He had everyone ring the camels round in a circle and lie down inside it. Then he had all the remaining water brought out and counted it once; when he had counted, he looked at the sky and said: three nights left.

"Three nights," he said. "The water's enough. People must go sparing."

That night, Jingfu did not drink. He poured his share into Salang's jar. Salang saw, said nothing, and poured his own share in as well.

Elijah watched them, watched a while, and untied his own waterskin too.

Jingfu shook his head and held his hand down.

"You're sixty-nine," said Jingfu. "You drink."

Elijah did not understand Tang speech. But he understood Jingfu's hand. He tied his waterskin up again.

---

On the eleventh night, near dawn, the camels at the front suddenly began to cry out.

They cried out urgently, cry after cry. The camels stretched their necks and pushed their noses forward, and the men leading them could not hold them back.

Old He said: "They smell water."

When the sun came up, they saw it.

On the horizon ahead there was a green line. Very thin, very faint, as if someone had dipped a brush in the least bit of colour and stroked it lightly between earth and sky. But it was green. After eleven nights of walking, it was the first green they had seen.

That was Yizhou.

Someone in the caravan wept. It was the woman of the *hu* household. All along the road she had not wept—not crossing the Yellow River, not at New Year in Liangzhou—and now, seeing that green line, she squatted on the ground and wept like a child.

Her husband stood beside her patting her back, patted a while, and then squatted down too.

---

Outside Yizhou a stretch of tents was pitched.

Like those outside Ganzhou, they were Uighur. Not many tents, thirty or forty, round a spring, and by the spring horses were tethered, very lean. Outside the tents, strips of meat were drying, hides were drying, and a few barefoot children ran about by the spring.

The caravan camped on the other side of the spring.

That afternoon, a Uighur woman came over. She was thirty or so, her face reddened by the wind, her hair in braids with a few bone beads tied to the braid-ends. In her arms she held a child of four or five, its face yellow, its eyes shut, limp.

She came to the edge of the caravan's camp, stopped, and looked at these people. She looked round once, went up to Old He, and said a few words. Old He listened and shook his head. She spoke again, and Old He shook his head again.

Jingfu went over and asked Old He what she was saying.

Old He said: "The child's had the flux, half a month now. She's asking if there's medicine."

Jingfu looked at the child. Its lips were white, the bones of its wrists stood out, thin as kindling. He thought of Xiaoman. In his second year at the monastery Xiaoman had had the flux like this once.

He remembered how, that time Xiaoman had the flux, he had gone round and round the back courtyard all night, not knowing what to do.

"Wait," he said.

He went back into the camp, opened his bundle, and took from the very bottom a small cloth bag. In the bag was ginger.

Xiaoman had given it to him. On the night before the day outside the Kaiyuan Gate, Xiaoman had squatted by the vegetable bed in the back courtyard, scraped aside the new earth tipped onto it when the stele was buried, scraped for a long time, dug out a dozen-odd knobs of ginger, put them in a cloth bag, and pushed it on him. Xiaoman said: ginger doesn't mind being buried.

Jingfu took the ginger out, cut a few slices, asked Salang for the jar, boiled a jarful of water, and put the ginger in to boil. It boiled till the whole camp smelled of ginger's bite.

He took the ginger broth to the woman.

The woman looked at the bowl of broth, smelled it, and frowned. She looked at Jingfu a while, and then raised the child's head and fed it to him mouthful by mouthful.

That night Jingfu boiled it again. The next morning, again.

On the third day, the child opened his eyes.

On the fifth day, the child could sit up, and sat in the door of the tent gnawing a strip of dried meat.

---

On the evening of the fifth day, an old man came from the tents and asked Jingfu to come over.

The old man was the eldest of this band of Uighurs, his hair and beard all white, the lines on his face deep enough to catch sand. He sat in the middle of the tent, had Jingfu sit opposite him, and had the woman bring a bowl of mare's milk.

The mare's milk was sour. Jingfu drank it, drank it straight down. The old man watched him finish and nodded.

Then the old man asked him: that evening by the spring, what had the white-bearded *hu* monk been saying to the sky?

Jingfu said it was prayer.

The old man thought a while, put out his hand, and drew something on his own chest. First the upright, then the crossbar. He did not draw it quite right; upright and crossbar were both askew.

Jingfu looked at him.

The old man said that when he was small, in the royal camp in the north, there had been men in white robes, from the west, who could say this, could draw this. They came with the Sogdian caravans, lived beside the royal camp, tended the sick, said prayers over the dead. Later the Khagan took up Mani, and the men in white robes grew fewer. Later still, they did not come.

"My mother," said the old man, "when she died, one of the men in white robes prayed over her. What he said, I don't remember. I only remember he drew this."

He drew it again.

The tent was very quiet. The woman sat by the door with her child in her arms, watching them.

"We're going north," said the old man. "North of the Tianshan, there's pasture there, nobody's taken it. Further north, and east, there are still our people, scattered over the steppe, band after band."

He paused a while.

"Where we are," he said, "people are born, people die. No one says the words."

Jingfu said nothing.

Nor did the old man say more. He took up the milk bowl, poured another bowl, and pushed it in front of Jingfu.

---

That night Jingfu sat by the spring a long time.

Salang came over and sat down beside him.

Neither spoke. The spring welled up from under the ground, *glug*, *glug*, beat after beat, welled up and flowed away. In the Uighur tents on the far side someone was singing, very low; the words could not be made out.

After a long while Jingfu said: "Tomorrow you go with Elijah."

Salang did not move.

"He's sixty-nine," said Jingfu. "From here to the Patriarchate is ten thousand *li* more. On the road he'll need someone to carry him."

"And the Master?"

Jingfu glanced at the far side.

Salang glanced at the far side too. He looked a long time.

"The Master's place of origin," he said, "is Xizhou."

"Go and have a look for me," said Jingfu. "North of the city there's a monastery. My master helped repair it the year he was eleven. See if it's still there."

Salang said nothing.

Jingfu took the paper from his coat. Folded into a small square, folded very tight, its edges pressed even; carried in his coat the better part of a year, it had gone soft, its edges furred.

He held the paper out to Salang.

"This," he said, "goes to the Patriarchate."

Salang took it. He could not read; of Syriac letters he knew not one. But he knew what it was. That night at Wugong, sleeping beside the Master and not asleep, he had seen the Master open it fold by fold by the moonlight, look a long time, and fold it back fold by fold.

He tucked the paper into his coat and laid it close against his chest.

"One more thing," said Jingfu.

From his bundle he took a bowl. Its bottom worn white, its rim smooth, touched by men's lips for decades.

"My grand-teacher's," said Jingfu.

Salang looked at the bowl and did not take it.

"Take it," said Jingfu. "To drink from on the road."

"Then what will the Master use?"

Jingfu took out the other one from his bundle. That bowl had a notch in its lip, and in it was a pinch of earth, wrapped in cloth, wrapped very tight.

"I've got this," said Jingfu.

Salang looked at the bowl, and then at Jingfu.

"Tip the earth out," said Jingfu, "and you can drink from it."

Salang wanted to say something, and did not get it out. He took the grand-teacher's bowl.

---

Early the next morning, a mist rose over the spring.

Not a heavy mist, a thin layer, lying close on the water; when the sun came up it would lift. The caravan had loaded the camels, and Old He stood at the front, looking west, waiting. The Uighur tents were half struck; the women were rolling up the felts, the men harnessing the horses, and the child sat on a horse's back gnawing dried meat, looking this way.

Elijah sat on a camel. The sheepskin book was in his coat, wrapped in three layers of cloth.

Kang Zhen stood beside the camel. He was a little better; his face was still yellow, and the cuts on his lips had scabbed. He came up to Jingfu, stood a while, said nothing, bent, and bowed.

Jingfu returned the bow.

Elijah looked at Jingfu from the camel and said one sentence in Syriac.

Jingfu understood. What he said was: "The Lord be with you."

Jingfu answered in Syriac.

Then Salang.

Salang had his bundle on his shoulder, the staff stuck through its strap, iron tip upward, the jar lashed to his back, and in his coat the bowl, and the paper. He stood before Jingfu, a head taller than Jingfu.

He looked at Jingfu, looked a while.

"Master," he said, "I'm going."

Jingfu did not turn to look at him. He was squatting on the ground, helping the Uighur woman tie a roll of felt. He pulled the cord tight, tied a knot, pulled it again, pulled it firm.

Then he raised one hand and waved it, behind him.
