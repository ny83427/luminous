# Book Three　Wind

# Volume Ten　Morning Praises

## Chapter 61　The Quota

> The office concerned at once built a Daqin monastery in the Yining Ward of the capital and ordained twenty-one monks.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

On the first seventh day after Jingjing was buried, while it was still dark, Jingfu went to stand in the middle of the courtyard as the Master had told him. He thought he was alone; only when he had stood still did he sense there were people behind him. He looked back. Lingbao was standing under the jujube tree, Siyuan by the well curb, An Yan by a gallery pillar; and further back, in the gallery, at the hall door, under the eaves of the west wing, standing, crouching, leaning on the wall, a dark mass of people, some telling strings of knots in their hands, a knot, a line.

While the Master lived, the morning office in the hall had been everyone's; this standing in the courtyard outside the hall had been the Master's alone: stand a while before light, tell the knots once, go into the scripture room and read a passage, write one character on that old board from Xizhou. Everyone at the monastery knew of it, and no one had joined him.

When the sky greyed, Jingfu counted. Including the old believers who had come in from the ward, there were forty-one.

When they dispersed, he stopped Lingbao at the scripture-room door. "Uncle Lingbao, why have you all come?"

Lingbao tucked his hands into his sleeves. "Your Master didn't tell us to come. He didn't tell us not to, either."

From that day, every morning before light, there were people standing in the courtyard.

---

Jingfu directing the monastery was not like the Master directing it.

While the Master was alive, the ward headman cupped his hands when he saw him, the recorder of the Metropolitan Prefecture called him Master Jing, and the minor clerks of the Commissioners of Merit's inspectorate, coming to the monastery, sat down and asked first who had written the characters on the stele. The first time Jingfu went to the inspectorate to hand in a petition, the clerk sitting behind his desk did not ask him to sit, took the petition, did not so much as turn its pages, and asked him: "That old monk of yours who wrote the stele—where is he?"

"He has passed away."

"Oh." The clerk put the petition under a pile of documents. "Come back in a few days."

Jingfu stood a while in the gallery before he went out. He knew in his heart where he fell short. His Syriac was still what it was. At the offering once in seven he could not keep up with the scriptures the elders recited. The sounds he made up himself the old believers of the ward could not tell from the real thing; the few old monks who had come from the Patriarchate could, and did not say so, only lowered their heads. The bread he could not break; the only one at the monastery now who could consecrate it was Lingbao. Hands had not even been laid on him as deacon: a deacon must be twenty-five, and the year the Master died he was twenty-three, two years short. In the eight years the Master was bishop he had laid hands on An Yan as deacon and on two old monks as priests, and both had died before the Master. With the Master gone, there was no one left in Chang'an who could lay on hands.

What he could do was the gruel, the lamp, the weddings and funerals in the ward, the cart for the dead, the talking with the ward headman, with the inspectorate, with those *hu* merchants of the West Market. His Tang was good, and people in the city knew him. Standing in the inspectorate's gallery he thought that the Master had probably picked him not for any great ability of his, but because when people in the city saw this face of his, nobody minded it.

---

In the first month of the fifteenth year of Yuanhe the Son of Heaven died suddenly in the palace. It was rumoured in the city that eunuchs had done away with him; the ward headman had every household shut its doors, and forbade talking of it in the street. The new Son of Heaven took the throne, and the next year the reign name was changed to Changqing. Jingfu was twenty-six and had directed the monastery more than two years.

Early in the spring of the year the reign name changed, an aide-de-camp came from the Commissioners of Merit's inspectorate, forty or so, unsmiling, and came in and handed Jingfu a dispatch.

"By disposition of the Commissioner's office, the monk registers of all monasteries in the capital are to be made afresh. Each monastery is to report its monks by name according to its quota. Monks within the quota are exempt from taxes and labour service, and live in the monastery; those beyond it are ordered to return to lay life, be entered on the registers as households, and pay under the Twice-a-Year Tax." When he had said it he added, "What your monastery's quota is, you know yourselves. Report the names to the inspectorate within ten days. Past the deadline, the inspectorate will send men to count. For every name too many on the register, the director is answerable."

The quota of the Daqin Monastery had been fixed in the Zhenguan years. The stele bore the edict of the twelfth year of Zhenguan: *The office concerned at once built a monastery in the Yining Ward of the capital and ordained twenty-one monks.* The monastery was still called the Persian Monastery then; it was renamed only in the Tianbao years. The name had changed; the number twenty-one had not changed in a hundred and eighty years. Those who lived, ate and worked at the monastery had never been counted to the number twenty-one. While Elder Samaan lived, every one of them was in the ledger, and in reporting to the government the monastery had always reported twenty-one by the quota. The extra ones the government didn't ask about, and the monastery didn't mention. While Jingjing was alive the inspectorate had come to count twice; when they had counted, they drank a bowl of broth, looked at the stele, and left.

That evening Lingbao carried the ledgers into the little room in the back courtyard and turned them page by page. Jingfu sat across from him and waited for him to finish.

"Thirty," said Lingbao. "Counting you and me, thirty monks in residence."

"Which thirty?"

Lingbao read them one by one. Over sixty, fourteen: "Elder Hanan, blind eight years, came from Xizhou with a Sogdian caravan in the second year of Baoying; he was nineteen the year he came. Old Cao, seventy-two, has drawn water at the well curb forty years and is still splitting firewood today." He read on: those from the Patriarchate, those from the Western Regions, the Tang men of the ward who had left home decades ago—name after name, very slowly.

Then those in between: "Me, fifty-four. Siyuan, past fifty; he's pushed the cart for the dead thirty years. An Yan, fifty, deacon. You, twenty-six. Joseph and Elijah, thirty or so, came by sea with Elder David in the fourth year of Yuanhe."

"And the rest?"

"Ten, all young. The eldest under thirty, the youngest seventeen. Guangzheng, the Shi brothers, the Luo family's Twelfth, Salang, grandson of old Sargis of the guard—they're all among them."

Jingfu reckoned it in his head. "Thirty, report twenty-one; nine have to go."

"Yes." Lingbao closed the ledger. "Of the ten days, one has gone today already."

---

They deliberated three evenings.

On the first evening Jingfu spoke first. His face was red before he said it, but he said it all the same.

"The ones to go should be the old," he said. "Uncle Lingbao, don't get worked up; hear me out. The monastery's future depends on the young. The scripture room needs people to copy scripture, the gruel shed needs people to carry rice, the cart for the dead needs people to push it, the offering once in seven needs people to sing. If the young go, the monastery will be left with a roomful of old men, and the old men will die one by one, and when they've all died the monastery will be empty. If the old leave the monastery, the monastery still keeps them: sends their meals every day, sees to them when they're ill. Their names are off the register, but they're still the monastery's people."

Lingbao heard him out and did not dispute it.

"There's reason in what you say," he said. "Then first work out an account for me. An old monk leaves the monastery and is entered as a household. First he has to have a household. A household needs a house; living in the monastery doesn't count as a household—the inspectorate will come and check. Once he has a household, he has to pay the Twice-a-Year Tax. The tax is fixed by property; an old monk owns nothing but the robe on his back, so the tax won't be much, but labour service he has to answer: repairing the walls, dredging canals, carrying things for the government. Past sixty some of it is remitted, not all. And that's a Tang man."

He pushed the ledger toward Jingfu.

"Those from the Patriarchate, from the Western Regions, have no native place. Returned to lay life, where do they get entered? The Metropolitan Prefecture will ask what country they're from, and when it's asked, they'll be sent back to their native place. Their native place is ten thousand *li* away; a man past seventy, sent back, will be gone halfway. Elder Hanan can't see. Away from this monastery he can't even feel his way to the well."

Jingfu said nothing.

"The young going out, their days will be harder, but they have hands and feet. They can carry rice, copy books, keep accounts for the *hu* merchants of the West Market. Chang'an is where they were born; they have a native place, they have relatives; they won't starve. The old going out—it means dying."

"And once the young have gone out, will they come back?"

"That I don't know," said Lingbao. "I only know that the old, going out, won't come back."

On the second evening An Yan spoke. He was fifty. In his early twenties he had followed Baaluo to the south of the city and lived more than ten years in the straw shed west of the Qujiang Pool, and lost half a toe to the cold, and it was Jingjing who had tramped through the snow and brought their seven households back.

"Lingbao's right; the old can't go out," he said. "The Director's right too. I went out once in my day, and once I was out, my heart ran wild. I was brought back, but someone has to go and do the bringing." He stopped and looked at his own foot in its cloth shoe. "Or I'll go out. I'm a deacon; hands were laid on me. Take me off the register, and the hands are still on me. At the offering once in seven I'll come and pass the bowl just the same."

"You going out alone makes only one," said Lingbao. "Eight still short."

"Then I'll go out first, and we'll deliberate about the rest."

Jingfu shook his head. "If the deacon goes out, the ward will say the monastery can't even keep its deacon any more. Uncle An Yan, not that."

---

The next day Jingfu went alone to the inspectorate again.

What he had in mind was one way out: if the old monks' names came off the register, could they be counted as lodgers—eating at the monastery, not counted as monks, not counted as households, and when the inspectorate came to count, taken for a few old invalids the monastery kept? He waited in the inspectorate's gallery until past noon before the aide-de-camp came out.

The aide-de-camp heard him out, still unsmiling. "Lodgers. Every monastery in the capital wants lodgers. The Jianfu Monastery has lodged a hundred-odd, the Daxingshan Monastery two hundred-odd, and all of them old and sick, they say. When the inspectorate goes to count, the old and sick go out by the back door one after another with sacks of rice on their shoulders. The word *lodger* the Commissioner's office no longer accepts."

"The fourteen in our monastery really are old. One has been blind eight years. I can take you to see him."

"I won't go to see. If I saw, it'd be the same answer," said the aide-de-camp. "The quota was fixed in the Zhenguan years. Twenty-one is twenty-one. If you think it too few, go and tell Taizong."

He turned and went in. Jingfu stood in the gallery. There were others in the gallery from other monasteries come to hand in petitions, and a young novice who knew him came over and asked, "Your Reverence, does your monastery have extra too?"

"It has extra."

"Ours has forty extra." The novice lowered his voice. "The abbot has already sent people to put up sheds at the estate outside the city. Take the names off the register, put the people out at the estate, and the inspectorate has no say over the estate."

Jingfu went back to the monastery and told Lingbao. Lingbao said, "The monastery has no estate. That piece of ground outside the Kaiyuan Gate is for burying people."

---

On the third evening Elder Samaan's ledgers lay open on the table, the lamp-wick sputtered several times, and the three of them sat until the second watch and still had settled nothing. An Yan went back to the west wing, and Lingbao leaned against the wall with his eyes closed. Jingfu got up to go to the privy in the back courtyard. Passing the row of rooms on the east side where the old monks lived, he saw Elder Hanan's door open and him standing in the doorway, one hand on the door frame and the other stretched out, groping at the air.

"Elder," Jingfu called.

Hanan knew his voice. "Is it the Director?" He spoke in Syriac. "I want to go to the well. I'm thirsty at night, and the water in my pot's gone. Usually Old Cao fills it for me; today Old Cao's dead asleep, so I'm going myself, and once out of the door I can't find the gallery pillar."

Jingfu held his arm and walked him from the gallery toward the well curb. A dozen-odd steps, and Hanan walked very slowly, feeling with his toes step by step, until he found the raised stone at the edge of the well curb, and stopped.

"Here we are," he said. "I remember this stone. When my eyes were still good it was here. I washed my face here every morning."

Jingfu drew water and filled his pot. Hanan did not go at once, but stood by the well curb with his face toward him.

"Director, these few nights the lamp's been lit in the little room, and I've heard. Is the monastery going to turn people out?"

Jingfu's Syriac was poor to begin with, and in his haste at night it was worse. He wanted to say, *no one's turning anyone out; the government is counting the register*. *No one's turning anyone out* he managed in Syriac; the second half he couldn't remember, so he had to say it in Tang.

Hanan understood. "I know. I heard you say 'go out' several times. I don't understand much Tang. Those two words, I understand."

He hugged the pot to him.

"I came in the second year of Baoying. I was nineteen the year I came, and I've washed my face at this well sixty years. Director, if I go out, I ask one thing of you. Have someone lead me out beyond the Kaiyuan Gate, to the edge of those rows of stakes, and I'll sit there by myself. The monastery needn't send meals. I'll sit a while, and then I'll go to see them."

Jingfu did not take up what he said. He helped Hanan back into his room, set the pot at the head of his bed, and took his hand and let him feel where the pot was. When he had found it, Jingfu came out.

He went back to the little room, and Lingbao opened his eyes. "Where've you been?"

"The well."

He sat down and sat until the third watch, and Lingbao sat with him until the third watch, and neither of them mentioned Hanan.

---

On the morning of the fourth day, when the morning office had broken up, Guangzheng came to find Jingfu, with the Shi brothers behind him, and the Luo Twelfth—ten in all, the ten young men, not one missing.

Guangzheng was twenty-eight, a Tang man of the Buzheng Ward. His father had died early; his mother did mending for people in the ward. He had entered the monastery at fourteen and studied Syriac letters with Jingjing, and wrote them best of the young men. Jingjing had said in his lifetime that this one could take over the scripture room one day.

"Director, yesterday we went to the Commissioners of Merit's inspectorate," said Guangzheng. "Ten of us. We put in a petition ourselves, asking to leave the monastery and return to lay life. The aide-de-camp accepted it and said volunteers make it easy: report twenty on the register; the quota's twenty-one; report twenty, and the inspectorate won't bother."

Jingfu stood there, his face scarlet. "Who told you to go?"

"No one told us. We ten talked it over ourselves the night before last in the west wing," said Guangzheng. "On the first evening, when Uncle An Yan came back to the west wing, his face looked bad; we asked, and he wouldn't say. On the second evening we went and stood under the little room's window and listened. Uncle Lingbao's account—we worked it out too. However we worked it, the ones to go could only be us."

"Not that all ten were willing at once," said the elder Shi brother from behind. "My little brother's only seventeen. We two brothers were left at the monastery gate in the famine year at the end of Zhenyuan; we haven't a single relative in Chang'an. He cried in the night. He said once out of this gate, he didn't know where to go."

"I told him, we've no home, but the West Market has storerooms enough." He patted his brother on the back of the head. "We brothers have already arranged it with a *hu* merchant in the West Market—keeping his accounts, two meals a day, and at night sleeping in the storeroom behind the shop."

"I'm going home," said the Luo Twelfth. "My home's in the Liquan Ward. My ma will be only too glad."

Guangzheng said, "I'm going back to the Buzheng Ward to live with my ma. She's on her own, and her eyes are bad now; I can thread her needles for her at night. Director, once we've left the monastery our names won't be on the monk register, but we'll still be here. We'll come to the morning just the same, before light; carry the rice at the gruel shed just the same, push the cart for the dead just the same, copy in the scripture room just the same. Only at night we won't sleep in the monastery, and at the offering once in seven we'll stand at the back, among the old believers of the ward."

"And the Twice-a-Year Tax? The labour service?"

"We're young. When labour service falls to us we'll go—repair the walls, dredge the canals—and come back and come to the morning just the same," said Guangzheng. "We've talked it over. Whether our names are in the register or not, Aluohe knows us."

Lingbao had been standing to one side all along. When he had finished, Lingbao only asked: "Twenty reported on the register leaves one empty. Have you worked out who the empty one is kept for?"

"We have," said Guangzheng. "That one stays empty. Of us ten, not one should take it. When someone comes from the Patriarchate—when someone who can lay on hands comes from the west—when he comes, there'll be a place for him on the register."

Lingbao did not praise him. He turned and went back to the little room.

---

Of the ten days' limit, on the ninth Jingfu took the register to the inspectorate.

Lingbao had written it: twenty names, Tang names first and *hu* names after—Lingbao, Siyuan, An Yan, Jingfu, Joseph, Elijah, Hanan, Old Cao, and the names of twelve more old men. Some of the old men's Tang names Jingjing had given them when they entered the monastery. Copying them out, Jingfu remembered how, whenever the Master named someone, he would go through the whole scripture first, until the table was covered with papers.

The aide-de-camp took it and counted once. "The quota is twenty-one. Why report twenty?"

"The ten beyond the quota have gone out. Twenty kept within it. One left empty."

"Empty for what?"

"For someone to come."

The aide-de-camp raised his eyes and looked at him, did not ask for whom, and put his mark on the register. "Next time the inspectorate comes to count, it'll count by this. One more, one less—either way there'll be questions."

---

The ten moved out that day. They had little: each a bundle and a quilt, and some carried a sheaf of half-copied scripture, saying they would light a lamp at night and go on copying and bring it back when it was done.

The Shi brothers' bundle was smallest. The elder said the storeroom of the *hu* merchant's shop had sacks enough; spread at night to sleep on, they were warmer than the monastery's straw mats.

The Luo Twelfth's mother came to fetch him, a plump Sogdian woman. She stood at the monastery gate, saw her son come out with his bundle on his back, and beamed. At the gate she turned back and made Jingfu a bow. "Your Reverence, he'll still come. He'll come every day. If he dares not come, I'll beat him."

Salang went empty-handed. "The Uighur warehouse in the West Market is short of porters, and gives you lodging," he told Jingfu. "My grandfather guarded the gate of this monastery all his life. I'll go and guard goods for the Uighurs. When I've guarded the goods, I'll come back before light and stand just the same."

Guangzheng was the last to go. He swept clean his own sleeping place in the west wing and put the broom back where it belonged. As he went out he stopped a moment before the lamp to the left of the gate. It was broad day, the lamp long since blown out. He crouched and looked at the ring blackened by oil on the stone block, got up, and went.

Old Cao did not split firewood that day. He sat on the well curb watching the young men go out of the gate one by one with their bundles on their backs, and said to Lingbao, "I'm seventy-two and I've taken a young man's place in the quota. I can't get over it."

"You can't get over it, but you'll have to," said Lingbao. "If you went out, where would you go?"

"I'm a man of the ward. My grand-nephew still lives at the east end of the ward."

"Your grand-nephew's family has nine mouths in three rooms. If you went, where would you sleep? On top of the stove?"

Old Cao did not answer. He laid his axe on the woodpile, got up, and went back to splitting firewood, splitting it louder than usual.

---

That night Lingbao put the ledgers in order in the little room. Jingjing had instructed before he died that Samaan's nine ledgers were to be kept with the paper Luo Haoxin had copied out. Jingfu had been busy these days and never got to it, so Lingbao saw to it himself.

The ledgers were bound in sheepskin. The oldest was frayed at the edges; Elder Samaan had begun it in the second year of Baoying, entry by entry in Syriac letters, small and dense, into the Zhenyuan years. In the later books the writing trembled more and more, and the last book was only half filled; the second half was in Lingbao's hand. He turned them over one by one. Some pages he knew: the costs of the day of Yisi's funeral; forty days' food for the stonemason father and sons the year the stele was set up; "Master Lü would not accept"; "'what is asked' changed to 'what is thanked.'" Some pages he had never seen; Samaan had written them and never shown them to anyone. They recorded scattered things: *Such a day of such a month, Granny so-and-so of such a ward came for gruel; did not receive. Such a day of such a month, from other wards at the gruel shed; given from the monks' own portion.* Below followed a few names, the names of those people from other wards; Samaan had asked each one and written it down, crookedly, and one name had *six* written after it.

When Lingbao turned to this page, his hand stopped. He remembered the gruel shed in the ward street that year, his brother on the first day pouring out his own bowl for a child in an old woman's arms, and on the second day he poured out his too. He was in his early twenties then. When he had poured it out, he went running errands in the West Market on an empty stomach. What that child looked like he no longer remembered.

He smoothed the page flat, closed the ledger, stacked the nine books square, wrapped them in a piece of old cloth, and put them on the bottom shelf of the scripture cabinet, on top of Luo Haoxin's paper and Jingjing's slip with the character *shi*, to give. Then he spread out the draft of the register reported to the inspectorate that day, and below the empty last line added a line in small characters: *Quota twenty-one; present twenty; one remaining, kept for one who is to come.*

---

The next day while it was still dark, Jingfu went to stand in the middle of the courtyard. Behind him people came one after another, some out of the west wing, some in through the monastery gate. Those who came in brought the cold of the ward street on them, stamping their feet and rubbing their hands, and went to stand in the gallery, among the old believers.

When the sky greyed he counted: forty-one, not one fewer. Guangzheng was standing in the last row, by the door.

Jingfu went into the scripture room, opened the scripture, read a passage, took up the charcoal, and on that old board from Xizhou wrote a character. What he wrote was *liu*, to keep, to stay.

---

Note: The Daqin Monastery's "ordained twenty-one monks" is from the edict of the twelfth year of Zhenguan recorded on the stele. In the first month of the fifteenth year of Yuanhe Xianzong died suddenly; opinion at the time largely blamed the eunuch Chen Hongzhi and others; Muzong took the throne, and the next year the reign name was changed to Changqing. From the second year of Yuanhe, Buddhist monks and nuns and Daoist priests came under the Commissioners of Merit of the Left and Right Streets, who controlled the monk registers of the capital's monasteries. The re-registration of monks by the Commissioners of Merit's inspectorate in the early Changqing years, with reports according to the authorized quota, is the novel's invention.
