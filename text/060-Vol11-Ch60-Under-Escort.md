# Volume Eleven　Jade Gate

## Chapter 60　Under Escort

> The office concerned at once built a Daqin monastery in the Yining Ward of the capital and ordained twenty-one monks.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

Out of the Kaiyuan Gate and twenty *li* west was the Lingao post station.

On the first day, they walked as far as that.

They did not walk fast. In the column there were people of seventy, people carrying earthenware jars, people with cooking pots on their backs. The clerk leading them walked at the very front; he walked a while, looked back once, walked a while more, looked back once more. His list was tucked in his coat, and every so often he put a hand inside and pressed on it, as if afraid it would run off by itself.

When they reached Lingao station, it was not yet dark. The station would not give them rooms; it gave them a row of stables behind the station. There was straw in the stables, dry straw, better than the ground.

The clerk stood at the stable door, took out his list, and called the names.

"Thirty-one souls," he said, when he had finished. "Correct."

Of the thirty-one, nine were from the Zoroastrian shrine, six were Muhu, four were Daqin, and the rest were a few households of *hu* with neither ordination papers nor registered homes, men and women, old and young, crowded at the very back of the stables, saying little. The escort was two soldiers from the county, swords at their hips, who did not trouble themselves much. Besides them there were six porters, to carry things, shoulder the pots, carry the old on their backs.

Salang was the tallest of the porters.

That day he carried two bundles, and on the way carried the old priest twice besides. The old priest would not let anyone carry him, said he could walk; he walked three *li*, his legs gave, and he sat down by the road. Salang squatted and turned his back to him. The old priest looked at his back a while, and climbed on. The earthenware jar he would let no one else hold; he hugged it to his own chest, wedged between Salang's back and himself.

At the station, Salang set the old priest down, then went to the well and drew a bucket of water and carried it into the stables.

The clerk watched him from the door and said: "Today you've carried three times what anyone else has."

Salang said: "Mm."

"The money's the same."

"Mm."

The clerk had nothing more to say, and turned to go and ask the station master for food.

---

Early the next morning, he called the names again.

The clerk unfolded the list and read them one by one. When he came to the Zoroastrians he read fast; he had read those names once already outside the Kaiyuan Gate, and his tongue knew them. When he came to the *hu* households he slowed; some names he could not say, and said them roughly, and someone below answered, and he drew a tick.

When he came to Daqin, he read "A-Fu" first.

Jingfu answered.

Then he read the two old Persian monks.

Those two names were Syriac; on the list they were Tang characters that the Chang'an County scribe had written down by their sound, one name five characters long, one four. The clerk read the first, stumbling, and when he had read it, no one answered.

The elder of the old monks stood there looking at him, and did not move.

The clerk read it again.

Still the old monk did not move.

Jingfu said in a low voice beside him: "Joseph."

Only then did the old monk say "Mm."

The clerk looked up. "What's he called?"

"Joseph," said Jingfu.

The clerk looked down at the five characters on the list, looked a while, and could not see which of them was Joseph. With his brush he wrote two small characters beside them, and when he had written them, read the second. No one answered the second either.

"Elijah," said Jingfu.

The younger of the old monks nodded and said: "Yes." His Tang speech was a little better than Joseph's; in over thirty years in Chang'an he had learned seven or eight hundred words of it, and could say "yes," "no," "thank you," "how much."

The clerk added two more small characters to the list.

The last was the one from Samarkand. His name was easy to say; the monastery called him Kang Zhen, and on the list too it was written Kang Zhen. The clerk read it, and he answered.

When he had finished, the clerk put the list away and said: "Move."

After that, every morning the clerk called names like this. When he came to Joseph, he no longer read the five characters on the list; he read "Joseph" straight off. Joseph heard it, and answered.

Joseph said to Elijah later that this little Tang official was the first person on the whole road to call him by his right name.

Elijah said: A-Fu taught him.

Joseph said: still, he's the one who called it.

---

From Lingao station westward, the road followed the River Wei.

The Wei was to the south, out of sight; all that could be seen was a line of willows along the river, their leaves half yellow. To the north was the tableland, on the tableland were fields, and in the fields the millet had already been harvested; what was left was row after row of stubble, and people in the fields gleaning.

The gleaners, seeing them, straightened up to look.

Many looked. At every village, a crowd stood at its edge. Some came for the spectacle, some to look at *hu*. In the city of Chang'an there were many *hu*, nothing rare; out of the city, *hu* were rare. And this was such a long string of them, men and women, old and young, pots on their backs, jars in their arms, a clerk in front and two soldiers behind—you could see at a glance they were under escort.

A child asked his mother what they had done.

His mother said: I don't know, the *hu* did something, probably.

The child said: is the tall one *hu* too?

His mother looked at Salang once and said: he doesn't look it.

Salang walked past, and did not turn his head.

Passing Wugong County, an old man stood waiting by the road with an earthenware pot. When the column came up to him, he handed the pot to a woman of the Zoroastrian shrine walking at the edge, and said it was water. The woman was taken aback a moment, took it, drank a mouthful, and passed it to the person beside her. One pot of water, passed from one to the next, passed to the back, and the pot was empty, and passed from one to the next back again, and returned to the old man.

The old man took the empty pot and said nothing, and went off carrying it.

The clerk in front saw, and let it be.

---

That night they lodged at Wugong.

The county gave them an empty room, its roof leaking, its floor damp, and they slept crowded together. Jingfu slept by the door. Salang slept beside him; he was too long, could not stretch his legs, so he slept slantwise, his feet out of the door.

Jingfu could not sleep. From his coat he took out the paper.

It was the one An Yan had given him. That day outside the Kaiyuan Gate, An Yan's hands had been full with the wooden board, and it was Lü Shao who had taken it out of his sleeve for him and pushed it into Jingfu's hand. Folded into a small square, folded very tight, its edges pressed even. For three days Jingfu had kept it in his coat and not opened it.

By the little moonlight from outside the door, he unfolded the paper, fold by fold.

The paper was black.

On the black ground, line after line, the letters were all white. Syriac letters, curling, like little worms, one after another. It was one of the sheets from Lü Shao's roll, from the side of the stele, the side with the names. When An Yan had got it from Lü Shao he did not know, nor how Lü Shao had borne to give it up.

Jingfu could read Syriac. His master had taught him. Not fluently; a name took him a while to spell out.

He read down the lines one by one.

In the middle, under one line, there was a mark on the paper. It was scored with a fingernail, scored very deep, scored till the paper had roughened.

Jingfu brought his face close to that line.

He spelled a while, and spelled it out.

Yisi.

He held the paper further off, and then nearer. He remembered An Yan saying that when he was small, the Master had taken his hand and run it over this line on the stele once, and said: this is my Pa.

On the back of the paper were a few Tang characters too. An Yan had written them. An Yan's hand was poor, written big and crooked, each character taking up the room of two.

What it said was:

"Take it with you."

Jingfu turned the paper over, and over again, and looked a while. Then he folded it back along its old creases, fold by fold, into a small square, tucked it back into his coat, and laid it close against his chest.

Beside him Salang turned over, and drew his feet back in from the door.

"Master," he said, "go to sleep."

"You're not asleep?"

"I'm asleep," said Salang.

---

On the third day, they reached Fufeng.

Fufeng County's post station was a little better than Wugong's; it had two rooms, and they did not leak. The clerk was very pleased, and said that tonight everyone would sleep well, tomorrow they would walk one more day, the day after they would reach Fengxiang, and he would hand them over.

That night, one person went missing.

It was one of the Muhu. A young man, eighteen or nineteen, the grandson of one of the old men of the Muhu. The old man fell asleep, woke, and the place beside him was empty.

Before it was light, the clerk knew. He called the names, came to that name, and no one answered. He called it again, and still no one answered. He called the list again from beginning to end, and counted thirty.

He stood there holding the list, his face gone white all at once.

"Where is he?" he asked the old man.

The old man sat on the ground, his head down, and said nothing.

The clerk asked again. Still the old man said nothing. The clerk asked the others—the few Muhu, the few Zoroastrians—and they all shook their heads.

The clerk went round once, came to Jingfu, and stopped.

"A-Fu," he said, "you sleep by the door. Did you see?"

Jingfu looked at him.

Everyone in the courtyard looked at Jingfu. The old man of the Muhu raised his head too, and looked at him.

"I saw," said Jingfu.

The clerk drew a breath. "When?"

"The second half of the night."

"Which way did he go?"

"East."

The clerk asked no more. He knew what was east. East was Chang'an. The young man had been born in Chang'an, grown up in Chang'an, had a sweetheart in Chang'an; everyone on the road knew it, for every evening he would stand looking east a while.

The clerk stood there, gripping the list, stood a long time.

The two soldiers of the escort came over and asked whether they should go after him. They would catch him; he'd left in the second half of the night, he couldn't have gone far.

The clerk did not answer them.

He went to the mounting-stone in the courtyard and sat down. He spread the list on his knee, took his brush and inkbox from his coat, dipped the brush, and held it poised, and for a long time did not bring it down.

The number on this list would be counted when they got to Fengxiang, by the men of Fengxiang Prefecture. If they counted one short, it was his affair. One short could be called died of illness, could be called escaped. Died of illness, there had to be a body, there had to be a county coroner's report. Escaped was negligence of escort; that meant a beating, a fine of salary, and for a little clerk like him, probably the end of his clerking.

He raised his head and looked at Jingfu once more.

"A-Fu," he said, "did you see that he was ill?"

Jingfu said nothing.

The clerk waited a while.

"Don't speak," said the clerk.

So Jingfu did not speak.

The clerk lowered his head and wrote a few characters beside that name on the list. When he had written them he blew on them, folded the list, and tucked it back in his coat. He stood up, waved a hand at the two soldiers, and said: "No pursuit. Move."

The old man of the Muhu was still sitting on the ground. Salang went over and helped him up. The old man stood, looked east, then looked at the clerk's back, and slowly followed.

That day on the road, no one spoke.

Toward evening, Jingfu came up beside the clerk and walked level with him a stretch.

The clerk looked at him once and said: "What do you want to say?"

"I don't want to say anything," said Jingfu.

"Then what did you come over for?"

Jingfu thought and said: "The road's narrow."

The clerk looked at the road. The road was not narrow; two carts could go abreast on it. He looked a while, and said nothing more.

---

On the fifth day, they reached Fengxiang.

Fengxiang was a prefectural city, its walls lower than Chang'an's and higher than a county town's. In the old days, when the Son of Heaven came back from Shu, he had halted here, and when the people of the city spoke of it they still straightened their backs.

The clerk led them to a courtyard behind the prefectural office and told them to sit and wait. He went in with the list, and was in for more than an hour. When he came out, a prefectural scribe followed him, fifty or so, with two wisps of moustache.

The prefectural scribe stood in the middle of the courtyard, unfolded the list, and counted them one by one.

He counted more slowly than the clerk, more carefully. He counted one, looked up once, checked the age, checked the looks. When he came to the Muhu name, he stopped, looked at the few small characters beside it, and looked up at the clerk.

"Fell ill on the road, left at Fufeng," he read aloud. "Where's the notice from Fufeng County?"

The clerk said: "It's coming after."

The scribe looked at him a while, said nothing, drew a tick, and went on counting.

When he came to Joseph, the scribe read the five characters, read them worse even than the clerk had on the first day.

Joseph answered.

When he had finished, there were thirty. The scribe put the list away, took out another sheet of paper, wrote a few lines, stamped a seal, and handed it to the clerk. This was the document of handing over. From this moment, these thirty souls belonged to Fengxiang Prefecture.

The clerk took the document, looked at the seal, folded it, and tucked it away.

His errand was done.

He stood in the courtyard a while, looking at these thirty souls. The old priest of the Zoroastrian shrine sat at the foot of the wall with his jar in his arms, dozing. The children of the *hu* households had drawn squares on the ground and were hopping about. Joseph and Elijah were leaning against the wall with their eyes shut, murmuring something. Kang Zhen was mending a shoe. Salang was squatting by the well, asking the men of the prefecture whether, going west, they would still be hiring porters.

The clerk looked at Jingfu one last time.

He came over and stood in front of Jingfu, stood a while, as if there were something to say and he did not know where to begin.

"That day at the Kaiyuan Gate," he said, "I didn't laugh."

Jingfu looked at him.

"I saw," said Jingfu.

The clerk was taken aback. Then his shoulders twitched again, just as on that day at the Kaiyuan Gate. He turned his face aside, turned it a while, turned it back, and the laugh had not quite left his face.

"A-Fu," he said, "on the road..."

He did not finish. He could not think what should be on the road. He was twenty or so, and in all his growing up the furthest he had gone was Fengxiang.

Jingfu nodded to him.

The clerk nodded too, turned, went out of the courtyard gate, and went east. He walked very fast; the list was no longer in his coat, and still he put a hand in and pressed.

---

That evening, the prefecture posted a notice: hiring porters to take people to Jingzhou. Twenty-five cash a day, meals provided.

Five cash less than Chang'an County.

Salang pushed his way to the foot of the notice and told the man writing down names that he was called Salang, on the Chang'an County register.

The man writing names looked up at him and said: "On the Chang'an County register, and you've come to Fengxiang to be a porter?"

Salang said: "Chang'an hired me to Fengxiang. Fengxiang hires me to Jingzhou."

"And after Jingzhou?"

"Jingzhou will hire again."

The man writing names laughed, and said that west of Jingzhou, nobody hired. West of Jingzhou was the Tibetans' country.

Salang did not take him up. He watched the man write his name in the book, and when he had written it, the man had him press a thumbprint. He pressed it; a smear of red stuck to his finger, and he wiped it on the front of his coat, and did not wipe it clean.

When he got back to the courtyard it was already dark. Jingfu was sitting at the foot of the wall, next to the old priest. The old priest had woken and was talking to Jingfu, in Sogdian, half of which Jingfu understood.

Salang did not go over to disturb them. He went to the heap of bundles, picked out Joseph's and Elijah's, then his own, and tied each one again. He tied them very tight, tucked the rope ends in, tucked them till not a bit could be seen.

When he had tied them, he took up the staff and stuck it through the strap of the bundles, iron tip upward.

Tomorrow there was more walking.
