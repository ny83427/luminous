# Volume One　Guo Ziyi

## Chapter 4　The Backward Thrust

> His art surpassed the three generations; his skill was broad and wholly sure.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

Seven days after Jingjing left, on the parade ground outside Lingzhou, someone wanted to fight Yisi.

The man who wanted to fight was Hun Shizhi.

Hun Shizhi was an old general of the Shuofang army, a little past forty, with a dark ruddy face and a great beard, and half his left ear missing, sliced off by a Tibetan in his early years. His forebears had been chieftains of the Hun tribe of the Tiele, who submitted to the Tang in the Zhenguan years, and ever since his family had served as soldiers in Shuofang, generation after generation. He had gone into battle at fifteen and fought for more than twenty years, and the sword scars and arrow scars on his body were past counting. He was tall, broad in the back and thick in the waist, a head higher than anyone in a crowd. When men in the camp talked of General Hun, the first thing they always mentioned was his lance: in battle, they said, he could pluck a man off his horse with one thrust, and before that man hit the ground his horse would already be at the next one. His son Hun Jian was twenty that year and also in the army, famous as a marksman. When the young soldiers in camp spoke of the Hun family, they knew only the young general's arrows, and hardly remembered that the old general could shoot too.

Hun Shizhi's wish to fight began with a bowl of wine.

---

That day Guo Ziyi was giving the generals a feast in the command tent, a send-off, he said, before the march east. When the wine had gone round three times, Guo Ziyi had Yisi brought into the tent, gave him a bowl of wine, and had him sit just below himself.

Yisi did not drink wine. He took the bowl, touched it to his lips, and set it down. He sat very straight, his hands on his knees, and said not one word.

The officers in the tent exchanged looks.

This foreign monk had been with the Shuofang army less than three months. In those three months, when Guo Ziyi left camp he went with him, when Guo Ziyi came back to his tent he came back with him, and someone had seen him come out of Guo Ziyi's sleeping tent in the middle of the night. He had no rank and no post, was not so much as a squad leader, and yet he stood closer to Guo Ziyi than many who had followed him ten years and more. Gossip had been going round the camp for some days. Some said he could read faces, some said he could tell fortunes, some said he had put some spell on Guo Ziyi. When this talk reached Guo Ziyi's ears, Guo Ziyi acted as though he had not heard it. Today Guo Ziyi had called him into the tent again and given him wine and seated him among the generals, and several faces in the tent did not look pleased.

Pugu Huai'en sat opposite, holding his wine bowl, studying Yisi through narrowed eyes. He was of the Pugu tribe of the Tiele, thirty or so, with a long face, high cheekbones and long narrow eyes, and when he looked at you it always seemed to be through something. He fought the hardest of anyone in the Shuofang army, had won the most merit, and was the hardest to get along with.

He took a drink of wine and suddenly spoke. "Master Yi. I hear that on the bank of the Yellow River you knocked down five Tongra soldiers on your own. Knocked them down, and didn't kill a single one?"

"No," said Yisi.

Pugu Huai'en smiled, a smile that hung only at the corners of his mouth and did not reach his eyes. "Now that's a rare thing. Ten-odd years I've been fighting in Shuofang, and I've heard of men killed, men wounded, men put to flight. I've never heard of anyone making a specialty of knocking men down and then helping them up again. A fight where no one dies—what kind of fight is that? That's a show. I've heard merchants from the west say they have a kind of monk out there who does tricks with swords and staves, sets up a pitch in the street for people to watch, a few coppers a show, and the more people watch, the livelier the tricks. You come from the west, Master Yi. Ever seen that sort?"

Some in the tent laughed along with him.

Guo Ziyi sat in the place of honour holding his wine bowl, and nothing could be read in his face. Yisi glanced once at Pugu Huai'en—a level glance, with no heat in it and no wish to please—and then looked away, down at the bowl of wine in front of him that he had not drunk.

Just then, Hun Shizhi spoke.

"Pugu, say less." He put down his bowl, turned, and saluted toward the head of the tent with his fists together. "Lord Guo, I have a request. I'd like to try myself against this Master Yi on the parade ground."

The tent went quiet all at once.

Guo Ziyi looked at him and asked, "Why?"

"It's not that I don't like the look of him," said Hun Shizhi. "My forebears were of the Hun tribe, General Pugu is of the Pugu tribe, more than half the men in this tent are Hu, and nobody holds it against anybody that he's Hu. What I hold against him is that he's a monk. Where there's fighting, you either kill or get killed; there's no third kind of man. If he won't kill, what does he live by in battle? If he gets himself killed, that doesn't matter. But he stands by Lord Guo every day, and when it comes to life or death, if he panics—what becomes of Lord Guo?"

It was roughly said, but nobody in the tent laughed.

There was another layer to it in Hun Shizhi's mind that he did not say. He had followed Guo Ziyi more than ten years and knew him for a man who was readier than anyone to trust, and slower than anyone to trust lightly; and now a foreign monk nobody knew anything about had, in three months, got as far as Guo Ziyi's bedside, and he could not rest easy about it. He did not want to hear other people say whether this foreign monk was a good man or a bad one, nor to hear the monk say so himself. He wanted to try him with his own hands, to see what the man really weighed, what he could do and what he could not.

He had thought Yisi would decline. The monk was a man of religion; he had only to plead his vows and nobody could say a word. In size, in strength, in years of rolling about on battlefields, he must know how things stood. And Guo Ziyi might not want one of his own men to make a fool of himself.

He turned and looked at Yisi. "Master Yi, I don't want your life, and I'm not trying to shame you. Whether you fight or not, you say. If you say no, I'll turn round and drink my wine, and never mention it again."

Yisi thought about it, rose, and bowed to Hun Shizhi. "I'll fight."

It was Hun Shizhi who was taken aback. He had not expected the monk to answer so readily, without so much as a "let me think about it." He measured Yisi with his eyes from head to foot again: not tall, but broad in the shoulder, standing very steady, his face showing neither fear nor bravado. And now he was less sure than before. Could this monk really be hiding some great skill that he had failed to see?

He gave a grunt. "Good. That's straight."

Guo Ziyi smiled at the head of the tent and put down his bowl. "So long as you two have agreed. Tomorrow on the parade ground. And when it's done, come back and go on drinking, and nobody bears a grudge."

---

At the hour of the Snake next morning, several hundred people stood around the parade ground.

Every soldier not on duty had come running to watch, three deep and more, and some had quietly opened a book on it. Simply backing General Hun to win, anybody could do that, and the bookmaker would lose the trousers off his back; so the book offered two bets. One was General Hun winning all three bouts, at evens, and men were fighting each other to get on it. The other was the foreign monk winning one bout, at eight to one, and only a scattering took it, for the fun of a flutter. Guo Ziyi sat on a folding stool at the edge of the ground with a few guards standing beside him, the generals seated on either side; Pugu Huai'en sat third on Guo Ziyi's left with a riding whip across his knees. Old Duan had gone west with Jingjing, and Yisi had not one familiar face beside him.

The provost of the central army stood in the middle of the ground and read out the rules at the top of his voice. They were the old rules of army reviews, which the men in camp had heard more times than they knew, and not long. The first bout was mounted: archery first and then the lance. The archery was only to show skill—hit or miss, fast or slow—and did not count for the result. The mounted bout was decided by three passes with the lance, the lance heads wrapped in cloth and dipped in lime; whoever had more lime on him lost. The second bout was on foot, with practice sword, leather shield and short mace, the mace head wrapped in felt and dipped in lime; a hit on a vital spot won. The third bout was wrestling and fists: a man thrown and pinned, or one who gave up, or a halt called by the provost, decided it. No strikes to the throat, no harming the eyes, no blows to the groin; whoever broke the rules got the rod first.

Both men agreed.

---

The first bout: mounted.

Archery first. On the range, as the custom was, three straw men had been set up in old leather armour, one straight ahead, one to the left, and one behind to the right. A rider had to hit all three in one run; the one behind to the right he could only reach once his horse had passed it, by turning in the saddle.

Yisi went first.

He rode the chestnut Guo Ziyi had given him, small, quick on its feet. Outside Balkh he had shot wild sheep with the hunters, on the merchant roads he had shot wolves, and later, looking at the road alone ahead of a caravan, when trouble came, whether he got away depended mostly on the bow in his hand; so his archery was something he had worked hard at. As he came up to the straw man in front he checked his horse slightly and put an arrow into the armour plate over its heart; the horse's head turned, and the one on the left was hit too, square in the throat. For the one behind to the right he had to twist his body round in the saddle. Turning to shoot backward was an old skill of the west—the old men of Balkh called it "the Parthian arrow," and he had learned it as a child. He reined in, waited for the horse to slow half a step, got his body steady in the twist, and only then loosed, and the arrow went into the straw man's side above the hip.

Three for three. The cheering at the edge of the ground was not small. The soldiers could see that the monk's archery was no show; most of the men in camp would have done well to put two of three. Only those who knew could also see that each time he shot, his horse had to wait for him.

Hun Shizhi went next.

He rode a black gelding, a big tall horse with a chest as broad as a drum. The horse came from the far end of the ground at full gallop and never checked once. On its back he took an arrow, nocked it and drew, as though doing something he had done all his life without a thought. The arrow in front went off and he had already turned to the left; the arrow on the left went off and the horse was already past; he twisted at the waist, his body turned more than halfway round to the right and behind, and the third arrow followed at once. Three arrows: one in the straw man's throat, one in the heart, one slanting in from the side through the gap of the armour below the ribs. None of the three was dead centre, but had they been living men, all three would have stayed down.

When he had shot he hung his bow beside the saddle and put out his hand, and it was already closing on the lance a guard held up to him, the horse still running, its head never once turning aside.

For a moment the edge of the ground was quiet; then the cheering burst out, louder by twice than what Yisi had got. Both had hit three for three; but in one case the horse had waited for the man, and in the other the man could not wait for the horse, and the arrows chased the galloping horse. The young soldiers had never seen the old general shoot; they slapped their thighs and shouted that so the young general's archery ran in the family. The old soldiers who had fought beside Hun Shizhi only bared their teeth, as if to say, *only now you know*. Hun Jian stood at the edge of the ground with his arms folded, looking a little proud and a little put out.

Pugu Huai'en sat on his stool, and the whip across his knees stopped swinging. He knew Hun Shizhi could shoot, but that was years ago; these past years Hun Shizhi had used only the lance in battle, and he had taken it that the old fellow had long since put the bow aside. He narrowed his eyes and looked at the three straw men for a while, then at Hun Jian, and the faint smile on his face had gone at some point without his noticing.

---

Then the lances.

Both lances were of white-wax wood, eighteen feet long, their iron heads taken off and replaced with cloth-wrapped wood, soaked in white lime.

Yisi had used a horseman's lance before. In his early years guarding caravans he had used one on horseback, and in these three months in Shuofang he had practised with the cavalry, putting a hole through a straw man with every thrust. But two horsemen charging each other, lance against lance, staking a life on a foot of reach—that he had never once done. Hun Shizhi had done it several hundred times at least. Before he mounted, Yisi knew this bout was lost.

At the provost's word, the two horses charged from opposite ends of the ground.

Hun Shizhi held his lance very steady, the shaft clamped under his right arm, the point low, aimed at Yisi's chest. Yisi watched that point, reckoning the reach, reckoning the run of the two horses, and reckoned that this thrust would not reach him; so he did not dodge, but only leaned his body a little to the left, meaning to brush past outside the lance point and strike back at Hun Shizhi's waist.

He reckoned wrong. In the instant the horses met, Hun Shizhi's waist went forward, his hips drove forward, and the lance shaft slid out more than a foot further from under his arm. The reach of a lance, it turned out, was not in the shaft. It was in the horse, in the waist, in that forward drive at the moment of the thrust. Yisi's left shoulder jolted, and the lime had already burst white on it, and the lance in his own hands had not so much as touched the hem of Hun Shizhi's coat.

One to nothing. A roar of cheering from the edge of the ground.

Yisi turned his horse and came back, his left shoulder numb. He did not charge Hun Shizhi head-on again. He had seen that the black horse, for all its size, turned clumsily, needing half a step more each time it was brought round; so he went for Hun Shizhi's right rear, cut a circle, then another, until the black horse was turning back and forth after him. At one moment, just as Hun Shizhi had brought his horse's head straight, Yisi was already at his right rear, no more than a lance length from his back. The few men at the edge of the ground who had backed the monk to win a bout jumped to their feet.

But to thrust at a man from his right rear, the lance head must first be swung from the right round to the front, and the butt drawn back as it went. For a man who had rolled through battles several hundred times that swing and draw came without thinking; Yisi had practised little, his hands were not used to it, and while his two hands were still shifting on the shaft, he was slow. Hun Shizhi did not look round, did not turn his horse again. He only gave the horse its head and let it leap forward two strides, leaned back in the saddle, turned the butt of the lance in his hands, and the lance head had already come round over his own left shoulder and touched Yisi on the right ribs.

Two to nothing.

Yisi's face felt hot. He knew he had not done anything wrong this time; the cut had been right, only slow. But slow was slow, and when you were slow, the lime was on you.

In the third pass, Hun Shizhi suddenly stopped fighting.

Yisi went for his right rear again, and on the second circle Hun Shizhi's black horse seemed unable to come round; he fended off twice, clumsily, all elbows, then turned his horse's head and made for the other end of the ground, the horse going fast, as if Yisi had harried him past bearing and he wanted to get clear and catch his breath and set himself again.

Everyone at the edge of the ground thought Hun Shizhi was pulling out.

Pugu Huai'en smiled first.

He sat on his stool, and the corner of his mouth went up, very slightly; nobody near him saw it. What he saw was Hun Shizhi's lance. The man was running forward, but the butt of the lance was pressed against his right hip, pressed there tight, and did not move. A man who really means to run draws his lance in forward.

Yisi did not see the butt of the lance. He saw only Hun Shizhi running, the heavy black horse running, and what he thought was: all those circles, and at last I've cut him loose. He already had lime on him in two places. If he did not give chase in this pass, then in three passes he would not have touched the other man once, and when the camp talked of this bout afterwards, all that would be left was the monk getting beaten. He pressed his heels into the horse. The chestnut was quick, and in a few strides they had closed. Yisi levelled his lance and was about to thrust at Hun Shizhi's back—

Hun Shizhi twisted violently at the waist.

He drove both feet hard into his stirrups, turned his whole upper body round to the right, both hands on the shaft, and drove the lance backward along his right hip, so that it came out reversed. All the force came from his waist and hips, and from the momentum of Yisi coming after him: Yisi's horse was charging forward, which meant he was delivering his own chest onto the point.

The lance head reached Yisi's chest, and in the last half foot Hun Shizhi drew his wrist back and took the force off it.

There was a *pfft*, and the lime exploded in a great white cloud across Yisi's chest.

Even with the force taken off, Yisi swayed in the saddle. It was as if someone had driven a pestle into his chest; a breath stuck inside him and would not come out. He did not fall from his horse. He reined in, looked down at the white mark across his chest, his face burning, and only after a while did the breath come out.

Three to nothing. The cheering at the edge of the ground shook the sky; those who had never seen that thrust shouted their throats raw. Pugu Huai'en cheered too. It did not sit well with him—once again it was Hun Shizhi showing off in front of Guo Ziyi—but that thrust had been truly well done, and good was good; his cheer was grudging, but it was not false. Hun Shizhi brought his horse round, grinning, his great beard bristling up.

Yisi rode over and stopped in front of him. "That thrust—what is it called?"

"The backward thrust," said Hun Shizhi.

"I've never seen it," said Yisi.

The whole edge of the ground burst out laughing.

"Course you haven't!" someone bawled. "Not many who've seen it are still standing!"

"Be glad you only got hit with it. You want to learn it too?"

"How many in this camp have seen General Hun's thrust with their own eyes? And what if they have—can they learn it?"

The soldiers all talked at once, laughing in a heap. And it was not all mockery. Of the officers in the Shuofang army, plenty had tried themselves against Hun Shizhi, and plenty had taken that thrust, and not one had learned it. Anyone could put on the look of a man in flight; the hard part was the turn. With the horse still running forward and the man thrusting backward, both feet must first be braced hard in the stirrups, braced as if nailed to the horse's sides, or else when the upper body twisted the man would be flung off with the lance. The waist must twist from the hips, not the shoulders; a thrust from the shoulders has no force in it, while a thrust from the hips has the other man's horse adding force behind it. The shaft must be laid early against the right hip, pressed there tight, so that at the turn it is only driven out, never pulled back—pull it back, and it is too slow. And then the moment itself: a step too early, and the other man has not closed and you cannot reach him; a step too late, and his lance is in your back first. Early or late differed by only one or two strides of a horse, and all depended on listening to the hoofbeats behind you, hearing where he had got to.

None of these secrets could be seen by watching. The watchers saw only that he suddenly turned and the lance was there; how the feet were braced, how the waist twisted, when to twist—without someone teaching you hand over hand, without falling off a horse dozens of times, it could not be learned. And even knowing he would turn, the pursuer had no good answer: if you did not chase, he got away, and got away to set himself and come again; if you chased, then at those one or two strides your horse could not stop. So Hun Shizhi never feared to use this move on the parade ground. In battle, though, he did not use it lightly. The backward thrust was a killing stroke to snatch life out of defeat; to feign flight was to sell your back to the enemy. If he chased too slowly, you turned and thrust at nothing; if he chased too fast and you turned a fraction slow, you died under his horse. In more than twenty years of fighting, he had used it in earnest in battle only a handful of times, each time when he had met an enemy too strong to beat and too fast to escape, at the very edge of life and death, gathering everything into one stroke and plucking the man off his horse with one thrust. Each time, afterwards, when he lay down at night, he broke out in a cold sweat.

The parade ground was different; nobody died there, and in ordinary bouts with the officers he used it often enough. Only he was a general, and what common soldier would dare go up and spar with him? So of the men in camp, many had heard of the backward thrust and few had seen it. Today, after the first two passes, he had seen that the monk's lance work was ordinary and that he himself had room to spare, and only then had he brought it out—partly to give the monk a shake, and partly because, before Guo Ziyi and the generals, he had wanted to show it off a little.

He had taught it hand over hand to only one man, his son Hun Jian, and while he taught it he had warned him again and again: don't use it lightly in battle; one slip, and you're a corpse under a horse.

And today Yisi had charged straight into it.

Hun Shizhi laughed along with everyone, his great beard shaking. Riding the laughter, he raised his voice: "Master Yi, could you learn it? If you really want to learn it, I'll teach you!"

It sounded like a joke, and the edge of the ground roared again; everyone in camp knew General Hun had not taught that move even to his own guards. But as he laughed, Hun Shizhi kept his eyes fixed on Yisi's face. He wanted to see whether the monk, with several hundred people laughing at him, would be angry, or ashamed, or would climb up the pole he had been offered and beg to be his pupil on the spot.

Yisi's face was still red and his chest still tight. He did not laugh with them, and he was not angry. He only said: "Whether I could learn it or not, I'd know once I'd learned it. Not today. Today there are two bouts still."

When he had said it he turned his horse back to the edge of the ground, dismounted, handed the lance back to a soldier, and bent his head to beat the lime off himself. One patch on the left shoulder, one on the right ribs, a great patch on the chest; he beat at them for a long while and could not get them clean.

---

For a while no one on the ground spoke.

The men who had backed General Hun to win all three bouts were already counting their money. Guo Ziyi sat on his stool; he was not angry and did not say anything for Yisi either, his face still mild, looking at the sand in the middle of the ground that the hooves had churned up. Pugu Huai'en took the bowl of water a guard brought him, drank a mouthful, said something to the man beside him, and the man laughed.

Hun Shizhi got down from his horse, handed his lance to a guard, and wiped his sweat with a towel, joking with the guard as he wiped. "Those two cuts the monk made at my right rear weren't bad. He had my Blackie turning till his head spun. I'll have to give him an extra handful of beans tonight." The guard laughed, and so did he. Three passes and not a touch on him; inside, he had relaxed, relaxed so much he did not notice it himself.

As he wiped, he glanced over toward Guo Ziyi.

Guo Ziyi was not looking at him.

Hun Shizhi looked across the ground, and saw Pugu Huai'en smiling. The towel stopped in his hand. Only now did it occur to him that in this bout, what the several hundred people at the edge of the ground were watching was not only the foreign monk, but the man who kept the foreign monk beside him. He had won the first bout cleanly; that was enough. If the second and third went on hammering down like this, one blow after another, by the end it would not be the monk's face that was hurt but Guo Ziyi's, and that mouth of Pugu's would have a few more things to say. He had asked to fight in order to test a man, not to knock the props out from under Guo Ziyi.

There was something else too: he wanted to see for himself. Five Tongra soldiers knocked down on the bank of the Yellow River and not one dead—he had heard it in camp more than once, and each time he had wondered: one small shield, one short mace, how had that laid five men on the ground and left them all still breathing? On horseback was his ground; there he could not see the monk's skill. On foot, with the weapons the monk was used to, he would take his time and have a proper look.

Hun Shizhi tossed the towel into his guard's arms and went to get a sword.

---

The second bout: on foot.

Hun Shizhi used a long-hilted practice sword of wrought iron, its edge ground blunt, the blade a good stretch longer than an ordinary sword, and the hilt long enough for two hands; it would draw no blood, but it could break bones. Yisi used the weapons he was used to: in his left hand a small round leather shield, in his right a short mace, its head the size of a fist, wrapped in three layers of felt and dipped in lime.

As soon as the two faced each other, those at the edge of the ground could see the difference in reach. Hun Shizhi's sword was long; standing still, he could reach Yisi with its point. Yisi's mace was short; to hit Hun Shizhi he would have to get inside that sword.

Hun Shizhi did not hurry to cut. He held the sword up and shifted his feet slowly, still with the smile from before on his lips, now and then sending out a cut, a long one, the stroke swung wide, as if he were sketching it, or waiting. Yisi kept his shield up and turned with him step by step. When a cut came, he met it with the face of the shield at a slant and let the blade slide off along it. A blade sliding off was not heavy; it gave his wrist a slight jar, and was gone.

At the seventh or eighth exchange Hun Shizhi cut at air, and his blade swung out wide, and a space opened under his right ribs.

Yisi saw it. His feet shifted, and he did not go in.

He was thinking of a lance laid against a right hip.

Hun Shizhi drew the sword back, sent it out again, swung wide again, and again there was a gap, bigger than before. Again Yisi saw it, and again did not go in, but stepped back half a pace.

Someone at the edge of the ground laughed aloud. Hun Shizhi smiled too, and this time the smile was a little different, the corners of his mouth turned down. He thought: one backward thrust has scared the monk so badly he won't come in with the door wide open. He hoisted the sword onto his shoulder, then brought it down again, and said: "Master Yi, you hold that shield up nice and tight, but if you just hold it up and don't move, do you mean to wear me down till dark? The door's open for you. Come on in."

Yisi did not answer.

Hun Shizhi sent out another cut. This one he swung wider than either before; he meant to see once and for all whether the monk dared come in, and how. As the blade went out his eyes were still on Yisi's feet. But this time the blade swung too far; his body leaned out after it, his weight came down on the outer leg, and when he went to bring the blade back he was half a beat slow.

Yisi saw that this time was different. The two times before, when Hun Shizhi's blade swung out, his feet had been nailed to the ground. This time, his feet had followed the sword.

Yisi went in.

He hurled himself forward behind the shield, his left shoulder against its face, and the face of the shield came down on Hun Shizhi's two forearms where they gripped the sword, pinning the long blade outside. Hun Shizhi tried to step back and bring the blade in, the sand slid under his foot, he went back half a step, and Yisi was already in his arms, close enough to smell the wine on his beard. The short mace in his right hand came out from under the shield—not smashing, not swinging, only driving forward—and stopped against Hun Shizhi's right side below the ribs.

A white patch of lime was printed on his armour.

The provost called out: "The monk scores!"

Hun Shizhi looked down at the white patch below his ribs, then up at Yisi. Yisi had already backed out, shield still up, mace still in his hand, breathing hard.

For a moment the edge of the ground was still; then all at once it was chaos. The men who had backed three straight wins had lost every coin in one stroke. Some cursed on the spot—cursed the monk, cursed General Hun, cursed their own itchy hands. The few who had backed the monk to win a bout leapt into the air, and one short, thickset fellow laughed loudest of all, laughing and slapping his thighs so that the men beside him shied away. The bookmaker's face had gone white; at eight to one, he had a good deal to pay out this time.

Hun Shizhi did not laugh. He rested the point of his sword on the ground and stood for a while.

Yisi went up to him, lowered his mace, and said quietly: "The general let me in just now."

"I let you get close. I didn't let you hit me," said Hun Shizhi. He paused, then added gruffly: "Don't go saying that too often. Say it too often and it sounds like I can't even land a blow on a monk."

With that he handed the sword to a guard and went to take off his armour. He tugged at it twice and could not get it undone, and swore at the guard for tying it too tight.

Guo Ziyi had not moved on his stool. He could see that Hun Shizhi had not put his full strength into this bout: the first two cuts were teasing, the third was looking. He could also see that the last stroke had not been handed to Yisi by anybody. He said nothing.

Pugu Huai'en put down his bowl of water. He looked at Yisi, looked at him a long time: how he breathed, whether his hand shook when he handed the mace back. After the mounted bout he had dismissed this monk in his mind—a showman, no more. Now he took those words back, took them back into himself, and did not say so.

---

The third bout: wrestling and fists.

Both men took off their armour and their shirts and stood facing each other in the middle of the sand in nothing but their trousers. As they stood, the edge of the ground laughed. Hun Shizhi was more than half a head taller than Yisi, with a chest like a door, arms thicker than Yisi's calves, and was at least thirty or forty jin heavier.

In the first bout, mounting, Hun Shizhi had been smiling; in the second, taking up the sword, he had been smiling too. This time he took off his belt, put it back on, cinched it tight, and did not smile.

Yisi saw that cinching of the belt and knew that in this bout the man meant business.

Hun Shizhi was Tiele, and had grown up wrestling. He crouched low, spread his hands, and came on step by step. Yisi would not let himself be caught. He kept moving, circling to either side. Hun Shizhi lunged; Yisi slipped aside and punched him in the face in passing. Hun Shizhi lunged again; Yisi slipped again and punched again, in the ribs. After a few rounds there was a red patch above Hun Shizhi's brow, but he did not so much as blink, and kept coming on, step by step.

Yisi's fists landing on him were like fists on a great vat full of water: a thud, and the vat did not stir.

After lunging at air twice, Hun Shizhi stopped lunging. He no longer chased Yisi round, but only pressed forward, a step to the left, a step to the right. When Yisi circled left, Hun Shizhi's left hand was already spread there waiting; when Yisi circled right, Hun Shizhi's right foot had already stepped half a pace across. Yisi circled and circled, and the space left for his feet grew narrower and narrower, until at last he could hear behind him the breathing of the wall of men at the edge of the ground.

Only then did Hun Shizhi spring.

Yisi slipped left, and slipped too slowly. Hun Shizhi's left hand caught his right wrist first, and his right arm came round Yisi's waist; the two arms closed like an iron hoop. He straightened his back and lifted Yisi's whole body clean off the ground, leaned back, and threw him down hard on the sand.

Yisi's eyes went black. His spine hit the sand and every organ in him jolted. Before he could get his breath, Hun Shizhi was already on him, lying across his chest, one arm locked under his neck, one knee braced against his hip, the whole weight of the man on top of him.

Yisi struggled once and could not move him. He drew his legs up, meaning to push outward; he pushed once, and Hun Shizhi's body sank down with his legs and pressed even more solidly than before. He twisted his waist, trying to turn on his side; halfway through, he could twist no further. What lay on his chest did not feel like a man. It felt like the stone roller of a threshing floor.

He could not breathe, and his face went dark red.

The provost came up, bent down and looked, saw that Yisi could get no strength into his hands or feet, straightened up, and waved his hand. "Stop! This bout to General Hun!"

Hun Shizhi let go of him at once, got up, and held out a hand. Yisi looked at the hand, and took it, and Hun Shizhi hauled him up with one pull, so that he staggered as he found his feet.

The provost turned toward the stool and called out in a loud voice: "Three bouts finished. General Hun wins two, the monk wins one!"

Guo Ziyi nodded.

---

The crowd at the edge of the ground broke up. The men who had backed three straight wins and lost their money were still shouting that the second bout shouldn't count, that General Hun had let him in, and nearly came to blows with the bookmaker before the officer of the camp patrol drove them off. The wind came off the Yellow River and set the flags on the parade ground flapping and cracking. The sand was trampled all over with the two men's footprints, every which way, so that you could no longer tell whose were whose.

The short, thickset fellow who had laughed loudest in the crowd—past forty, thick at the waist, thick at the neck, his face burned dark red by the wind—pushed his way up to Yisi now and saluted with his fists together. "Foreign master, I owe you thanks! My name's Tie, Tie Wanquan—they call me Old Iron in camp—I eat my rice in the scouts' battalion. Three hundred coppers, on you to win one bout, at eight to one."

Yisi asked him how he had known he could win a bout.

"Didn't know. Took a flutter." Old Iron laughed, then lowered his voice. "Not all a flutter, though. Yesterday when you came out of the tent I took a look at you. That shield of yours—the leather round the rim's worn shiny, and the face is all over old marks from real blades. The callus in the web of your thumb comes from gripping a mace haft, not from drawing a bow. And when you walk, your feet make no sound. On horseback, or bare hands, I wouldn't say. But on foot, with your own two tools, you'd hold out for one bout at least." He smiled as he talked, but his eyes kept moving over Yisi's hands and feet. "Tonight there's mutton behind the flatbread shop in town—I'm treating the scouts, and you're the first one invited. And if we ever go out together after this, you look your way and I'll look mine." He turned round and shouted to a long thin fellow, "Feng Nine, go round them up!" Then he jerked his chin at Yisi. "Lord Guo wants you. Go on over first. We'll keep you some mutton."

Guo Ziyi had not left. He had Hun Shizhi and Yisi brought before his stool, looked first at the white patch on Yisi's chest and then at the red mark on Hun Shizhi's brow, and said with a smile: "Old Hun, you've gone to a lot of trouble—knocked the man off his horse onto the ground, gone from sword and shield to fists—you've had a good look. So tell me. What do you make of him?"

Hun Shizhi had his coat thrown over his shoulders and had not got his breath back. He thought, and said: "Lord Guo, I'll tell you straight. On horseback, no good. He can use a lance, but against straw men; in battle, the other man's lance would be in him while he was still reckoning the reach. Bare-handed, no good either. Too light in the bone—picking him up is like picking up a sheaf of wheat. Close in with short weapons, he's good enough. Quick eyes, and no small courage. Only short of strength."

"And as a scout?"

"That's enough," said Hun Shizhi. "So long as he doesn't do anything stupid and get into a slugging match, he'll get out, and he'll get back."

Guo Ziyi nodded, asked nothing more, rose and went back to his tent.

Yisi stood there holding his chest. The white patch of lime was still on it; underneath there must be a great bruise, for it hurt every time he breathed.

Hun Shizhi looked at him, as though something had come back to him, and said: "Master Yi, yesterday in the tent you agreed so quick I thought you must be hiding some tremendous trick. I didn't sleep easy all night. Today we fought: on horseback you didn't touch me once, bare-handed I pinned you so you couldn't move, and the second bout, if I hadn't been teasing you on purpose…" Here he cleared his throat first and did not go on. "What I want to ask is—you knew you couldn't beat me. Why did you agree so fast yesterday? A monk who's left the world, weren't you afraid people would laugh at you for not knowing how high heaven is?"

"The Tang people are used to calling us foreign monks, and this humble monk calls himself so too," said Yisi. "But in fact our way is not the same as the Buddha's. We don't leave home. I'm a priest at the monastery, and may marry. I had a wife. She died of illness a few years ago. And I have a son, who left for the west seven days ago."

Hun Shizhi stared at him, as if hearing for the first time that there were monks like that under heaven. He opened his mouth, and then suddenly laughed. "Take a wife, have a son, and still call it a monk? Your monks out there have an easy time of it." He stroked his beard and thought, and the more he thought the funnier he found it. "So the not killing—that's not one of your way's vows either?"

"That is my own affair," said Yisi.

Hun Shizhi looked at his face, and asked no further.

Yisi's Chinese came slowly, each word bitten off clean before he let it out. "The general asked me just now why I agreed. I knew I'd lose. On horseback and bare-handed, I knew I'd lose. But Lord Guo has me follow him, and the men in camp don't know why Lord Guo wants me, or what I can do and what I can't. If I say it myself, I can say it a hundred times and no one will believe it. Fight once, lose or win, and everyone has seen it with their own eyes; then they know that what Lord Guo wants of me was never the general's work, and after that there's no more need to guess."

"Getting beaten in front of several hundred men—you weren't afraid of the shame?"

"I was," said Yisi. "But if I had refused yesterday, the doubt in your hearts wouldn't have gone away by itself."

Hun Shizhi said nothing, and it was a while before he spoke again. "Then I'll leave you a word of my own too, and don't take it ill. When it really comes to the front—horse against horse, lance against lance—you can't protect Lord Guo."

"I know that," said Yisi. "I can't."

"Then what do you follow him round all day for?"

"To look at the road," said Yisi. "The road ahead, the people ahead, the hoofprints on the ground, the footprints by the water. I look at them for Lord Guo."

Hun Shizhi looked at him a long time, and said: "Then look at the road true. If you get one step wrong over there, all of us following behind will be jumping into the pit one after another."

With that he turned and went. Hun Jian was waiting for him at the edge of the ground, and father and son went off toward the camp, one behind the other.

Pugu Huai'en was the last to rise. He glanced toward Yisi, then looked away; what he was looking at was the backs of Hun Shizhi and his son. The young one's back was already as broad as his father's.

---

That evening, Hun Jian went looking for his father and found him sitting alone on a camp stool outside his tent, rubbing his wrist and drinking, and laughing as he drank.

Hun Jian asked: "Pa, who won today, really?"

"I won two bouts," said Hun Shizhi. "Your pa's getting old. Two out of three—it'll do."

Hun Jian squatted down beside him. "The whole camp's talking about those three arrows of yours, Pa. Saying my archery runs in the family."

"It does run in the family." Hun Shizhi looked at him sidelong. "You shoot straighter. A copper coin at a hundred paces—your pa can't match you. But the day you can loose three arrows in one breath at a gallop, every one of them where it's meant to go, and still catch a lance in your hand when you're done—then come and talk to your pa."

Hun Jian was not convinced and muttered something, then remembered something. "Pa, didn't you warn me over and over not to use the backward thrust lightly? Today in front of several hundred people you used it as if it were nothing."

"Nobody dies on the parade ground," said Hun Shizhi. "Battle is battle. In battle, when that thrust goes out, either he dies or I do. Every time your pa uses it, it takes a year off his life. You remember this: till you're at that point, don't do what your pa did today."

Hun Jian pursed his lips and did not dare talk back again. He asked instead: "The second bout—did you let him in, Pa?"

"Let him in half a step."

"So you did let him in."

"You, boy—you've got your mother's temper. Have to pick everything apart till it's clear." Hun Shizhi took a drink. "I let him get close. I didn't let him put the mace into my ribs. I opened the door, opened it a crack; if he could get through it, that was his own skill. Any other man in camp, the door open, might not have dared come in, and if he'd come in might not have landed a blow."

Hun Jian said nothing more. After a while he asked: "Then what are you laughing about?"

Hun Shizhi drank again, and did not answer at once. The clouds at the edge of the sky had been burned red by the sun, the cooking smoke was rising in the camp, and far off someone was shouting as he watered the horses.

"I was afraid he'd stand in the wrong place," he said. "Now I know."

"Where should he stand?"

"Ten li ahead of the line."
