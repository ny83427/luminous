# Volume Twelve　Jade Gate

## Chapter 83　Salang

> A luminous star announced the blessing; Persians saw its brightness and came with tribute.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

On the seventh day after Lingbao was buried, Salang and Elijah left Balkh. Fahd saw them to the city's west gate. Salang carried that oilcloth bundle on his back; in it were Samaan's nine account books, Luo Haoxin's sheet, Jingjing's slip, Official Pei's register, Lingbao's little book, and Elder Alopen's few bark-skin cases; the bundle was heavy, and he carried it bent over. On his chest was still bound that clay jar, Old Zhu's; the oilcloth over its mouth had been changed twice, once at Kashgar and once at the Stone Tower; the oilcloth was changed but not the jar; that ring of bright marks Old Zhu's hands had worn on the jar's body his own hands had worn brighter. Elijah rode the yak that had carried them all the way from Kashgar; the yak was old and went slowly; Elijah said it didn't matter; he was old too.

Fahd stood at the city gate and said: of Milis's family, no one had come back in more than eighty years; this time one came back, came back and is buried here. He said every year from now on he'd pull the weeds on those four graves, pull them till he couldn't, and then have his grandson pull them.

---

West of Balkh they travelled a month and more and reached Merv. Merv was Elijah's home. He was born in Merv, went out at a little past twenty with Elder David, forty-three years ago. The day he entered the city he stood at the city gate looking at the gateway, at the walls, at the river below the walls; the river was the same river; the walls were a step lower than when he left; of the people in the gateway he knew not one. He said when he left there was a wooden board hanging over the lintel of this gate, and what was written on it he had forgotten; now the board was gone.

His home was in the east of the city; he went to find it, found the lane—the lane was still there—his family's house was gone, built over with someone else's house, where a family lived who spoke Persian; he asked them: the family who used to live here? The family said they didn't know; they'd lived here twenty years. He stood a while at the lane's mouth, and left.

Joseph's family's graves were behind the church outside the city. Merv's church was bigger than Balkh's—a metropolitan's church, its hall of brick, two storeys, a cross on the hall's roof; the burial ground behind it was big too, hundreds of graves; Elijah searched a whole morning and found them. Joseph's Pa and Ma, two graves side by side, names cut on the stones and years beneath the names; Joseph's Ma had died late, in the tenth year of Yuanhe, when Joseph was in Chang'an and didn't know.

Elijah knelt before the graves. He spoke in Syriac, saying what Joseph had asked him to say; he said Joseph went half the way, got to Shazhou, died at Shazhou, and is buried on the sand east of the town of Shazhou; the other half, he goes for him; today he's arrived. He said Joseph lived thirty-six years in Chang'an and argued with the monastery all his life—argued over the Tang hymns, argued over characters in the scriptures—and at the very last he said what had gone far wasn't the Chang'an church; it was anyone who could put bread in people's palms. When he had said it he stood before the graves, and Salang stood behind him.

Elijah stayed half a month in Merv. The metropolitan of the church at Merv heard people had come from Chang'an and called them to see him, asked about the affairs of Chang'an—asked about the stele, about the church, about morning devotion, about the sending back; Elijah told it thing by thing; when he came to Jingfu, he said he was the leader of the Chang'an church, had never had hands laid on him, and had taken two children over the Tianshan northward with the Uighurs; the metropolitan had his scribe enter these words in the church's register, and when they were entered said that the Patriarchate had had no sure word from Sinistan for decades; he said: go to Baghdad, see the Patriarch, and tell the Patriarch these things.

---

West of Merv was the great road of Khurasan. Outside the city of Nishapur, Salang saw a shrine by the road. The shrine was on a little hill outside the city, built of mud brick, square, not large; from the shrine's roof rose a wisp of smoke, blue, fine and thin, going straight up; when the wind blew it bent, and straightened again. People on the road said it was a shrine of those who worship fire; in the city now most believed in the Tazi's teaching, and the fire-worshippers were few; but that shrine still stood, and its fire had not gone out in hundreds of years.

Salang stood by the road looking at that wisp of smoke, felt the clay jar on his chest, and told Elijah he was going up. At the shrine's door sat an old man dressed all in white—white robe, white cap, and from beneath the cap a white cloth hanging over his mouth, for the fire-keeper fears his breath may pollute the fire. The old man saw Salang come up, stood, blocked the door, and said something in Persian, which Salang couldn't understand. Elijah behind him said a few words to him in Persian: this man comes from the east, from Sinistan, from Chang'an; he carries something—the fire of a shrine in Chang'an; the Chang'an shrine was sealed and its fire went out; its keeper put the ash in a jar and entrusted it to him to bring west, to a place with fire.

The old man heard, and looked at Salang, at the clay jar on his chest. He pulled down the white cloth over his mouth a little and asked where Chang'an was. Elijah said in the east, very far; if the caravans go well, more than half a year; they had stopped and gone, stopped and gone, and travelled five years. The old man asked again: the shrine in Chang'an—who lit its fire? Salang said a man of Kang, surnamed Zhu, his ancestors brought it from Kang; it burned in Chang'an over a hundred years and went out on the twenty-third of the eighth month of the fifth year of Huichang; the keeper put it out himself with an iron plate, and wouldn't let anyone pour water on it.

Elijah translated. When the old man had heard, he stood at the door with both hands joined at his breast and murmured a few words, and when he had murmured them he did not move from the door; he held out both hands and said something to Elijah. Elijah said: he says that in the fire-chamber those of other teachings may not enter; he says give him the jar, and he'll carry it in for you.

Salang untied the clay jar from his chest. The cord was in a hard knot; he tried several times before he undid it; the oilcloth over the jar's mouth had been changed twice; the ash in the jar was nearly five years old—from Chang'an to Jingzhou, from Jingzhou to Shazhou, from Shazhou to Xizhou, from Xizhou to Kashgar, over the Pamirs, to Balkh, to Merv, to here; he had carried it bound to his chest all the way, and had not once opened it. He held the jar and handed it over; the old man took it, held it, turned, lifted the curtain hanging over the door, and went in.

Salang stood outside the door, and Elijah stood beside him. The curtain hung down; inside it was very dark; nothing could be seen; that wisp of blue smoke on the shrine's roof, fine and thin, went straight up, and he watched that wisp of smoke. After about as long as it takes to recite a psalm, the smoke stirred, leaned to one side, straightened again, thicker than before, and deeper in colour, as if someone had put something on the fire. Salang watched it, watched it go straight up again.

The old man came out holding the clay jar, and the jar was empty. He gave the jar back to Salang and said something to Elijah, and Elijah translated for Salang: he says, the fire has come home. Salang took the empty jar and held it. He told the old man: that man surnamed Zhu stayed at Jingzhou, on the Tang's border, farming; he's past seventy; he said he couldn't reach a place with fire, so he asked him to go; today he's arrived; he can't go back to Jingzhou, he can't tell Old Zhu; if the old man is willing, would he say a line for Old Zhu. Elijah translated; the old man nodded and murmured a few more words.

When Salang left the shrine's door, he did not throw away the empty jar; he wrapped it in the oilcloth and bound it back on his chest. Elijah asked him: what are you still binding an empty jar on for? Salang said he was used to it; with nothing on his chest he couldn't walk steady.

---

They reached Baghdad that autumn. Baghdad was a round city, very large, smaller than Chang'an, more crowded than Chang'an; the Tigris flowed past the city's edge, with bridges, boats and waterwheels on the river and houses on both banks—brick houses, flat-roofed, dates drying on the roofs. Of the city's speech Salang understood not a word—Tazi, Persian, Syriac, Greek, stirred together, more mixed than the West Market at Chang'an.

The Patriarchate was in a quarter on the east bank of the Tigris, not inside the Round City, called the Quarter of the Romans; many who believed in Daqin lived in it; there was a great church, and beside the church the Patriarch's court. Elijah led Salang there, and they stood at the court's gate; the one keeping the gate was a young man whose beard had just come in, who asked whom they sought; Elijah said the Patriarch, and said they came from Sinistan, from Chang'an. The young man was taken aback and said: Sinistan? He went in, and after the time of a meal an old deacon came out, about fifty, thin, hunchbacked, a knotted cord in his hand, a honey-coloured patch sewn on the shoulder of his robe. Only later did Salang see that a wooden board had newly been nailed on the court's gate with a devil's face carved on it; Elijah asked, and the old deacon said the Caliph had ordered it this year: the houses of those not of the Tazi's teaching must all have one nailed on their doors, and patches sewn on their robes. That day the old deacon stood at the gate looking at these two—an old brother in a black robe and a porter who looked like a *hu*, a jar bound to his chest and a great bundle on his back—and asked Elijah: you come from Chang'an?

Elijah said yes. He said he was called Elijah, of Merv, who went to Sinistan with the metropolitan David in the second year of Yuanhe and lived thirty-six years in Chang'an; in the fifth year of Huichang the Chang'an church was abolished; they were sent back, travelled five years, and came here. He pointed to Salang and said: this one is called Salang; his grandfather's father was called Sargis, a guard of the church who went east from the Patriarchate with the seven elders in the first year of Zhide.

When the old deacon heard *Sargis*, his eyes moved. He said: Sargis—he knew of him; in the Patriarchate's library there's a register of names recording those who went east with the seven elders; of the twenty-four guards, the first is Sargis. He said: come in.

---

The Patriarch was ill that year, ill all summer, and not yet better when autumn came; he did not see them. The one who saw them was the old deacon; he was called Matthew, the Patriarch's scribe, in charge of the Patriarchate's library. He led them into the library, a long narrow room with shelves on both sides, and on the shelves scrolls, registers, leather-bound account books, row upon row, piled to the ceiling. He had a lamp lit, had them sit, and listened to Elijah.

Elijah told it from the beginning. He told of the Chang'an church, of the stele, of morning devotion, of the gruel shed, of the corpse cart, of the things of the Huichang years; told how the stele was buried, how the clerk of the Office of Receptions asked *what country are you*, how they went out of the Kaiyuan Gate; told that Joseph died at Shazhou, that He Siniang died on the Pamirs, that Lingbao died at Balkh and was buried beside the priest Milis's grave. At the very end he said there was one more in the north, called Jingfu, the leader of the Chang'an church, who had never had hands laid on him, who took two children to the steppe north of the Tianshan; there were a few households of Uighurs there who believed in Daqin, with no priest and no bread; he went, empty-handed. He told it two evenings, and the scribe Matthew listened two evenings, listening, sometimes noting a few lines with his brush, sometimes laying the brush down and gripping his knotted cord.

Salang untied that oilcloth bundle from his back, set it on the table, opened it, and took the things out one by one. The scribe Matthew picked up Alopen's bark-skin case, opened it, looked, and his hand shook; he said these are the Patriarchate's scriptures, of the batch that went east in the Zhenguan years; the library's register has it—*Alopen took so many rolls of scripture east*; more than two hundred years, and they've come back. He picked up Samaan's account books, opened them, looked at a few pages, and said: Samaan—he knew of him too; he went east with Elder Azarel in the first year of Baoying, keeper of the rule; the Patriarchate's register has him too.

Last of all Salang took out Lingbao's little book. He opened it, turned to that page, and as he opened it that mulberry leaf fell out, dried, yellow, onto the table. He picked up the leaf and set it aside, and pointed to that line on the page—Syriac letters, Lingbao's hand; he could not read them; he knew what that line said; Lingbao had told him. He said this line says: *Ninth month of the third year of Changqing: Guangzheng goes to the Patriarchate to ask for a bishop.* Below it, an empty line. He said the Great Worthy Lingbao, dying, told him to ask: did Guangzheng reach the Patriarchate, and after he reached it, what happened?

---

The scribe Matthew looked at that line and said: Guangzheng—let him think. He stood, went to the shelves, searched among row upon row of registers, searched a whole watch, and found a register—the Patriarch's book of events, recording each year's great matters of the Patriarchate; he turned it, turned to the Dahe years, turned very slowly, page by page, came to a page, and stopped.

He read. It was Syriac; he read a sentence and Elijah translated a sentence. *In the year of the Greeks one thousand one hundred and thirty-nine, there came a man of Sinistan from Chang'an named Guangzheng, baptismal name Enoch, bearing a letter of Adam, priest of Chang'an, together with the register of the Chang'an church. He said that since Jingjing's death Chang'an has had no bishop; the laying on of hands is broken; of priests there is but one; he asked that a bishop be sent, or that hands be laid on the bearer of the letter. At that time the Patriarch's throne was vacant. Not long after, the Patriarch Giwargis succeeded, read his letter, and judged that a bishop may not lightly be sent, nor be given to one come from afar; he permitted the Patriarchate to choose a man, to wait till such a man be found; and since Sinistan was far and without anyone, by exception kept Enoch at the Patriarchate to learn the scriptures and canons, ordained him deacon, and commanded him to carry a letter and take ship east from Basra, to report to Chang'an.*

He turned a few pages further, came to a page, and stopped again. *In the autumn of the year of the Greeks one thousand one hundred and forty-four, a letter came from Basra, saying that Enoch, deacon of Sinistan, waited two years at Basra for a ship; ships repeatedly could not be had; he fell sick of a fever and died at Basra, and was buried outside the city of Basra; before he died, Enoch entrusted a letter to a sailor going east, to be sent to Chang'an.*

When Elijah had translated it, the flame of the lamp lit in the library stood straight up. Salang sat by the table looking at that page; of the letters on that page he could read not one; he looked. He said: Guangzheng's letter arrived. He said in the autumn of the fourth year of Kaicheng the Li family's Observatory Gentleman brought it, an oilcloth packet, the oilcloth sewn by Guangzheng's Ma, and in it a piece of parchment with three sentences on it: *Arrived. The Patriarch consents. Waiting for a ship.* He said the Great Worthy Lingbao that day felt that parchment and said: this is something to hope for.

The scribe Matthew heard it, bowed his head, and looked at that page. He said the autumn of one thousand one hundred and forty-four he had entered himself; that year he was a little past thirty, newly made scribe; that letter from Basra he had opened himself; when he had entered it, he didn't know whether Chang'an knew or not. He said: Chang'an knew; Chang'an kept his letter and waited a dozen-odd years.

---

Salang pushed Lingbao's little book across to the scribe Matthew. He said the Great Worthy Lingbao said that if they could learn what became of Guangzheng, someone should write it under that line. He said he couldn't write Syriac letters; he begged the scribe to write it.

The scribe Matthew looked at the little book, at that line—*Ninth month of the third year of Changqing: Guangzheng goes to the Patriarchate to ask for a bishop*—and below it an empty line, empty and neat, the paper yellow, twenty-seven years. He took up the pen, dipped it in ink, and on that empty line wrote, wrote very slowly, wrote in Syriac letters. Elijah, beside him, read it out word by word:

*In the year of the Greeks one thousand one hundred and forty-four, Enoch Guangzheng, ordained deacon, waiting for a ship, died at Basra, and was buried outside the city of Basra.* When Elijah had read it, he borrowed the pen from the scribe Matthew and at the end of the line added a small sentence in Tang characters: *about the sixth or seventh year of Dahe.* When the scribe Matthew had finished writing, he laid down the pen and looked at that line, waiting for the ink to dry. When the ink was dry, he picked up that dried mulberry leaf, pressed it back in that page, and closed the book.

He pushed the book back to Salang. Salang took it, did not tuck it into his robe, but pushed it back again, and said: let this book be kept with those nine account books, left at the Patriarchate. He stood up and told the scribe Matthew: his grandfather's father went out from here, a guard of the church; he'd like to stay; the Patriarchate's gate—let him keep it. The scribe Matthew looked at him and nodded. That evening a low stool appeared at the Patriarchate's court gate, and Salang sat on it, leaning on the doorframe, that emptied clay jar bound to his chest, a wooden staff across his knees, bought in the market at Merv, shoulder-high, its head not iron-shod.

---

Note: In the mid-ninth century the Patriarch of the Church of the East resided at Baghdad; Giwargis II reigned c. 828–831 and Abraham II c. 837–850. A bishop of the Church of the East had to be consecrated by the Patriarch together with the bishops, and was not given to one newly entered into orders; though after Timothy I there was the title of a metropolitan of Sinistan, no certain sending of envoys is found in the mid-ninth century. Basra was the port at the head of the Gulf, and taking passage from Basra by way of the Lion Kingdom to Guangzhou was the ordinary sea route of the Tang. Around Nishapur in the ninth century there were still Zoroastrians and fire temples. Guangzheng's (Enoch's) ordination as deacon and death at Basra are the novel's invention.
