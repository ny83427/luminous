# Volume Six　The Shuofang Army

## Chapter 36　A Single Rider

> When Lord Guo Ziyi, President of the Secretariat and Prince of Fenyang, first took command of the army in Shuofang, Suzong had Yisi go with him on campaign.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

Yisi took six days to get back from Mingsha to Jingyang. The road back was easier than the road there. There were fewer Tibetan patrols, and the few parties he met were all heading back, their horses loaded with plunder, going at an easy pace, as if the war were already over. Only outside Jingyang did he learn that the war was not over. The plain outside the town was all tents, the Uighurs' to the north, the Tibetans' to the west, patch after patch, spread out further than the eye could see; at night when the campfires were lit, the town of Jingyang was like a stone steeping in a pot of fire. Guo Ziyi had a little over ten thousand men.

Yisi went into the headquarters tent and reported, very briefly: the flux, the red flux; he could not be saved; he had died just before dawn; his retainers had burned the body and buried the bones by the river at Mingsha and trampled the ground flat. When Guo Ziyi had heard it, he asked only one thing: had he been willing to drink? Yisi said he had drunk once, and afterwards would not. Guo Ziyi nodded and told him to go and rest. Yisi did not go and rest, but went out of the tent and stood at the camp gate, watching the Uighur campfires to the north. Some in the camp knew already that Huaien was dead; the news had come over from the north, and by afternoon even the cooks were talking of it, all of them keeping their voices down as though afraid the men to the north would hear; but the men to the north had known long since.

---

With Huaien dead, the north and the west were no longer one household—this Yisi understood only later. The Tibetans and the Uighurs had both been invited by Huaien alone; he had told the Tibetans there was no one left in the Tang army who could fight, and told the Uighurs he was the Khaghan's father-in-law and this campaign was to lend his son-in-law a hand; both sides listened to him, and both sides watched him. Now that he was dead, the two sides watched each other, neither yielding to the other; the Tibetans resented the Uighurs for plundering more, the Uighurs resented the Tibetans for sharing less; the two camps were pitched further and further apart, a great stretch of empty ground opening between them, and at night men fought on that empty ground and a few were killed, and the next day neither side would own to it.

Guo Ziyi sent his officer Li Guangzan into the Uighur camp to sound them out. Li Guangzan was gone a day, and it was already dark when he came back; he came into the tent and knelt, and when he spoke his voice was still shaking. He said the first thing the Uighurs asked was: is Lord Guo really here? They said Huaien had told them the Heavenly Khaghan was dead and Lord Guo was gone too, the Middle Kingdom had no master, and only then had they come; if Lord Guo was really here, could they be allowed to see him? He said too that there were a few elderly chiefs in the Uighur camp who asked after a *hu* monk who had guided General Pugu along the ditch the year of the Xiangji Temple, and asked whether that *hu* monk was still alive. Everyone in the tent looked at Guo Ziyi. Guo Ziyi sat there, gazing at the tent door; outside the tent door it was black, and beyond that, the campfires to the north.

---

Early the next morning, Guo Ziyi said he would go. At once the tent was in uproar. One old general said the hearts of the Rong and Di could not be trusted; another said if the Lord must go, let him take five hundred armoured horse to guard him; another said five hundred weren't enough, take a thousand. Guo Ziyi shook his head and said: "We're ten thousand; they're hundreds of thousands. Take five hundred horse, take five men—what's the difference? If they mean to kill me, five hundred horse can't protect me; and if I take troops, it'll look as though I've come to fight, they'll grow suspicious, and the whole thing will be ruined. I'll go alone and let them see my face."

As he came out of the tent and mounted, his son Guo Xi burst out of the crowd and seized the horse's bridle. Guo Xi was in his thirties, a commander of troops himself, and now his face was white. He said: "Father, you're a great general of the realm; those are tigers and wolves—how can you offer yourself to tigers and wolves?" Guo Ziyi looked down at him and said: "If we fight today, father and son will both die, and the realm will be in danger. If I go and speak to them with an honest heart, they may be willing to listen, and that's the realm's good fortune; if they won't, it's I alone who die, and the family is kept whole." Guo Xi still held on and would not let go; Guo Ziyi raised his horsewhip, struck him across the back of the hand, and barked, "Off!" Guo Xi's hand let go.

There was dead silence at the camp gate. Guo Ziyi turned his horse's head and went a few steps, then reined in again, looked back once into the crowd, saw Yisi, and said: "The Uighurs asked after that *hu* monk of the Xiangji Temple. Great Virtue Yi, come with me. Leave your weapons."

---

Yisi went back to his own tent and took off the things on his body one by one. First the curved sword; its scabbard was old, the leather worn white; he unbuckled it and laid it on the felt. Then the short mace, its head the size of a fist, iron, cut with fine grooves, the grooves black with who knew how many years' grime; he gripped the haft once and laid it on the felt too. Last the shield. That small round shield was buckled on his back with two leather straps, and had been buckled there nearly ten years; he undid the straps one by one and took the shield from his back. The face of the shield bore many marks, sword cuts, spear thrusts, arrow strikes; there was one very deep one, left by that time at Changshan. He turned the shield over, laid it face down over the mace and sword, straightened up, and his back was all at once empty. The wind blew in at the tent door, blew on his back; there was an old wound there—the day of the Xiangji Temple an arrow had slipped in through the gap between the shield's lower rim and his leather armour—and where the wind blew on it, it was cold. He stood a while, then went out of the tent. His bay horse was tied outside; it was old now, the hair round its mouth gone white, and when it ran it had long been nothing like as fast as before; he untied the reins and swung up.

Guo Ziyi took only a few horsemen with him, and none of the attendants carried weapons. The party went out of the camp gate and north, and the gate closed behind them; Yisi looked back once: the town wall was crowded with people, every one craning his neck, and not a sound could be heard. Three or four *li* to the north, they saw the Uighurs. The Uighurs had seen them too, and on the open ground before their camp gate a great mass of horsemen soon gathered, several thousand, drawn up in an arc across the road, the horses snorting and pawing, the men in the saddle all holding bows, some with arrows already nocked.

Guo Ziyi went forward, and when he was a hundred-odd paces from the horsemen the Uighurs in front drew their bows, several thousand bows drawn at once, the sound of the strings tightening a single *creak*, like a gust of wind across a field of dry grass. Guo Ziyi reined in and raised his hand and took the helmet from his head. His hair was all white, and it streamed in the wind. He handed the helmet to the man beside him, and reached to undo his armour; the armour straps were tied tight, and it took a good while to undo them; he took off the armour and threw it on the ground; he had been carrying a spear in his hand too; he looked at it once, and threw it on the ground as well. Then he urged his horse forward and went slowly. Yisi followed behind his horse, half a length back.

From a hundred paces to fifty, Yisi could see the faces opposite: young ones, old ones, bearded ones, beardless ones; some faces drawn very tight, some full of doubt; the bows still drawn, the arrowheads all pointing this way, glinting point by point in the sun. His back was empty. He did not reach behind him; he held the reins in both hands and looked ahead.

Twenty paces nearer, a few men came out of the Uighur ranks; at their head a tall man wrapped in a sable, a gold knife at his waist—the commander of this expedition, the Khaghan's brother Yaoluoge—and behind him a few elderly chiefs. Those chiefs narrowed their eyes and stared at Guo Ziyi's face, and not one made a sound.

One of the chiefs moved his eyes from Guo Ziyi's face to Yisi's. This chief was fifty or so, his face weathered like old oxhide, a scar on his left cheek. He looked at Yisi, frowned, and his brow cleared again; he raised his hand, jabbed two fingers at his own left brow-bone, then laid his palm over his eyes, and said a few words in Uighur, very fast, and the men beside him all turned their heads to look at Yisi. Yisi could not understand Uighur; he understood only two words, which were Tang, and which the Uighur said with a somewhat stiff tongue: "Fog. Ditch." Yisi, on horseback, facing him, nodded.

---

Yaoluoge had not spoken all this while; now he walked a few paces forward up to Guo Ziyi's horse, tilted his head back and looked at Guo Ziyi's face, looked a while, then turned and shouted something loud to the several thousand horsemen behind him. Yisi did not know what he shouted; he saw only that when he had shouted it, those several thousand drawn bows were lowered one by one. Yaoluoge jumped down from his horse, the chiefs behind him jumped down too, and they made a circle before Guo Ziyi's horse and knelt, their foreheads touching the ground; the horsemen behind dismounted row by row and knelt too, several thousand men, kneeling all over the plain. Guo Ziyi sat a while on his horse, then dismounted too, went over, put out his hand and raised Yaoluoge up, and took his hand.

What happened that day was told far and wide afterwards, told in Chang'an, told in Hebei, told in places further still; some said Lord Guo had turned back hundreds of thousands of *hu* soldiers with a single word; some said that when the Uighurs saw Lord Guo it was as if they had seen an immortal; and the more it was told, the more marvellous it grew. Yisi stood to one side and heard Guo Ziyi say: the Uighurs had done great service to the Tang; when the two capitals were recovered, the Uighurs had lent their strength, and the Tang had not forgotten; Huaien had deceived you; now Huaien is dead—would you join with the Tibetans and fight your own kin? Yaoluoge said it was Huaien who had deceived them. Guo Ziyi said again: the Tibetans have plundered the Tang's cattle and sheep and carried off the Tang's people, and they're right there to your west; if you'll fight together with the Tang, whatever you take back shall be yours. Yaoluoge thought and said: good. Someone brought wine; Guo Ziyi held his bowl, poured a little on the ground, and spoke a few words of oath; Yaoluoge held his bowl, poured a little, spoke a few words too, and the two of them drank.

The chief who had recognized Yisi also brought a bowl of wine and came up and handed it to him. Yisi took it, touched it to his lips, and set it down. The chief looked at him, grinned, clapped him on the shoulder, and said something in Uighur, which Yisi still did not understand.

---

On the way back to camp, the sun was already sinking west. Guo Ziyi rode ahead, Yisi half a length behind. Guo Ziyi wore no armour and no helmet; the attendants had picked those things up and slung them over a horse's back; the wind blew from the north and tossed his white hair into a tangle. After a while Guo Ziyi asked, without turning his head: "Your back empty—cold?" Yisi thought, and said: cold. Guo Ziyi said: "I'm cold too." After another while Guo Ziyi said: that chief knew you. Yisi said that the day of the Xiangji Temple, the one who led the way was Wei the Third; he died at the sluice gate. All the rest of the way, Guo Ziyi did not speak again.

When they reached the camp gate, the gate opened, and all at once the men on the wall began to shout, shouting every which way; some were weeping, some laughing, someone threw his helmet down off the wall. Guo Xi was the first to run out; he ran to the horse and stopped and looked at his father, his lips trembling; twice he opened his mouth; the weal on the back of his hand was still swollen. Guo Ziyi glanced at him and said: go and tell the kitchen to cook an extra pot of gruel tonight.

---

A few days later the Uighur troops and the Tang troops went west together, and at the western plain of Lingtai caught up with the Tibetans. The Tibetans had meant to leave anyway, and never expected the Uighurs to turn and fight them; their ranks broke, many died, and the cattle, sheep, camels and horses they left behind stretched along the road beyond sight, and with them more than four thousand Tang folk they had carried off. Those folk were led back company by company: old people, women, children, who had walked a very long way, blisters all over their feet, every one grey-faced with dust; when they reached the walls of Binzhou, they did not know where to go. Outside the walls Yisi set up a few pots and boiled gruel, boiled it pot after pot, ladled it bowl after bowl, and when at last his arm would not lift, he ladled with the other hand. He had them sit down first, fifty to a company, company by company seated in order, and then gave it out down the line. That winter, when the court assessed merit, Guo Ziyi's merit report had a line for Yisi, and he was raised to Grand Master of Splendid Happiness with Gold and Purple; the patent of appointment was later brought to the monastery by an administrative officer.

---

In the eleventh month, Yisi came back to Chang'an. As the drum for the closing of the gates beat its last round, he led the old bay horse in at the Kaiyuan Gate, and a turn south brought him to the Yining Ward; there was hardly anyone in the street; the ward guard knew him and opened the ward gate for him; from within the ward walls came now and then the bark of a dog. On the horse's back were his shield, mace and sword, wrapped in an old felt, tied behind the saddle; he had not undone them the whole way.

He went into the Yining Ward. A lamp was lit at the gate of the Daqin monastery, a very small lamp, set on the stone block to the left of the gate, a paper shade over it, the flame jumping inside the shade; it did not light far, only the few steps before the gate, and the man standing on the steps: Jingjing. He was nineteen, and a little taller again than when Yisi left in the first month of last year; wrapped in a thick robe, both hands folded in his sleeves, he stood beside the lamp, looking toward the mouth of the street. Only later did Yisi hear from people in the monastery that in these days, every day when Jingjing came back from giving out gruel at the West Market, he would put down his wooden bucket and always stand a while beside the lamp, looking toward the ward gate, before he went in. Someone told him the war wasn't over yet and Master Yi couldn't come back; Jingjing said he knew; and the next evening he still stood there a while.

Jingjing saw Yisi, came down the steps, came up to him, and took the reins. He did not ask whether his Pa was tired, nor how the war had gone; holding the horse, he stood side by side with Yisi, looked at the bundle wrapped in old felt on the horse's back, raised his head and looked at Yisi's face, and said: "Pa, the gruel's still hot."
