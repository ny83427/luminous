# Volume Six　The Shuofang Army

## Chapter 36　A Single Rider

> When Lord Guo Ziyi, President of the Secretariat and Prince of Fenyang, first took command of the army in Shuofang, Suzong had Yisi go with him on campaign.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

Yisi took five days to get back from Mingsha to Jingyang.

The road back was easier than the road there. There were fewer Tibetan patrols, and the few parties he met were all heading back, their horses loaded with plunder, going at an easy pace, as if the war were already over.

Only when he reached the outskirts of Jingyang did he learn that the war was not over.

On the plain outside the city there were tents everywhere. To the north the Uighurs', to the west the Tibetans', one swathe beside another, spread out until you could see no further. At night when the campfires were lit, the town of Jingyang was like a stone steeping in a pot of fire.

Guo Ziyi's troops numbered a little over ten thousand.

---

Yisi went into the command tent.

Guo Ziyi sat and listened to him. He told it very briefly: ill, the flux, could not be saved. Died in the ninth month. His men burned the body, buried the bones by the river at Mingsha, and trampled the ground flat.

When Guo Ziyi had heard it, he asked: "Did he drink it?"

Yisi said: "No."

Guo Ziyi nodded, and asked no more.

After a while he said: "Go and rest."

Yisi did not go and rest. He went out of the tent and stood at the camp gate looking at the Uighur campfires to the north.

There were already men in camp who knew. Huai'en was dead. The news had come from the north; no one knew who said it first, but by afternoon even the cooks were talking of it. Everyone who talked of it kept his voice down, as if afraid those in the north would hear.

But those in the north had known long since.

---

With Huai'en dead, the north and the west were no longer one household.

Yisi only worked this out later. The Tibetans and the Uighurs had been brought here by Huai'en alone. Huai'en had told the Tibetans there was no one left in the Tang army who could fight. He had told the Uighurs he was the Khagan's father-in-law and was doing this for his son-in-law. Both sides had listened to him, both sides had looked to him. Now he was dead, and the two sides looked at each other.

Neither would give way to the other. The Tibetans grudged the Uighurs for plundering more; the Uighurs grudged the Tibetans for sharing less. The two camps drew further and further apart, leaving a great empty stretch between them, and at night men fought on that empty ground and a few were killed, and the next day neither side would own it.

Guo Ziyi sent an officer of his guard to the Uighur camp to sound them out.

The officer was gone a day, and when he came back it was already dark. He came into the tent and knelt, and when he spoke his voice was still shaking.

"The Uighurs said," he said, "is Lord Director Guo here?"

Guo Ziyi looked at him.

"They said Huai'en told them the Heavenly Khagan had passed away, and Lord Director Guo was dead too, and China had no master, and that's why they came," said the officer. "They said, if the Lord Director really is here, could they be allowed to see him?"

Everyone in the tent looked at Guo Ziyi.

Guo Ziyi sat there looking at the tent door. Outside the tent door it was dark, and further out were the campfires of the north.

He looked that way, looked a long time.

---

Early the next morning, Guo Ziyi said he would go.

At once the tent was in uproar.

An old general said: "The hearts of the barbarians are not to be trusted."

Another said: "If the Lord Director goes, let him take five hundred armoured horse to guard him."

Another said: "Five hundred's not enough. Take a thousand."

Guo Ziyi shook his head.

"We're ten thousand, they're hundreds of thousands," he said. "What's the difference between going with five hundred horse and going with five men? If they want to kill me, five hundred horse can't protect me. Go with troops, and it looks as if we've come to fight; let them grow suspicious, and the thing is spoiled."

He stood up.

"If I go alone, they can see my face."

---

As Guo Ziyi came out of the tent and mounted, one of his sons rushed out of the crowd and seized his bridle.

It was his son, past thirty, a commander of troops himself, and now his face was white.

"Father!" he said. "They're tigers and wolves!"

Guo Ziyi looked down at him.

"Father is a great general of the realm," he said. "How can you throw yourself to the tigers and wolves?"

Guo Ziyi said nothing. He raised his whip and brought it down on the back of his son's hand.

The son's hand let go.

"Go," said Guo Ziyi.

There was dead silence at the camp gate.

Guo Ziyi turned his horse and went a few steps out of the camp gate, then reined in again. He looked back, looked once into the crowd, and saw Yisi.

"You come with me," he said.

Yisi stepped forward.

"Leave all weapons behind," said Guo Ziyi.

---

Yisi went back to his own tent and took off the things he carried one by one.

First the curved sword. The scabbard was old, the leather rubbed white; he unbuckled it and laid it on the felt.

Then the short mace. Its head was the size of a fist, iron, chased with fine grooves, and in the grooves something black, gathered over who knew how many years. He gripped the haft, gripped it once, and laid it on the felt too.

Last, the shield.

The small round shield was carried on his back, buckled with two leather straps, and had been buckled there nearly ten years. He undid the straps one by one and took the shield from his back. The face of the shield bore many marks—sword cuts, spear thrusts, arrow strikes—and one very deep one, left from that time at Changshan. He turned the shield over and laid it face down over the mace and the sword.

He straightened up.

All at once his back was empty. The wind came in at the tent door and blew on his back. There was an old wound there; that day at Xiangji Temple an arrow had gone in through the gap between the shield and the leather armour. The wind blew on it, cool.

He stood a while, and went out of the tent.

His chestnut was tethered outside. The horse was old, the hair round its mouth gone white, and it no longer ran as fast as it had. He untied the reins and swung up.

---

Guo Ziyi took only a few riders with him. None wore armour, and none carried weapons.

The little party went out of the camp gate and north.

The camp gate shut behind them. Yisi looked back once; the walls were lined with people, all craning their necks, not one making a sound.

Three or four *li* north, they saw the Uighurs.

The Uighurs saw them too. On the open ground before their camp gate a great body of horsemen quickly gathered, several thousand, drawn up in an arc across the road. The horses snorted and pawed. The men on them all held bows, and some had already nocked their arrows.

Guo Ziyi rode forward.

When he was a hundred paces or so from that body of horse, the Uighurs in front drew their bows. Several thousand bows drawn at once—the sound of the strings pulled taut, a *zhi*, like a gust of wind passing over a field of dry grass.

Guo Ziyi reined in.

He raised his hand and took off his helmet.

His hair was all white, and blew in the wind. He handed the helmet to the man beside him, and reached to undo his armour. The straps were tied tight, and it took him some time to undo them. He took the armour off and threw it on the ground. He had been carrying a spear too; he looked at it once, and threw it on the ground as well.

Then he urged his horse forward and went slowly on.

Yisi followed behind his horse, half a length back.

---

A hundred paces. Eighty. Fifty.

Yisi could see the faces opposite now. Young, old, bearded, beardless. Some faces were drawn very tight, some full of doubt. The bows were all still drawn, the arrowheads all pointed this way, glinting point by point in the sun.

His back was empty.

He did not reach behind him. He held the reins in both hands and looked ahead.

Thirty paces.

From the Uighur ranks a few men came out. The one in front was tall, a sable cloak over his shoulders, a golden sword at his waist—the commander of the Uighurs this time, the Khagan's brother. Behind him came a few chieftains, all well on in years.

Those chieftains narrowed their eyes and stared at Guo Ziyi.

Stared a long time.

Yisi saw that one of the chieftains' eyes moved from Guo Ziyi's face to his.

That chieftain was about fifty, his face weathered like old oxhide, a scar on his left cheek. He looked at Yisi, looked a while, and his brows drew together; looked a while more, and his brows relaxed.

He raised his hand and pointed at Yisi's forehead.

On Yisi's forehead there was a scar, from a little left of the middle of his brow, slanting up to the hairline.

The chieftain said a few things in Uighur, very fast. The men beside him all turned to look at Yisi.

Yisi did not understand Uighur. But one word he understood. It was a Tang word, and when the Uighur said it his tongue was a little stiff.

"Canal."

The chieftain said it again, and as he said it drew with his hand in the air, drew one line and another, and drew a horse going in front and many horses following behind.

"Fog," he said. "Canal."

Yisi, on his horse, facing him, nodded.

---

The commander in the sable cloak had said nothing all this while.

Now he walked forward a few steps, up to Guo Ziyi's horse, and looked up at Guo Ziyi's face. He looked a while, then turned round and shouted something loudly to those several thousand horsemen behind him.

Yisi did not know what he shouted.

But when he had shouted it, those several thousand drawn bows came down, one by one.

The commander leapt from his horse. The chieftains behind him leapt down too. Before Guo Ziyi's horse they formed a ring and knelt, their foreheads to the ground.

The horsemen behind dismounted rank by rank too, and knelt.

Several thousand men, kneeling across a whole stretch of plain.

Guo Ziyi sat his horse a while. Then he dismounted too, went over, reached out and raised the commander to his feet, and took his hand.

---

What happened that day travelled very far afterwards.

To Chang'an, to Hebei, to further places still. Some said Lord Director Guo had turned back hundreds of thousands of barbarian troops with a single word; some said that when the Uighurs saw Lord Director Guo it was like seeing an immortal. The more they told it, the more marvellous it grew.

Yisi stood to one side, and what he heard was in truth very ordinary.

Guo Ziyi said: the Uighurs did great service to the Tang; when the two capitals were recovered, the Uighurs gave their strength, and the Tang has not forgotten. Huai'en deceived you; now Huai'en is dead—will you join the Tibetans and fight your own kin?

The commander said: Huai'en deceived us.

Guo Ziyi said: the Tibetans have carried off the Tang's cattle and sheep, carried off the Tang's people, and they are just to the west of you. If you will join the Tang in fighting them, everything taken shall be yours.

The commander thought and said: good.

Someone brought wine. Guo Ziyi held his bowl, poured a little on the ground, and said a few words of oath. The commander held his bowl, poured a little on the ground too, and said a few words too. Then the two of them drank.

The chieftain who had recognised Yisi also brought a bowl of wine, came up to Yisi, and handed it to him.

Yisi took it, touched it to his lips, and set it down.

The chieftain looked at him and broke into a grin. He clapped Yisi on the shoulder and said something in Uighur. Yisi still did not understand.

---

On the way back to camp the sun was already past its height.

Guo Ziyi rode in front, Yisi half a length behind. Guo Ziyi wore no armour and no helmet; those things had been picked up by his attendants and slung over a horse's back. The wind blew from the north and tossed his white hair into a tangle.

After a while, without turning his head, Guo Ziyi asked: "Your back empty—are you cold?"

Yisi thought and said: "Cold."

"I'm cold too," said Guo Ziyi.

After a while more Guo Ziyi said: "That chieftain knew you."

"That day at Xiangji Temple, he was following behind."

"Did you know him?"

"No," said Yisi. "That day the fog was thick. I didn't look back."

Guo Ziyi said "Mm."

When they reached the camp gate, the gate opened. The people on the walls began shouting all at once, shouting every which way; some wept, some laughed, some threw their helmets down from the walls.

Guo Ziyi's son was the first to run out; he ran to the horse's head and stopped, looking at his father, his lips trembling, unable to say a word. On the back of his hand the welt from the whip was still swollen.

Guo Ziyi looked at him once and said: "Go and tell the cookhouse to make another pot of gruel tonight."

---

A few days later the Uighur troops and the Tang troops went west together and caught up with the Tibetans on the western plain of Lingtai.

That battle went very well. The Tibetans had meant to go in any case; they had never imagined the Uighurs would turn on them, and their ranks broke, and many died, and they left behind tens of thousands of cattle and sheep, and the several thousand Tang people they had carried off. All this is in the histories, and need not be told in detail here.

Those several thousand people were brought back company by company. Old men, women, children. They had walked a long way, their feet all blisters, every one of them grey with dust, standing outside Jingyang not knowing where to go.

Yisi set up a few cauldrons outside the town and cooked gruel.

Cauldron after cauldron he cooked, bowl after bowl he ladled. In the end his arm would not rise, and he ladled with the other hand.

He had them sit down first. Fifty to a heap, heap by heap sitting properly, and then it was given out to each in turn.

---

In the eleventh month, Yisi came back to Chang'an.

When he entered the city it was already dark. The gates were about to close; the soldier on the gate knew him and did not stop him. He led that old chestnut in through the gate and west along the street. There was hardly anyone in the street; the ward gates on either side were all shut, and only now and then a dog barked from within the ward walls.

On the horse's back were his shield, mace and sword, wrapped in an old felt and tied behind the saddle. He had not unwrapped them the whole way.

He walked into the Yining Ward.

At the gate of the Daqin monastery a lamp was lit.

The lamp was very small, set on the stone block by the gate, with a paper shade over it, the flame jumping inside the shade. It did not light far—only the few steps before the gate, and a person standing on the steps.

It was Jingjing.

He was nineteen, a little taller again than last year, wrapped in a thick robe, his two hands tucked in his sleeves, standing beside the lamp and looking towards the mouth of the street.

Later Yisi heard from the people of the monastery that in these days it was mostly the elder Yonan who lit the lamp. Every day when Jingjing came back from giving out gruel at the West Market, he would set down his wooden buckets and always stand a while beside the lamp, looking towards the ward gate, before he went in. Someone told him: the war isn't over yet; Master Yi can't come back. Jingjing said: I know. The next evening, he stood there a while all the same.

He saw Yisi, came down the steps, came up to him, and took the horse's bridle.

He did not ask his father whether he was tired, nor how the war had gone. He held the horse and stood beside Yisi, glanced at that bundle on the horse's back, and raised his head to look at Yisi's face.

"Is the road still open?" he asked.

Yisi looked at him. The lamplight came from behind him; it did not reach his face, only lit his outline.

"It's open," said Yisi.
