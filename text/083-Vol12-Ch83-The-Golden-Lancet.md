# Volume Twelve　Jade Gate

## Chapter 83　The Golden Lancet

> He offered up the glassware he received by grace, and spread out the gold-threaded carpets given him on retiring.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom

From Balkh west they travelled a month and more to Merv. Merv was Elijah's home. He had been born in Merv and had left in his early twenties with Elder David—forty-three years ago. On the day he entered the city he stood at the city gate looking at the gate passage, at the walls, at the river under the walls. The river was the same river; the walls were lower by a stretch than when he left; of the people in the gate passage he knew not one.

His family's home was in the east of the city. He went to look for it and found the lane; the lane was still there; his family's house was gone, someone else's house built in its place, and a household living there who spoke Persian.

"The family that used to live here—where are they?"

"Don't know," said the man of the house. "We've lived here twenty years."

Elijah stood a moment at the mouth of the lane, and went away.

Joseph's family's graves were behind the church outside the city. The church of Merv was bigger than Balkh's—the Metropolitan's church—its hall of brick, two storeys, a cross on the roof of the hall; and the burial ground behind the church was big too, several hundred graves. Elijah searched a whole morning and found them. Joseph's Pa and Ma, two graves side by side, names carved on the stones and the years carved below. Joseph's mother had died late, in the tenth year of Yuanhe; Joseph had been in Chang'an then and never knew.

Elijah knelt before the graves and said in Syriac:

"Joseph sent me. He walked half the way, as far as Shazhou, died at Shazhou, and is buried in the sand east of the city of Shazhou. The other half he asked me to walk for him. Today I've walked it," he said. "He lived thirty-six years in Chang'an, and argued with the monastery all his life—argued over the hymn in Tang, over the words in the scriptures. The year before he went, at Liangzhou, he said to Jingfu: the people haven't gone far; what's gone far is anyone who can put bread into their palms."

Salang, holding Lingbao up, stood behind him. Lingbao could not see the two graves; he stood with his face toward Elijah's voice.

"Joseph, your half—Elijah has walked it for you," he said. "My half is still a little short."

---

West from Merv was the great road of Khurasan. Outside the city of Nishapur, Salang saw a shrine by the road. It stood on a small hill outside the city, built of mud brick, square, not big, a wisp of smoke rising from its roof—blue, thin, going straight up; when the wind blew it bent a little, and straightened again.

"What's that?" asked Salang.

Elijah asked a donkey-driver by the road, and turned back and said, "A shrine of the fire-worshippers. In the city now most believe in the Arabs' religion, and there are fewer fire-worshippers; but that shrine's still there, and the fire in the shrine hasn't gone out for hundreds of years."

Salang stood by the road looking at that wisp of smoke, feeling the earthenware jar on his chest.

"I must go up there."

The caravan's headman would not wait. He said that on the road two stages ahead, caravans had been waylaid twice this month; today they had to reach the next post station before its gates closed, and anyone who fell behind would go on alone. Salang stood without moving, his eyes still on that wisp of smoke.

"Salang," Lingbao called from the sheepskin pannier. "When we come back, we'll come this road again."

Salang stood a while longer, pressed down the oiled cloth over the jar's mouth, and led the yak on. When they had gone a long way he looked back, and the wisp of smoke was still on the hilltop, thin, straight.

---

They reached Baghdad in the eighth month of that year. Baghdad was a round city, very big—smaller than Chang'an, more crowded than Chang'an—the Tigris flowing past the side of the city, with bridges, boats and waterwheels on the river, houses all along both banks, houses of brick, flat-roofed, dates drying on the roofs. Of the speech in the city Salang understood not one word—Arabic, Persian, Syriac, Greek, stirred together, more mixed even than the West Market of Chang'an.

The Patriarchate was in a quarter on the east bank of the Tigris, not inside the round city, called the quarter of the Romans. Many who believed in Daqin lived in that quarter; there was a great church, and beside the church the patriarch's courtyard. The one keeping the courtyard gate was a young man, his beard just come in.

"Looking for whom?"

"For the patriarch," said Elijah. "We've come from Sina, from Chang'an."

"Sina?" The young man's mouth fell open, and he turned and ran inside.

After as long as a meal takes an old deacon came out, fifty or so, thin, stooped, a string of knots in his hand, a honey-coloured patch sewn on the shoulder of his robe. On the courtyard gate was a newly nailed wooden board with a demon's face carved on it. Elijah asked about it later, and the old deacon said it was the caliph's order this year: households not of the Arabs' faith must all nail one on their doors and sew a patch on their robes. That day the old deacon stood at the gate looking at these few: an old monk in a black robe, a *hu*-looking porter with a jar tied to his chest and a big bundle on his back, and on the yak's back a blind old man.

"You've come from Chang'an?"

"Yes," said Elijah. "I'm Elijah, of Merv. In the second year of Yuanhe I went to Sina with the Metropolitan David and lived thirty-six years in Chang'an. This one on the yak's back is Adam, priest of the Daqin Monastery of Chang'an. This one is Salang; his grandfather was called Sargis, a monastery guard who went east from the Patriarchate with the seven elders in the first year of Zhide."

"Sargis." The old deacon's fingers stopped on his string of knots. "In the library there's a register of those who went east with the seven elders that year—twenty-four monastery guards, and the first is Sargis." He stepped aside. "I am Matthew, the patriarch's secretary. I wrote the reply. The patriarch is expecting you. Come in."

---

The patriarch had been ill all summer, and when autumn came he could not get up. He lay in a room facing east with a lamp lit, the window open a crack, worn down to a handful of bones, his beard white and spread over the quilt. On the small table at the head of the bed was piled a stack of letters, some opened, some not; on the very top one the clay seal was still wet. Salang carried Lingbao in and set him on a low stool before the bed. Lingbao felt the edge of the bed and was about to kneel, and the patriarch had Matthew hold him down.

"Sit," said the patriarch in Syriac, his voice very soft. "One come from Sina, who has walked so far, needn't kneel."

So Lingbao sat, and told it from the beginning. He told of the monastery in Chang'an, of the morning, of the gruel shed, of the tearing down of monasteries in the Huichang years, of how the stele was buried, of how the clerk of the Bureau of Receptions asked *what country are you*, of how they went out by the Kaiyuan Gate, of Joseph dying at Shazhou and Siniang dying on the Pamirs. He spoke more than an hour; the patriarch listened with his eyes shut, sometimes opening them to look at him, and shutting them again.

"That stele," said the patriarch. "Is the Patriarchate's name carved on it?"

"It is. *At that time the Lord of the Law, the monk Hnanisho, knew of the Luminous congregation of the East.*"

"The patriarch Hnanisho." The patriarch recited the name once. "The year that stele was set up, the patriarch Hnanisho had been dead a year already. Chang'an didn't know."

Lingbao said nothing.

"Letters travel slowly," said the patriarch. "By the time the things of the east reach here, and the things of here reach the east, the people have all changed."

"So I came myself," said Lingbao. "I've come to ask one thing for someone."

He spoke of Jingfu. He said Jingfu was the director of the monastery in Chang'an, on whom hands had never been laid, not even as deacon; that he had taken two children and gone with the Uighurs to the steppe north of the Tianshan, where there were a few households who believed in Daqin, with no priest and no bread; and he had gone empty-handed.

"He wouldn't come to the Patriarchate to receive the laying on of hands. He said there and back would be three years, five years, and those few households in the north couldn't wait," said Lingbao. "I ask the patriarch to send a bishop. If a bishop goes, he can lay hands on Fu'er—make him deacon, then priest—and then Fu'er's hands will have bread. If no bishop can be sent, send a priest—to consecrate the bread once for those few households."

The patriarch opened his eyes and looked at the ceiling.

"Matthew," he said, "bring the Sina volume."

Matthew went out, and after a while came back carrying a leather-bound volume, opened it, and held it before the patriarch's eyes. The patriarch did not look; he had Matthew read. What Matthew read was a list—the precedence of the Patriarchate's metropolitans everywhere—reading from Persia, reading to Merv, to Samarkand, to India, to Sina.

"*Metropolitan of Sina,*" Matthew read, "*consecrated by the patriarch Timothy in the twentieth-odd year of his reign; noted under the name: went east.*"

There was nothing below it.

"Went east," said the patriarch. "On the day of his consecration I was young; I stood at the back of the hall watching. He went, and for forty years the Patriarchate never received a letter from him. Whether he arrived, which city he was in after he arrived, what year he died, no one knows." Slowly he turned his head and looked at Lingbao. "That little book of yours has a line left empty. This volume of the Patriarchate's has a line left empty too."

Lingbao's two hands rested on his knees; his fingers moved, and he said nothing.

"You thought that once a thing reached the Patriarchate, it would be easy to manage," said the patriarch. "The people of Chang'an all think so. The people of India think so too, and the people of Samarkand. This stack of letters"—he lifted his chin at the pile at the head of the bed—"some from India, saying the priests there have all died, begging us to send someone; some from Khurasan, two bishops quarrelling over a church, begging the Patriarchate to judge; some from Mosul, saying a new church has been torn down by the officials, begging the Patriarchate to go and see the caliph. I lie here and hear Matthew read them one by one, and when he's read them, they lie there."

"The Patriarchate can't send anyone either?"

"Those the Patriarchate can send out are fewer every year," said the patriarch. "The caliph's order this year—you saw it as you came in at the gate. New churches may not be built, old churches that fall may not be repaired, patches must be sewn on clothes, and many of those who held office have been dismissed. To send one bishop to Sina—over the Pamirs, across the desert, as far as north of the Tianshan—one man, three years, five years—whether he'd come back, I don't know. And even if I could send one, to consecrate a bishop three bishops must be gathered, and with this roomful of sickness I can't even assemble those three."

"Then write a document," said Lingbao. "Write it to Fu'er. Allow him, in the north, to break the bread for those few households."

The patriarch looked at him, a good while, and shook his head.

"Bread isn't consecrated by a document," he said. "You've been a priest all your life in Chang'an; you know. A document the Patriarchate can write today, and tomorrow it's a sheet of paper. Bread broken by one on whom hands haven't been laid—write as many documents as you like, it's still food."

Lingbao bowed his head. What the patriarch had said was the very thing he himself had said to Jingfu that night in Xizhou.

"There's another thing you didn't know in Chang'an," said the patriarch. "Who the patriarch is isn't decided wholly by the bishops. I was chosen by the bishops, and when they'd chosen, waited for the caliph's appointment, waited the better part of a year; and before the appointment came down, how many words the palace physicians said for me, how much of the church's money was spent—Matthew knows." He panted a few breaths. "When I die, who the next patriarch is depends on Samarra. When the people in the palace at Samarra nod, there's a patriarch. Without a patriarch, no one consecrates bishops. The one you want is waiting in line behind those people's nodding."

Matthew stood by the bed holding that volume, his head bowed.

"I'm ill. If I took this on, when I'm dead there'd be no one to carry it out for me," said the patriarch. "I can't take it on. Wait for a patriarch to come after."

"When will a patriarch come after?"

"The Lord knows."

The patriarch drew one hand out from under the quilt, had Matthew support it, and laid it on Lingbao's head. That hand was very light, cold, and lay there a good while. Lingbao, head bowed, felt that the hand was trembling.

"You've come to ask for him," said the patriarch. "That sentence the Patriarchate has heard. What the Patriarchate can do, sometimes, is only to hear."

---

Coming out of the patriarch's room, Salang carried Lingbao across the courtyard. In the courtyard was a well, and by the well curb a date palm; Matthew saw them as far as the date palm.

"What the patriarch said—don't blame him," said Matthew. "These thirteen years, half his days have been spent waiting outside the palace gates at Samarra."

Lingbao, on Salang's back, turned his face toward the speaker.

"In Chang'an I thought the Patriarchate was heaven," he said. "Whenever something in Chang'an couldn't be settled, we said: ask the Patriarchate. Guangzheng went, and I waited twenty-odd years, waiting for one word from the Patriarchate."

"The Patriarchate is waiting too," said Matthew.

"Waiting for what?"

Matthew did not answer. He looked up at the demon-faced board on the courtyard gate, stood a while with his hands crossed, and went back in.

---

The next day Matthew took them to the library. The library was a long narrow room with shelves on both sides, and on the shelves scrolls, volumes, leather-bound ledgers, row upon row, piled to the ceiling. Matthew had lamps lit and bade them sit.

Salang untied the oiled-cloth packet from his back, set it on the table, opened it, and took the things out one by one. Matthew picked up Alopen's bark-skin case, opened it, and his hand trembled.

"These are the Patriarchate's scriptures," he said. "From the batch that went east in the Zhenguan years. The library's register has it: *Alopen took east scriptures, so many rolls.* Two hundred-odd years, and they've come back."

He picked up Samaan's ledgers, opened one, looked at a few pages. "Samaan, who went east with Elder Azarel in the first year of Baoying, Warden of the Rule—he's in the Patriarchate's register too."

Last of all Lingbao felt out his own small book, turned to that page, and pointed to that line. He could not see the line; he knew where it was; for twenty-seven years his finger had learned it.

"*Ninth month of the third year of Changqing: Guangzheng goes to the Patriarchate, to ask for a bishop,*" he said. "Below it, a line left empty."

Matthew looked at that line, stood, went to the shelves, and searched among the rows of volumes. He searched an hour and drew out a volume, the patriarchs' book of record, recording each year's great events at the Patriarchate. He turned the pages, turned to the Dahe years, turned very slowly, page by page, and at one page stopped. He read a sentence, and Elijah rendered it sentence by sentence:

"*In the year of the Greeks one thousand one hundred and thirty-nine, a man of Sina came from Chang'an, named Guangzheng, baptismal name Enoch, bearing a letter from Adam, priest of Chang'an, and the register of the monastery of Chang'an. He said that since the death of Jingjing Chang'an had had no bishop, the laying on of hands was broken, there was only one priest, and he asked that a bishop be sent, or hands be laid on the bearer. At that time the patriarch's throne was vacant. Not long after, the patriarch Giwargis succeeded, read the letter, and judged that a bishop could not lightly be sent, nor the office given to one come from afar; he allowed that the Patriarchate would choose a man, and wait until there was such a man; and, Sina being far and without anyone, by exception kept Enoch at the Patriarchate to study the scriptures and the canons, ordained him deacon, and commanded him to bear a letter back east by ship from Basra, to report to Chang'an.*"

He turned on a few pages and stopped again.

"*In the autumn of the year of the Greeks one thousand one hundred and forty-four, a letter came from Basra, saying that Enoch, deacon of Sina, had waited two years at Basra for a ship, repeatedly failed to get one, fell ill of a fever, died at Basra, and was buried outside the city of Basra. Before he died, Enoch entrusted a letter to a sailor going east, to be sent to Chang'an.*"

When Elijah had finished rendering it, the flame of the library's lamp stood straight up.

"Guangzheng's letter arrived," said Lingbao. "In the autumn of the fourth year of Kaicheng; the Li family's youngest son brought it—an oiled-cloth packet. The oiled cloth was sewn by Guangzheng's mother; inside was a piece of parchment, and on the parchment three phrases: Arrived. The patriarch consents. Waiting for a ship." His hand rested on the book of record. "I felt that piece of parchment all night. I told Siniang: this is a hope."

Matthew bowed his head over the page.

"That entry of one thousand one hundred and forty-four I wrote myself. I was in my early thirties that year, newly made secretary; the letter from Basra—I opened it. When I'd recorded it, I didn't know whether Chang'an knew," he said. "And Chang'an kept his letter and waited all these years."

"What the patriarch consented to was making him deacon," said Lingbao. "On the parchment he wrote only the word *consents*. In Chang'an I took it that a bishop had been consented to, and waited eleven years."

He pushed his small book across to Matthew.

"Below this line—I ask you to write it in."

Matthew took up the brush, dipped it in ink, and wrote on that empty line, very slowly. Elijah, watching beside him, read it out word by word: "*In the year of the Greeks one thousand one hundred and forty-four, Enoch Guangzheng, having been ordained deacon, waiting for a ship, died at Basra, buried outside the city of Basra.*" When Elijah had read it he borrowed the brush from Matthew and at the end of the line added, small, in Tang characters: *about the sixth or seventh year of Dahe.*

---

The patriarch's physician was called Hunayn. Every other day he came over from the city to take the patriarch's pulse and change his medicine, and when he had seen him, stood a while in the courtyard talking a few words with Matthew. He was in his early forties, black-bearded, not tall, quick of speech, a man of al-Hira, an Arab, a believer of our religion, who treated the caliph and treated the patriarch too, and translated the Greeks' medical books one after another into Syriac and Arabic. That day he came out of the patriarch's room and saw a blind old man sitting in the middle of the courtyard, Salang crouched beside him peeling dates for him. He went over, crouched, took Lingbao's face in both hands, parted his eyelids with his thumbs, leaned close and looked—looked at the left, then at the right.

"A cataract," he said in Syriac. "The Arabs call it the water that comes down—they say it's water from the brain flowing down and blocking the inside of the eye. This layer on the left is thick; the right a little thinner."

"In Chang'an people said the same," said Lingbao. "Thick on the left, thin on the right."

"This right one can be couched."

Lingbao did not answer at once.

"When my Pa was young, he went with an old teacher of the law at the Chang'an monastery who healed eyes, and treated the eyes of the little princess of the Eastern Palace," he said. "Pa carried the medicine chest, handed the knife, handed the needle—the needle he handed was the needle for couching eyes. Later that old teacher of the law died, Pa grew old, and in Chang'an no one could do it any more. In Chang'an those who could handle that needle were only the Indian monks in the Buddhist monasteries. I wouldn't go."

"Where did that old teacher of the law come from?"

"From the Patriarchate."

"Then the needle he used and my needle are of one family," said Hunayn. "Both come down from the Greeks. I've translated their books; the books draw this needle, and where it goes in, and which way it couches."

Salang, beside them, couldn't understand. He looked at the two men's faces and saw it was about the eyes, and reached out and tugged Lingbao's sleeve.

"Great Worthy, in Chang'an you were afraid of that needle."

"In Chang'an I was afraid that if I were pricked blind, Chang'an wouldn't even have a blind priest," said Lingbao. "Now Chang'an hasn't a single priest. If I'm pricked blind, I'm just a blind old man." He turned toward Hunayn. "Couch it."

Hunayn did not stand at once. He was still crouched, his two hands on his knees.

"When I was young I studied medicine in Baghdad under a great physician. I asked too many questions, and he found me tiresome and said: a son of al-Hira, what's he studying medicine for? Go and change money in the market," he said. "I went—went to the Greeks' country, learned Greek, learned it several years, came back, and translated for him the books he couldn't read. This needle—that's how I learned it." He stood up. "If you'll let me put the needle in, I'll put it in."

---

The day of the couching was clear, in the tenth month; the summer heat of Baghdad had gone. Hunayn had Lingbao helped into a room facing north; the north window was open, the light even, not glaring. Lingbao sat on a low stool, Salang standing behind him.

"Hold his head," Hunayn told Elijah, and Elijah rendered it for Salang. "Both hands—one under the chin, one on the back of the head. He mustn't move, and you mustn't either."

Salang held it. His two hands were very big, and Lingbao's head in his hands was as small as a child's.

Hunayn's disciple held open Lingbao's right eyelids with two fingers. Hunayn drew a needle from a leather sheath—bronze, fine, its point three-edged—held it up to the window, and looked at the point.

"Look at the tip of your own nose," he said. "Whatever you feel, don't turn the eye."

Lingbao looked at the tip of his nose. The needle went in at the white beside the dark of the eye, went in very slowly. Lingbao felt a swelling inside his eye, as if someone had pressed it with a finger. He did not move, and Salang's two hands did not move either. Hunayn's wrist pressed down lightly, held, counted a few breaths, and pressed down again.

"Do you see my hand?"

In the mist before Lingbao's eye, a patch suddenly grew bright—bright to whiteness—and in the white a dark shape swayed once.

"I see a shape."

"Good." Hunayn drew the needle out. "Close it."

His disciple pressed a wad of cotton soaked in egg-white on Lingbao's eyelid. Both eyes were covered and bound three times round with a strip of cloth. Hunayn said seven days: for seven days he was not to get up, not to turn his head, not to cough—if he must cough, hold it in—and to be fed by others; after seven days he would come and take it off.

Those seven days Salang did not leave that room. He fed Lingbao gruel—fed him a mouthful, waited for him to swallow it, fed him another. At night when Lingbao wanted to cough, he pressed his palm on Lingbao's chest, pressed and held it until the urge to cough had passed.

On the fifth night the wooden board was struck in the church—struck very slowly, a beat, a beat, carried from the church into the courtyard, into this room. Lingbao lay with the cloth bound over his eyes, listening.

"Salang, who is it?"

Salang went out to ask, came back, and told him: the patriarch.

Lingbao lay without moving; Hunayn had said he must not turn his head. Lying there, he recited, in Syriac, the office for the repose of the patriarch—recited a line, waited, the board sounded once, and he recited the next.

---

On the seventh day Hunayn came to take off the cloth. First he had the window half closed, then unwound it turn by turn, and when it was unwound lifted off the wad of cotton.

"Open slowly. The right first."

Lingbao opened his right eye. What he saw first was light. A whole room of light, coming in through the half window, spread on the floor, spread on the walls, white, soft. He looked at that sheet of light, and the tears came down at once, and he did not wipe them. There were things in the light, patch after patch, some dark and some pale, some moving.

"Things look blurred," said Hunayn. "That layer has been couched down; there's one thing missing inside the eye now, and from now on everything will be blurred. You won't see characters clearly; you'll see light, you'll see people."

Lingbao turned his head and looked at the biggest shape beside him. The shape was dark, broad, a cloth wound round its head.

"Salang?"

"It's me, Great Worthy."

"So this is what you look like," said Lingbao. He looked a good while, reached out and felt Salang's face, found it, and looked again. "This is what you look like."

Matthew stood at the door. Lingbao's eye stopped on him, stopped on his shoulder.

"What colour is that?"

"Honey-coloured," said Matthew. "This patch—the caliph ordered it sewn."

Lingbao looked at that honey colour and said nothing.

He had Salang bring the glass bowl. Salang opened the bundle and held the bowl out to him. He held it in both hands, raised it toward the window. Light came through the bowl and fell on his knees, on the backs of his hands—ring upon ring, ring within ring, faint and bright.

"Pa said so," he said. "That ring of facets on the bowl's base—hold it to the light, and the light comes through the facets and throws ring upon ring of shadow. Pa said the Sasanians ground it, ground all their lives, to grind it so even." He held up the bowl, his hands trembling a little, and the rings of light trembled with them. "Pa said so. I've seen it."

That evening he had Salang bring the small book again and turned to Guangzheng's page. The characters he could not see clearly; the paper was yellow, line after line of greyish shadow; he found the line Matthew had written, its ink new, blacker than the rest.

"That line isn't empty any more," he said.

---

On the day of the patriarch's burial Lingbao's eye still feared the wind, and he did not go to the grave, but sat in his room listening to the chanting outside, wave after wave. Matthew came back from the grave and stood a moment at his door.

"The bishops are to gather in Baghdad to choose the next patriarch," he said. "When they've chosen, there'll still be Samarra to wait for."

"How long?"

"Last time, we waited two years."

---

On the tenth day after the patriarch's burial, Hunayn came back from Samarra to the Patriarchate to look at Lingbao's eye, and told him the caliph wished to see him.

"The caliph heard me speak of an old man come from Sina, who had lived all his life in the capital of the king of Sina, whose eye I had couched," said Hunayn. "He wants to ask about the king of Sina. You eastern merchants have said a great deal about Sina these two years; he believes half and doesn't believe half."

When Matthew heard, his face changed.

"Hunayn," he said, "he's past eighty and has only just had the cloth taken off."

"When the caliph says he wishes to see someone," said Hunayn, "there's no not seeing him."

Samarra lay north of Baghdad. By boat up the Tigris it took three days. The first night the boat moored in a bend of the river; Salang kept watch at the bow, and Hunayn sat in the cabin beside Lingbao and talked half the night.

"Do you know of the patriarch Timothy?"

"I know of him," said Lingbao. "He wrote to my brother about the province of Sina. Before my brother died, those few letters were still pressed under his pillow."

"When the patriarch Timothy was young, he saw the present caliph's great-grandfather, al-Mahdi," said Hunayn. "Al-Mahdi had him into the palace and questioned him two days running. Asked him how God could have a son—does God have a wife? Asked him whether our prophet is written of in your scriptures. Asked him: our prophet—what do you say of him?"

"What did he say?"

"He said: the prophet walked in the path of the prophets," said Hunayn. "That one sentence our people have passed on seventy years. Some say he spoke well and saved a whole church of people; some say he said too much; some say he said too little. The caliph has read that passage—read it more than once."

Outside the cabin the river slapped the boat's side.

"At the end, the patriarch Timothy gave al-Mahdi a likeness," said Hunayn. "He said: we're like a crowd of people in a dark room groping for a pearl. Everyone who finds something thinks he holds the pearl in his hand. Some hold the pearl; some hold a piece of glass, a stone. When day comes, each opens his hand, and then you can see."

Lingbao said nothing, listening.

"I tell you this so you'll know that of the things he asks, some are light and some are heavy," said Hunayn. "The light ones—he'll ask how big the king of Sina's city is. The heavy ones—when he asks them, his face will be smiling." He stopped. The cabin was very dark; Lingbao could see only his dark shape. "Some years ago he had me make up a poison for him; he said he meant to poison someone. I said, the craft I learned is for saving people, and my religion doesn't allow me to kill either. He shut me up a year. After a year he called me again, laid a sword before me, and asked whether I'd make it up. I said no. He laughed and said he'd been testing me."

"Were you afraid?"

"Afraid," said Hunayn. "That's why I'm telling you this. Tomorrow, whatever he asks, answer it truthfully. But before you answer, think."

---

The caliph's palace was by the river, its walls of brick, very high, gate after gate, soldiers standing at every gate—Turks, flat-faced, narrow-eyed, leaning on long spears, much like those Uighurs in the West Market of Chang'an. Hunayn led Lingbao, Salang holding him up; they walked a long way, through three gates, waited more than an hour in a gallery, and only then entered a great hall. The hall was spread with carpets; Lingbao could not make out the patterns on them, only patches of red and blue. At the far end of the hall sat a man of twenty-eight or nine, black-bearded, a few men standing by him, all in wide robes: one in a tall cap, a judge; one with a pen in his hand, a secretary; and by the curtain a Turkish general, one hand on his sword hilt. Hunayn knelt and made obeisance, his forehead touching the carpet. Lingbao made to kneel, and Salang helped him down.

The caliph asked, and Hunayn rendered it into Syriac; Lingbao answered, and Hunayn rendered it into Arabic.

"The king of Sina's city—is it as big as Baghdad?"

"Bigger," said Lingbao. "A hundred and eight wards, each ward as big as one quarter here, and some wards bigger than a whole city here."

The caliph smiled a little, said something to those beside him, and they smiled too.

"The merchants say the king of Sina sits in a very high tower and comes out only once a year."

"The Son of Heaven often comes out," said Lingbao. "Comes out to sacrifice to Heaven, comes out to hunt. My Pa saw three Sons of Heaven."

"What was your father?"

"A man of Balkh. He fought under the king of Sina's great general and was his eyes and ears. The Son of Heaven granted him a purple *kasaya*."

The caliph looked at him a while, and asked again: "If the king of Sina treated you so well, why did he drive you out again?"

"That was a later Son of Heaven," said Lingbao. "The later Son of Heaven believed the Daoists and sought long life. The Daoists said the *hu* religions stood in his way. So he tore down the Buddhist monasteries, tore down our monastery, tore down the fire-worshippers' shrines, and told us each to go back to our own countries."

"Long life," said the caliph—said that one word—and smiled again. "Did he live long?"

"The spring of the year after he tore down the monasteries, he died."

The hall was quiet a while. The judge in the tall cap stepped half a pace forward, bent, and said a few words to the caliph, very fast. Hunayn did not render them.

"What did he say?" Lingbao asked under his breath.

Still Hunayn did not render it. The caliph turned his face and looked at Hunayn.

"He asks you what the judge said," said the caliph. "Say it as it is."

Sweat came out on Hunayn's forehead.

"The judge says this old man, saying his king tore down the monasteries and died, is using words to frighten Your Majesty," he said. "Your Majesty issued an order this year too."

Lingbao knelt, not moving. Salang couldn't understand, but he could read Hunayn's face, could read the faces of those few in the hall; his two hands pressed on the carpet, pressed until the knuckles were white.

"I wasn't frightening Your Majesty," said Lingbao. "Your Majesty asked whether he lived long, and I answered truthfully. The Son of Heaven of Chang'an died of elixirs. If Your Majesty asked me whether every king who tears down churches dies, I couldn't answer. I know only that kings all die, and kings who don't tear down churches die too."

Hunayn rendered it, rendered it very slowly.

The caliph looked at Lingbao. The judge's face reddened and he was about to speak; the caliph lifted his hand a little, and he did not speak.

"Your Timothy," said the caliph, "said before my great-grandfather that our prophet walked in the path of the prophets. That sentence—you know it?"

"I know it."

"Do you say so too?"

Not one person in the hall made a sound. When Hunayn rendered this sentence his voice was dry. The Turkish general by the curtain shifted the hand on his sword hilt.

Lingbao knelt, and only after a few breaths spoke.

"What the patriarch Timothy said, the patriarch said. He was the patriarch; it was his to say," he said. "I'm an old priest of Chang'an; I've no standing to add one word to the patriarch's, and no standing to take one word away."

"I'm asking you."

"All my life I've known only one road—the one our Lord walked," said Lingbao. "Other people's roads I've never walked, and I daren't say where they lead. A road I haven't walked—to say it's good would be deceiving Your Majesty; to say it isn't good would be deceiving Your Majesty too."

When Hunayn had rendered it, still no one in the hall made a sound. The caliph leaned back on his cushion, his fingers tapping on his knee, tapping a good while.

"Timothy spoke of a pearl too," he said. "Groping for a pearl in a dark room; when day comes, each opens his hand. Tell me: has the day come?"

Lingbao raised his head and looked toward the far end of the hall. He could not make out that man's face, only a seated shape, and behind the shape a sheet of brightness—the windows at the back of the hall.

"This one eye of mine was made bright in Your Majesty's city," he said. "Bright, and still it sees only light; it can't make out things. Your Majesty asks whether the day has come; I can't see clearly. I only know what I hold in my hand. I've held it from Chang'an to here, five years' walking. What Your Majesty holds in his hand, Your Majesty knows himself. When the day comes, whom it will make open his hand, is the Lord's affair—not mine, and not Your Majesty's either."

When Hunayn reached *and not Your Majesty's either*, he stopped.

"Render it," said the caliph.

Hunayn rendered it.

The caliph said nothing. Those few in the hall all looked at him. After a while he laughed—laughed loud this time—and when he had laughed said something to the judge, and the judge bowed his head and withdrew.

"You eastern people," said the caliph, "here with me nail boards on your doors and sew patches on your clothes. You've walked from a king who drove you out to a king who makes you sew patches. What do you think in your heart?"

"In Chang'an the Son of Heaven granted my Pa a purple *kasaya*, and it was a Son of Heaven too who tore down our monastery. The one who granted was a Son of Heaven, and the one who tore down was a Son of Heaven," said Lingbao. "My Pa said: when you see a king, tell the truth. Whether the king listens is the king's affair. Telling the truth is ours."

"Your eye," said the caliph, "was it healed by your God, or by my physician?"

Hunayn's hands clenched on the carpet.

"The needle came down from the Greeks; the hand that held the needle is one Your Majesty's palace keeps," said Lingbao. "What kept that hand from trembling was the Lord."

The caliph looked at him a while longer.

"Hunayn," he said. "Write this old man a document to go back east. At the checkpoints on the road, he's not to be troubled." He paused. "A blind old man, come all this way."

"He's not blind now," said Hunayn. "His right eye sees light."

"Then let him see for himself," said the caliph.

Out of the hall door, through the three palace gates, at the riverside, Hunayn stopped at last and wiped his face with his sleeve.

"That sentence of yours—*and not Your Majesty's either*," he said. "I very nearly didn't render it."

"You rendered it," said Lingbao.

"He told me to." Hunayn looked at the river water. "He was in a good humour today. When he's not, say the same sentence, and not one of the three of us would come out of that gate."

---

Back in Baghdad the weather had turned cold, and mist rose on the Tigris. The document was on parchment, in Arabic letters, a seal pressed at its foot. Lingbao held it, raised it before his eye, and saw line after line of curving black, and could make out not one character.

"Fu'er said: set up by one edict, torn down by one edict," he said to Salang. "He was right."

He rolled up the document and put it in his breast, together with that corner of blue cloth.

"But without this one," he said, "we couldn't get back to Balkh."

Note: Merv (modern Mary in Turkmenistan) was the seat of the Metropolitan of Khurasan of the Church of the East. Abraham II, patriarch of the Church of the East, died in 850; thereafter the patriarchal throne was vacant until Theodosius succeeded in 853. The caliph al-Mutawakkil (r. 847–861) had his capital at Samarra, and his palace guards were mostly Turks; in 850 (AH 235) he ordered the *dhimmi* to wear honey-coloured garments and badges and to nail wooden images of demons on their doors, and forbade the building of new churches—see al-Tabari's *History of the Prophets and Kings*. Hunayn ibn Ishaq (809–873), a man of al-Hira and a believer of the Church of the East, was al-Mutawakkil's physician; he translated Greek medical books into Syriac and Arabic and wrote *Ten Treatises on the Eye*; Arab physicians called cataract "the water that descends" and couched it with a needle, leaving the vision blurred after the operation. The audience of Timothy I (r. 780–823) with the caliph al-Mahdi (r. 775–785) took place around 781; Timothy recorded it himself in a Syriac letter, with his answer that the prophet "walked in the path of the prophets" and the likeness of seeking a pearl in a dark room; al-Mahdi was al-Mutawakkil's great-grandfather. That Hunayn as a youth was rebuked by his teacher Yuhanna ibn Masawayh—"a man of al-Hira should go and change money"—and was imprisoned a year for refusing to make a poison for al-Mutawakkil is in Ibn Abi Usaybi'a's *Lives of the Physicians*, the dating uncertain. In the ninth century the election of a patriarch had to await the caliph's appointment, and Christian physicians at court often mediated; before Abraham II the patriarchal throne had been vacant about two years. Timothy I once consecrated a metropolitan for Sina, after which nothing certain is known. On the stele "at that time the Lord of the Law, the monk Hnanisho": Hnanisho (Hnanisho II) died in 780 and the stele was set up in 781, Chang'an not yet having heard of his death, as scholars have long noted. The patriarch's audience, Hunayn's couching of Lingbao's cataract, the caliph's audience and the document are the novel's invention. The affairs of Guangzheng (Enoch) are in Chapter 67 and this chapter, and are invented.
