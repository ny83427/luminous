# Volume Twelve　Baghdad

## Chapter 66　Salang

> At that time the Father of fathers, Hnanisho, the Great Virtue, was universal Patriarch.
>
> —The Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin in the Middle Kingdom, Syriac on the side (free rendering)

West from Samarkand, across the Amu, to Merv.

Elijah had travelled this road once, more than thirty years before, going east. He had been in his thirties then, going with the men the Patriarchate sent out, all the way to Chang'an. Now he was going west, seventy years old, and could not walk it.

Salang carried him.

On the Long Mountains he had carried the old priest, in the Moheyan Desert he had carried Kang Zhen, and here he carried Elijah. Elijah was heavier than the old priest and lighter than Kang Zhen. Salang carried him a stage, set him down to rest, and carried him another. Elijah lay on his back, the sheepskin book wedged between them, as the old priest's jar had been wedged there once.

In Merv there was a bishop.

The bishop was a fat man of fifty or so who talked very fast. When he heard they had come from Chang'an he had them lodged in the rooms behind the church, and every evening came and sat a while, asking about the east. He asked very closely. He asked how many monasteries there were in Chang'an, how many people in the monastery, what was written on that stele; asked about Xizhou, about Shazhou, about the Uighurs on the steppe.

About the steppe he asked most closely of all.

Elijah told him about Jingfu. Told how at the spring at Yizhou he had saved a child with ginger broth; how he had gone north with that band of Uighurs; how among them there was an old man who could draw the cross, and drew it askew.

The bishop listened, took out a book, and wrote a few lines.

Salang could not read, and did not know what he wrote. He only saw that when the bishop had written it, he pressed his finger on those few lines, pressed a while, before he closed the book.

---

Past Merv, past Nishapur, past Rayy, past one city after another, one range of hills after another.

When Salang thought back on this stretch of road afterwards, he could not recall what the cities had looked like. He only remembered that in every city there were the Arabs' temples, each with a round dome on top and a tall tower beside the dome, and on the tower every day a man called out, five times a day, long and high. He remembered that every time Elijah heard it he shut his eyes and murmured his own prayers, very quietly.

And he remembered the bowl.

It was the grand-teacher's bowl, which the Master had given him by the spring at Yizhou. Its bottom worn white, its rim smooth. All the way he drank water from this bowl. First Elijah drank, and when Elijah had drunk, he drank.

Once, on a mountain pass, the bowl fell out of the bundle, rolled over and over on the stones, rolled toward the ravine. Salang threw himself after it, flat on the ground, and pinned it with his hand.

Behind him Elijah cried out in fright.

Salang got up, took the bowl and looked it over: not broken. He wiped it with his sleeve, put it back in the bundle, and pulled the strap of the bundle one notch tighter.

Elijah looked at him and said something in Syriac.

Salang could not understand. On this road he had learned some dozens of Syriac words from Elijah, and Elijah had learned some dozens of Tang words from him. Between the two of them they could muster a hundred-odd words: enough to ask about eating, enough to ask about sleeping, enough to ask whether something hurt. Not enough for anything else.

Seeing he did not understand, Elijah thought, and changed it for two Tang words.

"Slowly, slowly," he said.

---

In the spring of the fourth year of Dazhong, they reached Baghdad.

The Arabs did not call that year the fourth year of Dazhong; they had their own reckoning. Elijah told it once, and Salang did not remember it.

Baghdad was larger than Chang'an.

Salang stood on the riverbank outside the city and looked at it, looked a long time, and thought it was larger than Chang'an. The city had several rings, and the innermost was round, like a great flatbread, and in the middle of the flatbread was the Arab king's palace, and on the palace a green dome, fearsomely high. Outside the round city the houses spread all the way to the river, spread across to the far bank, spread past the reach of the eye.

The river was the Tigris.

There was a bridge on the river, made of boats, boat against boat, planks laid across them, and men walked on it, horses walked on it, camels walked on it too, and the boats swayed. On the river were boats, big and small, under sail, under oar, coming and going, so crowded the face of the river could hardly be seen.

Salang, with Elijah on his back, stepped onto the bridge of boats.

Elijah, on his back, looked at the water the whole time. After a long while he said something in Syriac. This time Salang understood half.

What he said was: the river hasn't changed.

---

The Patriarchate was on the east bank of the river, a monastery, walled round with earth, the walls not high, but built very thick. At the gate stood two men in grey robes, short staffs in their hands.

Elijah climbed down from Salang's back and walked to the gate by himself. He walked very slowly, step by step, came up to the two men, stopped, and said a few words.

The two men looked at him, and then at the big man behind him. The big man had a staff in his hand, shod with iron at both ends, twice as long as the short staffs in theirs.

They stood aside.

In the courtyard, something was happening.

Several deacons stood at the door of the church round a wooden board. On the board something was painted, painted very ugly, with horns, with a tail, baring its teeth. A deacon with a hammer was about to nail the board to the church door; he struck once, nailed it crooked, pulled it out, nailed it again.

An old deacon beside him watched him nail it and said not one word.

Elijah stood there looking at the board, looked a long time. He asked the old deacon what it was.

The old deacon said it was the Arab king's order. Just issued. On the doors of Christians' houses, a board like this must be nailed. Christians going out must wear yellow clothes and tie yellow girdles. Newly built churches were all to be pulled down. Christians' graves were to be levelled, level with the ground.

Elijah said nothing.

Salang could not understand what they said. He only saw the board, saw the deacon nail it three times before he got it straight. When it was straight, the deacon stepped back, looked at it, and then looked at the hammer in his hand.

Salang thought of a piece of paper. A patch of white, brushed with paste, pasted on a monastery gate in the Yining Ward, and a palm slapping it, *pa*, *pa*, *pa*.

---

The Patriarch saw them on the third day.

The Patriarch was old, and ill, and sat in a chair with a blanket over him. His beard was very long, very white, hanging down onto the blanket. The room was very small, its walls white, nothing hung on them, only a wooden cross nailed on the east wall.

Elijah knelt. Salang knelt too, following him.

The Patriarch told them to rise. Elijah rose; Salang stayed kneeling, and Elijah tugged at him once before he rose.

Elijah took the sheepskin book from his coat, one layer of cloth, two layers, three, peeled them off one by one, and held it up in both hands.

"This was Joseph's," he said. "He took it from the Patriarchate more than thirty years ago. He died in Shazhou. He said: give it back to the Patriarchate."

The Patriarch took it, laid it on his knee, and stroked the cover with his hand.

Then Elijah began to speak.

He spoke very slowly. He began with Chang'an: told of the monastery in the Yining Ward, of the lamp at its gate, of how many people sat in the hall at the seventh-day offering and what hymn they sang. Told how the Manichaean temple was burned first, how the county men came and called the names, counted the things, and last of all counted the stele. Told how the stele was buried, buried over three nights. Told of the Kaiyuan Gate, of Fengxiang, of Jingzhou, of the Long Mountains, of the Xiao Pass, of the ice on the Yellow River, of the family in Liangzhou at New Year, kneeling on the ground in Tang clothes, weeping. Told of Shazhou, of Joseph, of the windowless room behind the temple. Told how there was no one at the Jade Gate. Told of the Moheyan Desert. Told of the spring at Yizhou. Told how Jingfu went north. Told how Kang Zhen sat by the channel in Samarkand with his feet in the water.

He spoke a long time. By the end his voice had gone hoarse, and when it would not come he stopped a moment, drank a mouthful of water, and went on.

The Patriarch listened throughout, and did not interrupt him.

In the corner of the room sat a secretary with a pen, writing on a roll of paper. Elijah said a sentence, and he wrote a sentence. When it came to Chang'an, the secretary's pen stopped a moment, and he looked up at the Patriarch. The Patriarch did not look at him. The secretary lowered his head again and went on writing.

Elijah finished.

The room was silent a long time.

The Patriarch asked: "How many are left?"

Elijah thought and said: "I don't know."

The Patriarch nodded.

---

Then Salang took the paper from his coat.

The paper had been in his coat more than a year; it had gone soft, its edges furred worse than ever, nearly parted along its folds. Salang took it out, did not dare open it, held it up in both hands, and gave it to Elijah. Elijah took it and gave it to the Patriarch.

The Patriarch took it and opened it, fold by fold.

The paper was black. On the black ground, line after line, the letters were all white.

The Patriarch looked at the paper, looked a long time.

"This is from the stele?" he asked.

"The side of the stele," said Elijah. "Names."

The Patriarch raised the paper close to his eyes. His eyes were failing; to read he had to bring things very near.

He began to read.

One by one he read them. He read very slowly, giving each name in full, giving it clearly. There were elders, priests, deacons. After some names came a place, where they had come from; after some an office, what they had done.

Salang stood there listening. He did not understand Syriac, but he could hear that these were names, one after another.

In the middle, the Patriarch stopped a moment.

He ran a finger over the mark on the paper. Scored with a fingernail, scored very deep, scored till the paper had roughened.

He read that line aloud.

"Yisi."

Salang understood that word.

The Patriarch read on. A few lines on, Salang understood another.

"Sargis."

Salang's body moved.

The Patriarch looked at him once, and read on. A few lines on, another Sargis. Further down, another.

On the stele there was not just one called Sargis.

Salang did not know which was his grandfather. Perhaps none of them was. His grandfather had been a monk-soldier who guarded seven teachers of the Law coming east, and stayed in Chang'an afterwards, died in Chang'an, had carried a man's coffin once and wept like a child. These things the stele would not have written.

But each time the Patriarch read one, he answered it in his heart.

When he had finished, the Patriarch turned the paper over.

On the back of the paper were Tang characters, big and crooked, each taking up the room of two.

The Patriarch could not read Tang characters. He asked Elijah what this was.

Elijah could not read them either. He turned to look at Salang.

Salang knew these characters. That night at Wugong he had not been asleep, and had seen the Master turn the paper over by the moonlight and look a long time. The Master had told him once afterwards what these characters were.

"Take it with you," said Salang.

Elijah put it to the Patriarch.

The Patriarch nodded, turned the paper back over, and looked at the line at the very bottom. That line's letters were larger than the rest: it was the opening sentence of the side of the stele, saying under which Patriarch the stele had been raised.

The Patriarch looked at that line, looked a while.

"Hnanisho," he said.

Elijah said: yes.

"When the stele was raised," said the Patriarch, "Hnanisho was already dead."

Elijah said: they didn't know.

The Patriarch looked at that line. The corner of his mouth moved, as if he smiled.

"They didn't know," he said.

---

Last of all, the Patriarch looked at Salang and asked one thing.

Elijah put it to Salang. His Tang was not enough; he thought a while, and could put only a few words of it across.

"The road," said Elijah. "Is it still open?"

Salang looked at the Patriarch.

He thought of the stone outside the Kaiyuan Gate, carved to say that from here westward to Anxi it was nine thousand nine hundred *li*. Thought of the clerk at Fufeng sitting on the mounting-stone, his brush poised, for a long time not bringing it down. Thought of the fort beyond Jingzhou, the fort commander saying, past this ravine nobody will pay you. Thought of the Xiao Pass, of the ice on the Yellow River—you can't stop on ice. Thought of the Moheyan Desert—follow the bones. Thought of the spring at Yizhou, the Master squatting on the ground tying a roll of felt, not turning his head, raising one hand and waving.

"It's open," said Salang. "I've walked it once."

Elijah put it across.

The Patriarch looked at Salang, looked a long time. On that old, white-bearded face there was no expression at all.

Then he folded the paper back along its old creases, fold by fold, into a small square, laid it on the sheepskin book, and kept his hand pressed on it.

"Get up," he said.

Only then did Salang find that at some moment he did not know, he had knelt again.

---

That summer, the old Patriarch died.

The men of the Patriarchate said that in his thirteen years on the throne, nothing great had happened. Some said too that in his thirteen years great things had all happened elsewhere, and by the time the news reached the Patriarchate, years had gone by.

The secretary's roll was afterwards put into the Patriarchate's archives. What was written on it, Salang did not know. He asked Elijah once, and Elijah said: it wrote about Chang'an.

"How much?"

Elijah held up one hand, measuring.

"A few lines," he said.

Elijah lived two more years at the Patriarchate. He lived in a small room at the back of the monastery, facing the river, where at night you could hear the water. Every morning he went to the church, and in the evening he came back and sat at his door, looking at the river. Sometimes he would say a few Tang words to Salang, and they were all things of Chang'an. West Market. Flatbread. Lamp.

Salang stayed at the Patriarchate and kept the gate.

At first the men of the Patriarchate did not know what to do with him. He could not speak Syriac, could not say the prayers, could not write; he was on the Tang register, his mother was Tang. But he was strong, said little, and did not steal. The monastery gate was short of a gatekeeper.

So he stood at the gate.

He did not stop people, nor drive them off; he only stood. In his hand was the staff, shoulder-high, shod with iron at both ends, the iron worn bright, the wood marked by gripping hands with streak upon streak of oil. Those coming and going passed by him and all looked up at him once. And he nodded to them.

On the gate was nailed that board. On the board was painted that thing with horns and a tail. Salang stood beneath it every day.

He never once used the staff.

---

In those years, at the door of the Lü house in the Yining Ward of Chang'an, Lü Shao nailed up an iron hook. He nailed it high; Xiaoman could not reach it, and had to go up on his toes every time he lit the lamp.

Outside Jingzhou, a household surnamed Shi sowed its first crop of wheat.

In the Zoroastrian shrine at Liangzhou, the fire still burned.

Behind a temple east of Shazhou city, in a windowless room, rack upon rack, stack upon stack of scriptures were heaped all the way to the roof. In the very back corner lay two scrolls wrapped in blue cloth.

In the church in the west of Samarkand, a few children would not sit still, and a yellow-faced man of middle years, with a thin stick, wrote stroke by stroke in a sand tray for them to see.

On the steppe, by a river, on top of a wooden post, a dish of mutton fat, a point of fire.

In Baghdad, on the east bank of the Tigris, a big man stood at a gate.

They had lost the monastery. Lost the stele. Lost the name-board and the purple robes the court had given. Lost Chang'an. Some had lost their lives, and were buried in the sand, two sticks planted at the head of the grave.

They had gone on west, gone on north, gone scattered, scattered like grass seed in the wind, and wherever they fell, there they were.

The lamps were all very small. They did not shine far. They shone on the road underfoot.

And that was enough.

(End of Books One to Three)
